The galactic disc is bright, rotating, structured, and rich in gas, dust, stars, bars, spirals, and nebulae. The galactic halo is wider, fainter, and partly invisible. Moving from disc to halo therefore shifts from a luminous sheet to an inferred mass environment measured by rotation curves, stellar streams, satellite motions, circumgalactic gas, and gravitational lensing `empirical-result`. The disc remains inside the halo, but it no longer sets the whole display.

What survives is response grammar: visible motion and light return information about a distributed source. Disc rotation, feedback, merger history, stellar populations, and stripped material all carry upward into halo inference `derived-under-assumptions`. What is integrated out is most of the arm-by-arm and cloud-by-cloud structure. H II regions, reflection nebulae, dust lanes, and local supernova feedback become contributors to baryonic tracers and circumgalactic exchange rather than the principal objects.

The upper level adds variables that a disc map cannot provide by itself: virial mass, concentration, velocity anisotropy, subhalo abundance, satellite distribution, circumgalactic temperature and metallicity, lensing shear, stream morphology, and halo shape. `interpretive`: The Atlas reads the halo as an extended response layer, not as a hidden subject. The kernel is still gravitational, but the evidence becomes indirect: the source is often unseen, and the response is reconstructed from many tracers whose uncertainties must remain visible.
