Zooming from the planetary level to the stellar-cluster level changes the displayed object from a small set of bodies orbiting a primary to an ensemble of stars, binaries, remnants, and escaping members in a shared gravitational field `interpretive`. What survives is orbital grammar: angular momentum, binding energy, resonance, capture, ejection, and tidal stripping still organise motion `derived-under-assumptions`. What is averaged away is most planetary-scale detail. Atmospheres, surfaces, oceans, geology, moons, and local chemistry matter only if they affect mass, luminosity, debris, or a detectable timing signature `derived-under-assumptions`.

The upper level needs new variables because pairwise Keplerian elements cannot describe relaxation, mass segregation, binary heating, stellar evolution, tidal tails, or evaporation. The useful state becomes a distribution in position, velocity, mass, age, and composition, not one ellipse per object `derived-under-assumptions`. In [T]-Theory terms, the edge preserves response grammar while changing the substrate from nested orbital systems to a statistical many-body population `interpretive`. The transition does not claim that planets, stars, or clusters have subjective states; it only marks how perturbations become recoverable phase-space structure at the larger scale `interpretive`.

The picture should therefore move from clean nested ellipses to a cloud of trajectories, where membership, escape, and common origin matter more than any single closed orbit `interpretive`.
