The move from compact object to stellar cluster is a change of display from one dense endpoint to a many-body population. Compact remnants do not vanish; they become members, mass components, binaries, heating sources, or dark populations inside a shared gravitational environment `empirical-result`. A cluster is therefore not a larger compact object. It is a statistical ensemble of stars and remnants whose collective motion, formation history, and external tidal field define the level.

What survives is gravitational response, mass segregation, spin and binary history where measurable, and the fact that compact remnants can dominate local energy exchange. What is integrated out is the detailed ringdown, horizon, surface, or accretion physics of each object unless those details affect the cluster's dynamics or observables `derived-under-assumptions`. The upper level needs phase-space variables: position, velocity, distribution function, mass function, half-light radius, relaxation time, binary fraction, tidal radius, age, and metallicity.

`interpretive`: The Atlas reads this step as a transition from sparse boundary inference to ensemble response. One compact signal can reveal mass and spin; a cluster signal appears in colour-magnitude diagrams, proper motions, velocity dispersion, tidal tails, and population gradients. The response grammar remains, but the kernel is now many-body gravity and relaxation, not a single object's quasi-normal bell. No cluster subject or collective feeling is claimed.
