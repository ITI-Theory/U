Molecules become cellular-synaptic variables when chemical events are enclosed by a membrane, regulated by proteins, and coupled to thresholds `empirical-result`. The molecular view keeps electron density, binding geometry, conformational change, hydration, and stochastic collisions in the foreground `empirical-result`. The cellular view preserves only the consequences that survive into a living boundary: membrane voltage, ion gradients, channel state, calcium concentration, receptor occupancy, vesicle-release probability, cytoskeletal tension, and extracellular composition `derived-under-assumptions`.

What is averaged away is not unreal; it is too fine-grained for the next question. Many molecular microstates can produce the same postsynaptic current, and many receptor conformations can be summarised as an effective conductance `derived-under-assumptions`. New variables are needed because a membrane is an active boundary with capacitance, pumps, feedback, refractory periods, and spatial cable properties. The transition therefore adds excitability: a small molecular binding event may remain local, or it may help launch a propagating spike `empirical-result`.

The [T]-Theory reading keeps this as an `interpretive` retyping, not a proof of cellular mind. Molecular source events enter a cable-response grammar; voltage and timing are the variables carried upward. Penrose--Hameroff microtubules remain an `open-hypothesis`, not the default explanation of cellular excitability.
