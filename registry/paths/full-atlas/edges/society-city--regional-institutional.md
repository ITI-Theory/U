Zooming from city to region changes both medium and boundary `interpretive`. Streets, neighbourhoods, and local institutions are still present, but they are no longer the displayed variables. They become sources, sinks, catchments, and boundary conditions inside a larger map of valleys, corridors, jurisdictions, watersheds, hospitals, courts, schools, utilities, and transport systems `derived-under-assumptions`. Diffusion geography shows that distance, barriers, and contact networks shape spread `empirical-result`, but regional response also depends on authority, budgets, and procedure.

What survives from city scale is spatially constrained diffusion: a disturbance still travels through channels, delays, and feedback. What is averaged away is block-by-block motion, individual street geometry, and the small routines of neighbourhood life. The upper level needs variables for administrative competence, service capacity, legal authority, passability, hydrology, and geographic bottlenecks. A hospital overload, flood warning, policy change, or rail disruption may be delayed or redirected by a mountain pass, watershed boundary, referral rule, or border that does not exist inside a single city. `interpretive`: The transition therefore retypes urban diffusion into regional propagation under explicit constraints.

The shift also changes evidence. City observations may be counts of trips, buildings, or encounters. Regional observations add catchment maps, inter-municipal agreements, utility grids, water gauges, and service mandates. `open-hypothesis`: A valid Atlas use should show which city-scale variables became boundary conditions and which regional variables newly explain the response.
