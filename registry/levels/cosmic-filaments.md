## In ordinary science

Cosmic filaments are elongated bridges of dark matter, galaxies, gas, and diffuse baryons connecting clusters and groups. They are part of the large-scale structure that emerged as tiny early density fluctuations grew by gravity. Filaments are not solid tubes and do not have sharp walls. They are statistical overdensities in a web of sheets, nodes, and voids, identified by algorithms and by agreement among tracers. Surveys infer them from galaxy positions, weak lensing, X-ray and Sunyaev-Zel'dovich signals, absorption systems, and simulations [@bond1996cosmicweb; @tempel2014filaments].

The registry equation $(\partial_\eta^2+2\mathcal H\partial_\eta-c_s^2\nabla^2)G_{18}=\delta^{(4)}$ is a cosmological perturbation response. In words, a density impulse evolves in an expanding universe: the $2\mathcal H\partial_\eta$ term damps motion through expansion, the effective sound-speed term resists collapse on pressure-supported scales, and gravity amplifies overdensities. The equation is schematic for the atlas. Real filament formation is nonlinear and requires collisionless dark matter, baryonic gas, feedback, shocks, heating, cooling, and the finite selection functions of surveys.

Typical filament lengths are tens to hundreds of megaparsecs, with widths of a few megaparsecs depending on definition. Their timescales are cosmic: they trace billions of years of accretion and merger history. Filaments channel galaxies and gas toward cluster nodes, statistically align galaxy spins, and may contain a significant fraction of the warm-hot intergalactic medium. They need variables that clusters do not: skeleton topology, connectivity, linear density, cross-section, node degree, void boundary, and environmental anisotropy.

Measurement is ensemble-based. A single claimed filament may depend on the catalogue and algorithm, but repeated detections across surveys and simulations make the web-like pattern a robust feature of structure formation [@springel2005millennium]. The physical contents are mixed. Dark matter supplies most of the gravitating scaffold, galaxies mark high-density knots, and warm or hot gas can be too diffuse for easy direct imaging. Filament claims are strongest when geometry, lensing, galaxy density, and gas measurements agree across a statistically defined sample.

This makes the filament a bridge between object astronomy and field statistics. It is drawn like a thread, but its boundary is a threshold in a density field and its identity depends on the larger network.

Filaments are also environments. Galaxies inside them can differ statistically from galaxies in voids or cluster cores, because gas supply, tidal fields, merger rate, and ram-pressure history change with location. Those environmental effects are subtle and must be separated from galaxy mass and selection bias. The level therefore requires population thinking: not one filament as a specimen, but many filaments compared across tracers and simulations. Orientation, redshift-space distortion, and survey depth all change how clearly a bridge appears.

## How it was found and measured

Theoretical roots lie in gravitational instability and the Zel'dovich approximation, which showed how initially small perturbations could collapse anisotropically into pancakes, sheets, and filaments [@zeldovich1970]. Bond, Kofman, and Pogosyan later popularised the language of filaments woven into a cosmic web [@bond1996cosmicweb]. Observationally, redshift surveys revealed slices and walls of galaxies rather than random scatter; larger surveys then enabled filament catalogues with repeatable algorithms [@delapparent1986slice; @tempel2014filaments].

Measurements now combine galaxy redshifts, weak lensing, X-ray stacking, Sunyaev-Zel'dovich stacking, absorption lines, and simulations. The Millennium Simulation and later hydrodynamic runs made it possible to compare observed skeletons with structure grown from specified initial conditions [@springel2005millennium]. Because filaments are faint, their discovery is not a single telescope image. It is the convergence of maps, statistics, and simulated universes.

## Objects at this level

### Dark-matter filament

A dark-matter filament is the gravitational scaffold connecting larger haloes and clusters. It is governed by collisionless gravitational collapse, tidal fields, and continuing accretion. It is inferred through galaxies, lensing, and simulations rather than seen directly. In [T]-Theory language, it is a channel of preserved cosmological response, not a material nerve or intention `interpretive`.

### Galaxy filament

A galaxy filament is the visible tracer population along a dark-matter bridge. Its galaxies follow biased sampling: they mark overdensities but do not equal the total matter distribution. Classical variables include number density, luminosity function, redshift, and environment. The [T]-Theory reading treats the galaxy positions as a sparse returned signal of the underlying density kernel `interpretive`.

### Warm-hot intergalactic medium

The warm-hot intergalactic medium is diffuse gas at roughly $10^5$ to $10^7$ K expected in and around filaments. It is governed by shocks, ionisation, cooling, and gravitational heating, and is searched for through absorption, X-rays, and stacking. The [T]-Theory overlay may mark it as a delayed baryonic response to collapse, with particle-scale plasma detail integrated out `interpretive`.

### Filament node and saddle

Nodes are dense junctions where filaments meet clusters or groups; saddles and bridges mark lower-density connections between them. They are governed by the topology of the density field and by anisotropic gravitational flow. In the [T]-Theory reading, nodes are not command centres. They are points where response channels converge under declared coarse-graining `interpretive`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary filament science is limited by faint signals and definitions. How much baryonic matter is in the warm-hot intergalactic medium? Which filament-finding algorithms are most robust? How do feedback and magnetic fields alter gas in the bridges? How strongly do galaxy spin, morphology, and star formation depend on filament environment? These questions require deeper surveys, better lensing maps, high-resolution simulations, and careful selection modelling.

The [T]-Theory reading could be tested by forcing the response grammar to make explicit predictions `open-hypothesis`: which connectivity measures should survive changes of tracer, which are artefacts of algorithms, and whether model-derived ratios improve any filament observable beyond standard cosmology `open-hypothesis`. Without such discrimination, the reading remains an interpretive map of topology `interpretive`.

A strong negative result would also be informative `open-hypothesis`. If connectivity measures changed arbitrarily with catalogue depth, smoothing length, or filament finder, then the programme could not treat those measures as stable response variables without adding further assumptions `derived-under-assumptions`.
