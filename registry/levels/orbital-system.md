## In ordinary science

An orbital system is a set of bodies whose paths are dominated by mutual gravity: planets around a star, moons around a planet, debris in a ring, or artificial satellites around Earth. At this scale the first working description is Newtonian gravity, with the potential $G_{12}(r)=-Gm/r$: $m$ is the source mass, $r$ is distance, and the minus sign records an attractive potential. Gradients of that potential give acceleration, so an orbit is not a static circle but a continuing free fall around a shared centre of mass [@murray1999solar].

Typical lengths run from $10^7$ m for a close satellite system to $10^{13}$ m for the outer Solar System. Times range from minutes for low Earth orbit to centuries for distant planets and resonant minor bodies. The conserved quantities are energy, angular momentum, and, in the two-body approximation, orbital elements such as semi-major axis and eccentricity. Real systems are many-body systems: tides exchange spin and orbit, planets perturb one another, resonances trap or destabilise bodies, and radiation or collisions remove small grains.

Observations combine astrometry, Doppler tracking, transit timing, radar ranging, spacecraft navigation, and long-baseline imaging. The important measured objects are not only positions but residuals: tiny deviations from a simple two-body orbit reveal unseen planets, oblateness, relativistic precession, mass distribution, or non-gravitational forces. General relativity replaces the Newtonian potential when high precision or strong fields matter, but the inverse-distance kernel remains the everyday organising approximation for most planetary and satellite dynamics.

The level is also where scale separation first becomes visually obvious. A planet can be treated as a point mass for its orbit while remaining a complex world internally. That approximation is powerful, but it is always conditional: tides, oblateness, atmospheres, radiation pressure, and resonant small bodies show when the point-particle picture must be refined.

Orbital dynamics is therefore a controlled loss of detail. The calculation does not need every mountain on a moon or every grain in a ring unless those features exert a measurable torque, change a multipole moment, or exchange material. The same body can be represented by a position vector in one equation, by a spinning oblate spheroid in another, and by a rubble pile or atmosphere in a third. The governing choice is always observational: the variables kept are the variables that change the predicted ephemeris, transit time, occultation, or spacecraft range.

## How it was found and measured

The modern level begins with Kepler's laws of planetary motion, published in 1609 and 1619 from Tycho Brahe's precise Mars observations, and with Newton's 1687 synthesis of those laws into universal gravitation [@kepler1609astronomia; @newton1687principia]. Nineteenth-century perturbation theory showed the method's power when the residuals of Uranus led to the prediction and observation of Neptune. In the twentieth century, radar ranging to planets, radio tracking of spacecraft, and lunar laser ranging turned orbital dynamics into a centimetre-to-metre precision science.

Exoplanets extended the same logic beyond the Solar System. Doppler wobble, transit photometry, timing variations, direct imaging, and microlensing all infer bodies from gravitational or geometric signatures rather than from planetary surfaces. Comets and rings were measured first by telescopic sky position and later by spacecraft fly-bys, occultations, and in situ dust instruments. The evidence is cumulative: a reliable orbit is a curve that survives repeated measurements, not a single sighting.

## Objects at this level

### Planetary systems

A planetary system is a star or compact remnant with bound planets, minor bodies, and debris arranged by gravity, collisions, migration, and radiation. Resonances can protect or destabilise orbits, while tides exchange spin and orbital energy. The [T]-Theory reading treats the system as stored response history: a perturbation leaves phase, eccentricity, inclination, and resonance records `interpretive`. It does not treat the system as a subject or decision-maker `interpretive`.

### Moons and satellites

Natural moons, artificial satellites, and captured bodies show how a local primary can dominate motion inside a larger stellar orbit. Their paths record tidal dissipation, oblateness, atmospheric drag, third-body forcing, and station-keeping. In the atlas reading, the useful object is the nested kernel: one gravitational response inside another `interpretive`. The lower surface detail is averaged into mass, shape, drag, and torque terms `derived-under-assumptions`.

### Barycentres and binary-system centres

A barycentre is the common centre of mass about which two or more bodies move. In binary systems it may lie inside one body or in empty space between them, but it is a dynamical point, not a material object. Classical modelling uses it to reduce internal structure into effective orbital variables. The [T]-Theory reading treats it as an integration boundary: distributed mass becomes one response source `interpretive`.

### Comets and comet tails

Comets are icy, dusty bodies on eccentric orbits. Solar heating drives sublimation; gas and dust form a coma, while radiation pressure and the solar wind push tails away from the Sun rather than simply behind the orbit. Classically they are thermophysical small bodies plus orbital dynamics. The [T]-Theory overlay reads a comet as a delayed response object: stored volatile material becomes visible when boundary conditions change `interpretive`.

### Asteroids and irregular moons

Asteroids and irregular moons are small bodies whose trajectories are often more diagnostic than their surfaces at this level. Collisions, resonances, tidal torques, Yarkovsky drift, and capture histories govern their long-term motion. Their geology belongs partly to the planetary level. Here the [T]-Theory reading keeps orbital impulse and response, while shape, regolith, and composition are reduced to mass, cross-section, and torque `interpretive`.

### Asteroid belts

Asteroid belts are populations of rocky and metallic bodies shaped by accretion leftovers, resonances, impacts, and planetary perturbations. The main belt is not a failed planet in any simple sense; Jupiter's resonances and collisional history matter. The atlas reading treats a belt as a distributed memory of formation and clearing `interpretive`. Individual shapes are integrated out into size distribution, families, and orbital-element clusters `derived-under-assumptions`.

### Planetary rings

Planetary rings are thin discs of ice, rock, and dust around giant planets or smaller bodies. They are controlled by Roche limits, shepherd moons, resonances, collisions, self-gravity wakes, and electromagnetic effects for fine grains. In ordinary science they are granular discs, not solid hoops. The [T]-Theory reading uses rings as an orbital response sheet: disturbances become waves, gaps, edges, and clumps `interpretive`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary orbital science still contains hard problems: long-term stability in chaotic many-body systems, the detailed history of migration in young planetary systems, the strength of tidal dissipation inside planets and moons, and the origin of fine ring structure. Small non-gravitational forces also matter for dust, comets, and spacecraft.

For the [T]-Theory reading, the test is discipline rather than metaphor. A valid extension would need to specify which response variable is added to the standard state vector and what ephemeris, timing, resonance, or debris-pattern prediction changes `open-hypothesis`. If all observed behaviour is already captured by gravity, collisions, radiation, and material physics, the atlas reading remains interpretive bookkeeping `interpretive`.
