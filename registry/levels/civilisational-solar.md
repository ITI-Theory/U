## In ordinary science

This registry level is deliberately hybrid. There is no single ordinary-science object called a civilisational-solar system. The YAML joins two earlier ladder branches that both involve large, slow, path-dependent systems. On the social branch, civilisations are studied through settlement systems, states, institutions, energy use, demography, trade, records, conflict, ecological constraint, and technological lock-in. On the orbital branch, solar-system dynamics studies bodies moving under gravity around a star. The comparison is useful for an atlas of scale, but the two branches are not the same physical system.

The displayed equation, $\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho$, is Poisson's equation for Newtonian gravity. In words, the gravitational potential $\Phi$ is sourced by mass density $\rho$, with $G$ setting the strength of gravity. It belongs cleanly to the orbital branch: planets, moons, asteroids, dust, and spacecraft move in a potential shaped by masses and boundary conditions [@murray1999solar]. Characteristic distances run from planetary radii to astronomical units, and characteristic times range from hours for spacecraft manoeuvres to billions of years for orbital evolution.

The civilisational branch uses different equations. It works with population dynamics, resource flows, institutional persistence, network models, economic feedbacks, energy return, conflict, trade, and historical data. There is no accepted Poisson equation for civilisation. A cautious shared vocabulary is attractor landscape: some configurations become stable because roads, laws, writing systems, taxation, military systems, energy infrastructures, educational institutions, and archives make them easier to maintain than to leave. Institutional economics describes these persistent constraints without treating them as forces in the physical sense [@north1990institutions]. Historical-dynamics programmes attempt quantitative models of long social change, but their assumptions and data quality must be inspected carefully [@turchin2003historical].

The ordinary-science lesson is therefore twofold. Solar systems show real gravitational basins, resonances, secular perturbations, and chaotic zones. Civilisations show durable social basins, state capacity, path dependence, and collapse or reorganisation under stress. Both require attention to initial conditions and to slow variables, but the units, evidence, and equations differ. The Atlas keeps them at one broad scale for navigation, while keeping their mechanisms separate.

The level also marks a change in observational style. At city and regional scale, many processes can be observed within one human lifetime. Civilisational change often requires archaeology, palaeoclimate, written records, demography, comparative history, and economic reconstruction. Solar-system change requires celestial mechanics, spacecraft tracking, crater counts, spectroscopy, and long numerical integrations. Both branches therefore depend on archives: cultural and material archives on one side, dynamical and geological archives on the other.

## How it was found and measured

The civilisational branch was assembled from archaeology, philology, demography, economic history, comparative politics, climate proxies, and the study of institutions. Excavated settlements, inscriptions, coins, tax records, shipwrecks, crop remains, tree rings, ice cores, and written chronicles let researchers infer population, exchange, conflict, and state capacity. Modern datasets add satellite night lights, trade statistics, energy accounts, migration records, and conflict databases. The interpretation remains contested because sources are uneven and political categories change.

The solar branch became quantitative through celestial mechanics. Kepler's empirical laws, Newton's gravitational theory, perturbation methods, ephemerides, radar ranging, spacecraft navigation, and numerical N-body integrations turned the solar system into a measured dynamical system. Poisson's equation links mass density to gravitational potential; orbital elements, resonances, and stability analyses then describe motion in that potential [@murray1999solar]. The two measurement histories are separate, but both show why slow variables matter. A civilisation's archive or a planet's orbit can preserve the effect of earlier constraints long after the initiating event has passed.

## Objects at this level

### Civilisations

Civilisations are large historical formations of settlements, institutions, technologies, symbols, energy systems, and records. Ordinary science studies them through archaeology, history, demography, political economy, palaeoenvironmental data, and comparative institutions. They are governed by material capacity and social organisation, not by a single equation. `interpretive`: The Atlas reads a civilisation as a slow response basin: impulses are filtered by archives, law, infrastructure, resource flows, and inherited legitimacy.

### State and imperial systems

States, empires, federations, and confederations coordinate territory through taxation, coercion, law, communication, and administration. They are measured by boundaries, records, fiscal capacity, roads, military reach, population, and institutional survival. Their dynamics include integration, rebellion, reform, and fragmentation. `interpretive`: In response-grammar terms, the state is a high-level routing system. It can amplify, redirect, or suppress regional signals while integrating local differences into central variables.

### Energy regimes

Civilisational scale depends strongly on energy capture: biomass, animal labour, water power, coal, oil, gas, nuclear power, electricity, and renewables. Ordinary analysis uses energy flows, infrastructure, conversion efficiency, emissions, and dependence on supply networks. `interpretive`: The Atlas treats energy regimes as deep boundary conditions. They do not determine culture mechanically, but they change which responses are affordable, repeatable, and stable.

### Solar-system basins

On the physical branch, planets and small bodies occupy gravitational basins around a star. Their ordinary dynamics are governed by Newtonian gravity, perturbations, resonances, tides, and occasional impacts [@murray1999solar]. This object belongs to orbital mechanics, not social science. `interpretive`: The Atlas comparison is limited to basin structure and long memory. It does not claim that societies orbit like planets or that gravity explains civilisation.

## The [T]-Theory reading

`interpretive`: The programme treats civilisational-solar as a reconciliation layer, not an identity claim. `derived-under-assumptions`: The orbital branch may use gravitational potential and long-lived basin structure; the civilisational branch may use attractor language for records, infrastructure, law, institutions, and energy regimes. The hybrid exists because the app's ladder preserves both the social continuation from region and the physical continuation toward planets.

`interpretive`: What is carried upward is slow constraint, not a common material substrate, and the two branches may diverge. In the civilisational branch, regional rules and corridors are integrated out into taxation, archives, trade, energy, military reach, standards, and large-scale legitimacy. In the solar branch, individual surface features are integrated out into masses, orbital elements, resonances, and gravitational potentials. `open-hypothesis`: Any cross-branch analogy must state which variables are being compared and what would count against it empirically. `interpretive`: The level does not claim that societies orbit like planets, that solar systems have culture, or that civilisation is governed by Newtonian gravity.

`derived-under-assumptions`: The programme's response grammar can still ask a disciplined question: what happens when a disturbance enters a system whose slow variables are much longer-lived than the disturbance itself? `interpretive`: For civilisation, examples are reforms, invasions, energy shocks, or archival innovations. For solar systems, examples are perturbations, resonances, impacts, or migration. The comparison stops at response structure.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary science faces different uncertainties on the two branches. Civilisational models must avoid deterministic collapse narratives, sparse archives, Eurocentric categories, and overfitted historical cycles. Solar-system models must handle chaotic sensitivity, incomplete small-body catalogues, uncertain early migration histories, and limits of long integrations. The hybrid label is therefore useful only if it keeps its own caution visible.

`open-hypothesis`: A [T]-Theory use would be testable only when a specific slow variable, impulse, and response metric are declared. In the civilisational branch, a candidate test might compare energy shocks across polities with different archive and infrastructure depth. In the orbital branch, the ordinary test is numerical prediction against ephemerides. The analogy would be refuted if it blurred mechanisms or predicted cross-branch equivalence where the underlying variables differ. `interpretive`: The safe claim is shared response grammar under declared assumptions.
