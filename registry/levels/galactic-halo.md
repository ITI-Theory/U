## In ordinary science

A galactic halo is the extended region around a galaxy that contains dark matter, old stars, globular clusters, hot gas, satellite galaxies, and tidal debris. It is not a visible shell. Much of it is inferred from motion, lensing, gas emission, and the way satellite systems occupy space. Rotation curves remain flatter than the luminous mass alone predicts; weak and strong lensing measure projected mass; stellar streams trace the shape and granularity of the potential; and hot circumgalactic gas reveals a baryonic component far beyond the bright disc [@rubin1980rotation; @binney2008galactic].

The registry equation $\nabla^2\Phi_{16}=4\pi G\rho_{\mathrm{halo}}$ is Poisson's equation specialised to halo density. In words, a distributed mass density $\rho_{\mathrm{halo}}$ sources a gravitational potential $\Phi_{16}$, and that potential governs orbits and light deflection. The equation is Newtonian in form, but it remains a useful limit for galactic dynamics when velocities are small compared with light and gravitational fields are weak. In the standard cosmological model, cold dark matter haloes grow hierarchically: small overdensities collapse, merge, and accrete material into larger systems. A common fitting form is the Navarro-Frenk-White profile, with a central concentration and extended outskirts, though real galaxies are modified by baryonic feedback, tides, non-sphericity, and uncertain substructure [@navarro1997nfw].

The physical scale is large. A Milky-Way-like halo extends to hundreds of kiloparsecs if defined by an approximate virial radius, far beyond the optical disc. Its response times are correspondingly slow: satellite infall, stream wrapping, halo relaxation, and group accretion unfold over hundreds of millions to billions of years. A halo therefore needs variables that a disc alone cannot supply: virial mass, concentration, velocity anisotropy, spin, subhalo population, circumgalactic gas, stellar-halo metallicity, and lensing shear.

Dark matter is measured here by gravity, not by direct laboratory detection. Modified-gravity models remain comparison classes for some galaxy-scale regularities, but clusters, lensing, cosmic microwave background fits, and structure formation make the dark-sector question broader than any single rotation curve. Halo science is consequently an inference problem with cross-checks. A stream can reveal lumps in the potential; stacked weak lensing estimates average mass around many galaxies; satellite counts test small-scale structure; and X-ray or ultraviolet absorption maps the circumgalactic medium. The halo belongs with fields and distributions, not with ordinary visible surfaces, because it is reconstructed from many responses to one extended mass environment.

## How it was found and measured

The historical route to haloes passed through missing-mass arguments. In the 1930s Fritz Zwicky inferred that galaxies in the Coma cluster moved too fast for the visible matter alone, introducing a cluster-scale form of the problem [@zwicky1933]. In the 1970s Vera Rubin, Kent Ford, and collaborators measured extended rotation curves in spiral galaxies and showed that orbital speeds stayed high well outside the bright central regions [@rubin1980rotation]. Radio 21 cm measurements of neutral hydrogen then pushed rotation curves farther into galactic outskirts.

Modern halo work combines several instruments and methods. Optical surveys and Gaia astrometry map stellar streams and halo stars; radio telescopes measure gas rotation; X-ray and ultraviolet observations trace hot and warm gas; gravitational lensing surveys infer mass statistically; and numerical simulations compare observed systems with hierarchical structure formation [@gaia2018dr2; @navarro1997nfw]. None of these methods alone draws a complete halo. Their strength is agreement among independent tracers with different systematics.

## Objects at this level

### Dark-matter halo

A dark-matter halo is the collisionless gravitating scaffold inferred around a galaxy. In ordinary science it is governed mainly by gravity, phase-space dynamics, mergers, and tidal stripping; the density profile is fitted from rotation curves, lensing, and satellite kinematics. In the [T]-Theory reading, the halo is an inferred response layer: visible orbits are the returned signal of an unseen source distribution `interpretive`.

### Stellar halo and streams

The stellar halo is a sparse population of old stars, globular clusters, and tidal streams surrounding the brighter disc. It is governed by orbital dynamics, accretion history, metallicity gradients, and stripping of smaller systems. Streams are especially useful because their long arcs respond to the shape and lumps of the halo potential. In [T]-Theory terms, they are records of past forcing carried as present geometry `interpretive`.

### Circumgalactic medium

The circumgalactic medium is diffuse gas around a galaxy, detected through emission, absorption lines, and its exchange with the disc. It is governed by heating, cooling, feedback, infall, outflows, and gravity. It contains ordinary baryons rather than dark matter, but it shares the halo volume. The [T]-Theory overlay treats it as a baryonic filter through which feedback impulses are delayed, mixed, and returned `interpretive`.

### Satellite galaxies and subhaloes

Satellite galaxies and dark subhaloes orbit inside the larger halo. Their abundance, disruption, and spatial distribution test small-scale structure formation and the effects of baryons. The classical variables are mass, orbit, tidal radius, star-formation history, and survival time. The [T]-Theory reading treats them as resolved knots in the halo response, not as separate minds or intentions `interpretive`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary halo science still has hard open questions. The particle identity of dark matter is unknown; the relation between central density cusps and observed cores is debated; the abundance of small satellites depends on selection effects and baryonic disruption; and the mass and phase structure of the circumgalactic medium remain difficult to measure. Better lensing surveys, Gaia-era stream maps, absorption-line tomography, and hydrodynamic simulations test these questions.

The [T]-Theory reading could be constrained by demanding measurable correspondences `open-hypothesis`. A valid halo overlay should predict which response variables survive coarse-graining from disc to halo, which are lost, and whether the programme's dark-sector comparison improves any inference beyond ordinary $\Lambda$CDM fits `open-hypothesis`. If it adds no discriminating prediction, it remains an interpretive atlas layer rather than a physical alternative `interpretive`.
