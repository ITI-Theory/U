## In ordinary science

A region is larger than a city but smaller than a civilisation: a valley system, metropolitan area, canton, watershed, logistics corridor, language area, health-service area, electricity market, court circuit, or administrative unit. Its measurable substrate combines geography and institutions. Mountains, rivers, passes, ports, roads, power grids, property boundaries, school districts, courts, hospitals, planning authorities, archives, and funding rules all affect how people, materials, diseases, policies, and information move. The important scale is tens to hundreds of kilometres and months to generations.

The registry equation, $(\nabla^2+k_{\mathrm{geo}}^2)G_{\mathrm{geo}}=\delta$, is a Helmholtz-style Green-function equation. In words, a point disturbance $\delta$ produces a response $G$ shaped by spatial coupling, boundaries, and a characteristic wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{geo}}$. In physical acoustics the same mathematical form describes waves in a bounded medium. In geography it is safer to read it as an analogy for constrained propagation: a change travels easily along some channels, is damped in others, and may reflect from administrative or physical boundaries. The point is not that regions are acoustic cavities, but that boundaries and permitted paths matter.

Diffusion geography has a long tradition. Hägerstrand's work on innovation diffusion treated distance, contact networks, and barriers as first-class variables [@hagerstrand1967innovation]. Later regional science and urban modelling added transport costs, land markets, commuting, service catchments, and network centrality. Institutional economics adds a second kind of boundary: rules and procedures that persist even when the people inside them change [@north1990institutions]. The institution is therefore not merely a building. It is a reproducible constraint on allowed actions, response times, accountability, records, and memory.

A regional model must keep both pieces. A hospital network, court circuit, watershed authority, rail corridor, language area, or power grid cannot be predicted from population density alone. The map matters, but so do jurisdiction, funding, protocols, legal authority, legitimacy, and professional practice. These variables explain why nearby places may respond very differently to the same shock. Typical observations include travel-time matrices, service catchments, voting districts, flood maps, school or clinic capacity, electricity interconnectors, commuting flows, and the timing of policy adoption across municipalities.

Regions also contain nested times. Flood response may unfold in hours, electoral realignment in years, rail investment over decades, and legal precedent over generations. Ordinary science therefore uses both dynamic models and institutional history. The same river can be a hydraulic system, a legal boundary, a transport route, a hazard, and a cultural symbol; a responsible regional model states which of these variables is active.

## How it was found and measured

Regional analysis emerged from cartography, surveying, hydrology, transport planning, epidemiological mapping, and administrative record-keeping. Censuses, cadastral maps, parish records, court rolls, railway timetables, water gauges, and early public-health reports made it possible to compare places through common categories. Twentieth-century regional science added location theory, gravity models of interaction, input-output tables, and commuting-based definitions of metropolitan areas. Hägerstrand's diffusion studies showed that innovation spread could be measured as a space-time process rather than narrated only as a sequence of anecdotes [@hagerstrand1967innovation].

Modern instruments include geographic information systems, satellite elevation and land-cover data, hydrological models, traffic sensors, hospital and school catchment datasets, air-quality stations, administrative microdata, and network analysis. Institutional variables are measured less like weather and more like constraints: statutes, budgets, professional standards, service mandates, protocols, and archived decisions [@north1990institutions]. A regional claim is strongest when physical geography and institutional data agree, for example when observed delays match both mountain-pass travel times and jurisdictional hand-off rules.

## Objects at this level

### Watersheds

A watershed drains precipitation and groundwater through slopes, channels, soils, reservoirs, and floodplains. Hydrology governs runoff, infiltration, sediment movement, and flood timing, while law and infrastructure govern abstraction, dams, sewage, and cross-border allocation. `interpretive`: In the Atlas, the watershed is a boundary-shaped response surface. Rainfall is an impulse, but the response is filtered by geology, land use, engineering, and authority.

### Transport and logistics corridors

Rail lines, motorways, ports, tunnels, and freight depots connect cities into regional economies. Ordinary models use capacity, travel time, reliability, cost, and network centrality. Disruption at one node can reorganise flows across the region. `interpretive`: The [T]-Theory reading treats corridors as high-conductance channels in the response field. What moves upward is not a single trip but the corridor's patterned ability to transmit or delay change.

### Administrative regions

Cantons, provinces, districts, planning zones, and health-service areas are institutional objects. They are governed by law, budgets, elections, records, and delegated authority rather than by geometry alone. Their boundaries may coincide with mountains or rivers, but often reflect history. `interpretive`: The Atlas reads administrative borders as semi-permeable membranes. They can amplify, block, or delay an impulse without being physical walls.

### Regional institutions

Hospitals, courts, universities, utilities, archives, and emergency services act across many settlements. They are measured by capacity, jurisdiction, staffing, referral rules, response time, and legitimacy. `interpretive`: At this level, institutional memory means reproducible procedure. The response is stored in records, training, buildings, and authorised pathways, not in a regional mind.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary regional science still struggles with cross-scale causality. Administrative data often follow political borders, while water, commuting, housing markets, pollution, and disease follow different boundaries. Institutions also change when measured: funding rules, reporting requirements, and incentives can reshape the response being studied. Some regional models are too smooth for real politics, while some local histories are too detailed to compare.

`open-hypothesis`: The [T]-Theory reading could be tested by selecting a regional impulse and predicting where response will be fast, delayed, reflected, or blocked. Candidate cases include flood-warning uptake, hospital referral cascades, rail disruption, policy diffusion, drought restriction, or cross-border disease control. It would be weakened if the response-field language failed to improve on ordinary GIS, network, and institutional models, or if it blurred the distinction between geography and agency. `interpretive`: The intended use is a labelled bridge between maps and rules.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** A regional map at 10--100 km scale: one city, smaller towns, roads, rail, river, mountains, administrative borders, hospitals or government buildings. Show several arrows of ordinary flow: commuters, freight, water, or information moving along permitted channels, with boundary labels included.
- **Lens on:** Overlay a Green-function map. A point impulse in one town sends stronger waves along rail and road corridors, weaker waves across mountains, and delayed reflections at institutional borders. Gates and bottlenecks are drawn as semi-transparent boundary lines with delay marks.
