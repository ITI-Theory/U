## In ordinary science

A region is larger than a city but smaller than a civilisation: a valley system, metropolitan area, canton, watershed, logistics corridor, language area, or administrative unit. Its measurable substrate combines geography and institutions. Mountains, rivers, passes, ports, roads, power grids, property boundaries, school districts, courts, hospitals, and planning authorities all affect how people, materials, diseases, policies, and information move. The important scale is tens to hundreds of kilometres and months to generations.

The registry equation, $(\nabla^2+k_{\mathrm{geo}}^2)G_{\mathrm{geo}}=\delta$, is a Helmholtz-style Green-function equation. In words, a point disturbance $\delta$ produces a response $G$ that is shaped by spatial coupling, boundaries, and a characteristic wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{geo}}$. In physical acoustics the same mathematical form describes waves in a bounded medium. In geography it is safer to read it as an analogy for constrained propagation: a change travels easily along some channels, is damped in others, and may reflect from administrative or physical boundaries.

Diffusion geography has a long tradition. Hägerstrand's work on innovation diffusion treated distance, contact networks, and barriers as first-class variables [@hagerstrand1967innovation]. Later regional science and urban modelling added transport costs, land markets, commuting, and network centrality. Institutional economics adds a second kind of boundary: rules and procedures that persist even when the people inside them change [@north1990institutions]. The institution is therefore not merely a building. It is a reproducible constraint on allowed actions, response times, accountability, and memory.

A regional model must keep both pieces. A hospital network, a court circuit, a watershed authority, or a rail corridor cannot be predicted from population density alone. The map matters, but so do jurisdiction, funding, protocols, and legitimacy. These variables explain why nearby places may respond very differently to the same shock. Typical observations include travel-time matrices, service catchments, voting districts, flood maps, school or clinic capacity, and the timing of policy adoption across municipalities.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** A regional map at 10--100 km scale: one city, smaller towns, roads, rail, river, mountains, administrative borders, hospitals or government buildings. Show several arrows of ordinary flow: commuters, freight, water, or information moving along permitted channels, with boundary labels included.
- **Lens on:** Overlay a Green-function map. A point impulse in one town sends stronger waves along rail and road corridors, weaker waves across mountains, and delayed reflections at institutional borders. Gates and bottlenecks are drawn as semi-transparent boundary lines with delay marks.
