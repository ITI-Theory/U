## In ordinary science

The observable universe is the region from which light, gravitational waves, neutrinos, or other signals have had time to reach us since the hot early universe. Its radius is about 46 billion light-years in comoving distance, larger than the age of the universe times the speed of light because space has expanded while the light travelled. It is not necessarily the whole universe. It is the causally accessible domain around the observer, described statistically by radiation, baryons, cold dark matter, dark energy, neutrinos, galaxies, clusters, voids, and metric expansion.

The registry equation $\Box h_{\mu\nu}=-16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ is the linearised Einstein equation. In words, stress-energy $T_{\mu\nu}$ sources a small metric perturbation $h_{\mu\nu}$, and $\Box$ is the spacetime wave operator. Full cosmology uses the Friedmann equations for the homogeneous background plus perturbation theory for fluctuations. The linearised equation captures the atlas idea that matter-energy and geometry respond to one another under causal boundary conditions, but it is not a substitute for the full relativistic model.

Planck 2018 reports a $\Lambda$CDM parameter set including $H_0=67.36$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$, $\Omega_\Lambda=0.6847$, and cold dark matter $\Omega_c=0.2645$ [@planck2018cosmology]. Those numbers come from fitting CMB anisotropies and other data under a model. They sit beside, not outside, independent tensions and tests involving BAO, Type Ia supernovae, gravitational lensing, cluster counts, primordial element abundances, and local distance ladders [@riess1998observational; @perlmutter1999measurements].

The observable universe is measured through several windows. The cosmic microwave background records the surface of last scattering; galaxy surveys map later structure; gravitational lensing measures projected mass; supernovae trace distance-redshift relations; primordial abundances test early nuclear reactions; and simulations connect initial conditions to late structure. Its key variables are scale factor, redshift, density parameters, power spectrum, horizon, curvature, optical depth, growth rate, and selection function.

This boundary matters for language. Maps of the observable universe put the observer at the centre because all past-light-cone maps do, not because Earth is physically central. Distant shells show earlier epochs. A high-redshift galaxy is both far away and seen when the universe was younger, so the picture is a history as well as a spatial survey. The level is therefore an observational limit and a modelled spacetime volume, not a metaphysical claim to totality.

Cosmic variance sets another limit. On the largest scales there is only one observable universe available, so statistical uncertainty cannot be beaten by surveying another independent cosmos. Cosmologists reduce uncertainty by combining probes, modelling foregrounds, and checking consistency, but horizon-scale claims always carry this boundary condition.

## How it was found and measured

The modern scale of the universe emerged through several discoveries. Edwin Hubble's 1929 distance-redshift relation established cosmic expansion in observational form [@hubble1929relation]. Penzias and Wilson detected the cosmic microwave background in 1965, giving direct evidence for a hot early universe [@penzias1965cmb]. COBE measured large-scale anisotropy, and later WMAP and Planck mapped the CMB with increasing precision [@smoot1992cobe; @planck2018cosmology].

Late-time acceleration was inferred in the late 1990s from Type Ia supernovae, leading to the dark-energy term in the standard model [@riess1998observational; @perlmutter1999measurements]. Today the observable universe is measured by combining CMB spectra, BAO, supernovae, weak lensing, galaxy clustering, cluster counts, gravitational waves, and local calibration. No single instrument sees the whole domain at once; the model is assembled from signals arriving across time.

## Objects at this level

### Cosmic microwave background

The cosmic microwave background is relic radiation from the era when photons decoupled from matter, observed today as a near-blackbody field with tiny anisotropies. It is governed by early-universe plasma physics, expansion, recombination, and gravitational potentials. The [T]-Theory reading may treat it as a boundary response surface, but the temperature map remains ordinary CMB data first `empirical-result`.

### Observable horizon

The observable horizon marks the causal limit of signals that can have reached the observer. It is governed by expansion history, light propagation, and the age of the universe, not by a physical wall in space. Its comoving radius changes with cosmological parameters. In [T]-Theory terms, it is a response boundary: what cannot have arrived cannot be part of the observed atlas `interpretive`.

### Hubble flow

The Hubble flow is the large-scale relation between distance and recession caused by cosmic expansion. It is governed by the scale factor, matter-energy contents, and general relativity. Peculiar velocities perturb it locally, but the relation dominates on large scales. The [T]-Theory overlay treats it as background response geometry, not as motion through a pre-existing substance `interpretive`.

### Distance-ladder and supernova samples

Distance-ladder and Type Ia supernova samples are observational systems rather than cosmic objects, but they belong to this level because they constrain expansion history. They are governed by calibration, standardisation, selection effects, and light-curve physics. The [T]-Theory reading can compare response histories only after preserving the ordinary error budget and model dependence `interpretive`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary cosmology has open problems: the nature of dark matter, the cause of accelerated expansion, the $H_0$ tension, possible clustering-amplitude tensions, early-universe initial conditions, neutrino masses, inflationary evidence, and the limits imposed by cosmic variance. Future CMB experiments, galaxy surveys, gravitational-wave standard sirens, supernova samples, and lensing maps will refine these tests.

The [T]-Theory reading could be constrained only by declared predictions `open-hypothesis`.
