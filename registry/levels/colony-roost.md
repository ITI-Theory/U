## In ordinary science

A colony or roost is a repeated gathering of birds at a habitat, cliff, island, reed bed, tree stand, building, or other resting and breeding site. The main variables are no longer wingbeat phase or short-range alignment alone. They include population density, arrivals, departures, site fidelity, nest spacing, predation pressure, weather, food access, breeding stage, and daily or seasonal timing. Communal roosts can also act as information centres where the presence and movement of birds affect foraging opportunities [@ward1973importance].

The registry equation, $\dot\rho_b+\nabla\cdot(\rho_b\mathbf v_b)=0$, is a continuity equation. In words, the density of birds $\rho_b$ changes because birds flow into, out of, and through regions with velocity $\mathbf v_b$. If there are births, deaths, immigration, emigration, or measurement losses, source and sink terms would be added. The basic equation states conservation of counted birds across a chosen domain.

The scale is larger and slower than a flock: tens of metres to kilometres, minutes to days for daily roosting, and seasons for breeding colonies or migration staging. Ordinary fieldwork measures counts, trajectories, nest occupancy, arrival times, acoustic density, weather, and resource location. Remote sensing, radar, cameras, tags, and direct counts each introduce sampling limits.

The upper-level variables are needed because individual aerodynamics are not the main explanatory target. A roost can persist across many different individual birds and many separate flights. It can also fail, move, or split when habitat, predation, light, or disturbance changes. Conservation, ecology, and behaviour questions usually ask about these persistent patterns rather than a single wing stroke. Counts are often approximate, so the field picture must tolerate sampling error, night visibility, and missing birds. What matters is the site-level pattern: where birds gather, when density peaks, how disturbances redistribute the population, and how local movements accumulate into habitat-scale continuity.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The programme reads the colony-roost as the bird path's density-memory layer: repeated arrivals and departures leave a persistent site pattern `interpretive`. The continuity equation carries forward the flock velocity field while retyping it as population flow `derived-under-assumptions`. What survives is response grammar, boundary, density, and transport `interpretive`. What is integrated out is individual wingbeat, momentary neighbour choice, and most single-flight history `interpretive`. The phrase memory means recurrence of a site pattern, not recollection by a super-organism `interpretive`. Source and sink terms remain open model choices until tied to counts and habitat observations `open-hypothesis`. The level is useful for asking how local flight decisions accumulate into a durable place-pattern `interpretive`. No colony mind, teleology, or human emotional state is asserted `open-hypothesis`.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** Show a landscape roost or colony: trees, cliff, reed bed, island, or urban structure with many birds arriving, resting, and departing. Use density shading, flight paths, weather icons, and a hundred-metre to kilometre scale bar.
- **Lens on:** Add a continuity-field overlay: density contours, inflow and outflow arrows, and a disturbance wave redistributing birds through the site. Mark source and sink terms as optional model additions, not hidden intentions.
