## In ordinary science

The string-boundary level represents a theoretical band just above the Planck boundary, where some quantum-gravity models replace point particles with one-dimensional extended objects. In perturbative string theory a string sweeps out a two-dimensional worldsheet, and different vibrational modes are interpreted as different particle states. Closed strings include a spin-2 mode with the right properties for a graviton; open strings can end on D-branes, where gauge fields arise in many models [@polchinski1998string1; @witten1995].

The ordinary equation shown in the registry is the free worldsheet Green's function,
$$G_{\mathrm{string}}(s,s')=-\frac{\alpha'}{2}\log |s-s'|^2.$$
In words, $\alpha'$ is related to string tension, $s$ and $s'$ are worldsheet coordinates, and the logarithm describes the response of a two-dimensional field to a local source on the worldsheet. This is not an image of a tiny violin string. It is a quantum field theory on a surface, with boundary conditions determining which modes are allowed.

M-theory is the name for a conjectural unifying framework in which the five ten-dimensional superstring theories appear as different limits of an eleven-dimensional theory [@witten1995]. Horava-Witten theory is one important boundary construction: eleven-dimensional supergravity is studied on a manifold with boundary, leading to gauge degrees of freedom on those boundaries [@horava1996]. These are mathematical and high-energy theoretical structures. They are not direct observations at $10^{-32}$ m, and they do not by themselves select the observed low-energy universe.

Typical string lengths depend on the model and need not equal the Planck length exactly; the registry scale should therefore be read as an order-of-magnitude theoretical boundary, not a measured ruler mark. The measured world at accessible energies is still described by quantum field theory and the Standard Model. The string-boundary level is included because it supplies a well-studied example of propagators, modes, compactification, branes, and boundary conditions before ordinary particles are treated as localised excitations.

In ordinary science the level is therefore conditional. If strings or branes are the right ultraviolet description, then particles are excitations of extended objects and compact dimensions help determine spectra and couplings. If another quantum-gravity theory is right, this level becomes a historically important theoretical language rather than a literal layer of nature.

The most important ordinary terms are worldsheet, target space, tension, mode, brane, duality, and compactification. Tension sets the energetic cost of stretching the string; modes name the allowed excitations; branes impose boundary conditions; dualities show that apparently different descriptions can encode the same physics. These terms make the level precise enough to draw, even though its direct empirical status remains unsettled. They also show why the atlas treats the boundary as a rule system rather than a miniature mechanical object in observed space.

## How it was found and measured

String theory began in the late 1960s as a model for hadron scattering, especially the Veneziano amplitude [@veneziano1968]. It was later reinterpreted as a candidate quantum theory including gravity when the closed-string spectrum was seen to contain a graviton-like state. The 1984 anomaly-cancellation result in superstring theory made the subject central to high-energy theory [@green1984]. Witten's 1995 duality synthesis then motivated the M-theory name for an eleven-dimensional unifying framework [@witten1995].

There is no direct measurement of a string, brane, or compact dimension. Evidence is mathematical: internal consistency, dualities, anomaly cancellation, black-hole entropy calculations, and links to quantum field theory. Experimental high-energy physics has not observed supersymmetric partners or string resonances. The atlas therefore treats this level as a theoretical boundary language: powerful, structured, and useful for response grammar, but not empirically confirmed at its native scale.

The instruments are consequently indirect: particle colliders search for missing-energy or resonance signatures, precision tests constrain extra dimensions, and cosmological observations constrain early-universe scenarios. These searches have produced limits rather than confirmation. In the atlas that negative result is not a reason to remove the level; it is a reason to label it carefully as a conditional theoretical bridge, not direct observation or measurement.

## Objects at this level

### Closed string

A closed string is a loop-like excitation in perturbative string theory. Its allowed vibrational modes include a massless spin-2 state with the formal properties expected of a graviton. The governing object is a quantum theory on the worldsheet plus consistency constraints. The [T]-Theory reading keeps the loop as a mode-response example `interpretive`: a source changes allowed amplitudes, but no literal somatic state is attributed to the string.

### Open string and D-brane

Open strings have endpoints, and in many constructions those endpoints lie on D-branes. Gauge fields can arise from open-string modes attached to such branes. The classical picture is still mathematical rather than directly observed. The [T]-Theory reading treats endpoint constraints as boundary grammar `derived-under-assumptions`: changing the boundary changes the response spectrum, while the claim remains a modelling analogy `interpretive`.

### Compactification boundary

Compactification describes extra dimensions curled or otherwise constrained so that low-energy physics appears lower-dimensional. Shape, topology, and flux choices can affect the effective particles and couplings. In the atlas, this is the main "boundary" object. [T]-Theory uses it to mark how lower variables can be hidden while response rules remain visible `interpretive`; it is not presented as proof that the programme derives the Standard Model `open-hypothesis`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary open questions are large: whether string theory describes nature, how vacua are selected, how de Sitter cosmology is represented, whether supersymmetry appears at reachable energies, and whether any distinctive low-energy signature can be isolated. The theory's mathematical success does not settle its empirical status.

For [T]-Theory, the main test is whether the compactification analogy does real work `open-hypothesis`. A useful atlas rule should say which degrees of freedom are hidden, which boundary variables remain, and what changes when the boundary changes. If the same language can be mapped onto every later level without producing different constraints, it is merely decorative. If it predicts specific transition bookkeeping or renderer behaviour, it becomes a refutable modelling choice `open-hypothesis`.

A concrete failure mode would be over-translation: treating every hidden variable at later scales as if it were a compact dimension. A concrete success would be narrower: a declared projection that changes the effective kernel, exposes a measurable variable, or forbids a proposed cross-scale analogy because its boundary conditions do not match.
