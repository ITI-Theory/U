## In ordinary science

A stellar cluster is a gravitationally bound or recently associated population of stars. Open clusters contain hundreds to thousands of stars and are often disrupted by tides and encounters. Globular clusters contain up to millions of old stars in dense, nearly spherical systems. Larger stellar neighbourhoods and associations connect cluster dynamics to the galactic field [@portegies2010starclusters].

The registry displays the linearised gravitational equation $\Box h_{\mu\nu}=-16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$: stress-energy $T_{\mu\nu}$ sources a metric perturbation $h_{\mu\nu}$, and $\Box$ is the wave operator. For most cluster work, Newtonian $N$-body gravity is the practical limit, but the equation records the same source-response idea in relativistic notation. The central measured variables are positions, velocities, masses, luminosities, metallicities, ages, binary fractions, and tidal tails. Gaia astrometry has made cluster phase space a directly measured object rather than only a projected star count [@gaia2018dr2].

Cluster timescales include crossing time, relaxation time, stellar lifetime, binary hardening time, and evaporation time. Encounters exchange energy; massive stars sink by mass segregation; binaries heat the core; external tides strip stars into streams. A cluster therefore needs variables unavailable at the single-star level: distribution functions, velocity dispersion, mass function, half-light radius, tidal radius, and orbit in the host galaxy.

The scientific point is collective gravity. The cluster is not reducible to one star scaled up, because many weak interactions accumulate into emergent structure and long-term memory of formation and disruption.

Observation is now population work. Colour-magnitude diagrams date the population; proper motions separate members from foreground stars; radial velocities and metallicity identify subgroups; tidal streams reveal escape and external stripping. The governing equations can be simple pairwise gravity locally, but the useful description is statistical because the number of stars is large.

The same cluster can therefore be read as a snapshot, an orbit, and a fossil record of star formation.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The programme reads this level as a transition from single resonant plasma objects to collective gravitational ensembles `interpretive` [@johnson2026usf]. It carries up stellar composition, ages, winds, remnants, and orbital histories, while individual convection cells, flares, and most stellar surface detail are integrated out into population variables `derived-under-assumptions`.

In *The Universal Somatic Field: Green's Functions as Scale-Invariant Oscillators across Twenty Scale Levels*, clustered stars illustrate that response may be statistical rather than local `interpretive` [@johnson2026usf]. The claim is not that clusters feel or deliberate. The [T]-Theory statement is only that perturbations propagate through a many-body potential and leave recoverable structure in phase space `interpretive`.

The status is not formal proof of a new cluster law; it is a scale placement and response analogy derived under the programme's catalogue assumptions `derived-under-assumptions`.
