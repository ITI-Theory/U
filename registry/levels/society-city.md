## In ordinary science

A city is a dense physical settlement coupled to a social network. Its measurable parts include land use, street geometry, housing density, transport flows, energy demand, communication links, income distributions, crime reports, disease incidence, and the timed movements of people and goods. Urban science treats many of these quantities as fields on a network or on a map: population density varies from block to block; traffic congestion propagates along roads; information and contagion spread through contact and media channels. The relevant scale is usually kilometres and minutes-to-decades, with daily commuting at one end and infrastructure renewal at the other.

The registry equation, $\partial_t u=D\nabla^2u+f(u)$, is the standard reaction--diffusion form. In words, the local amount of some quantity $u$ changes because it diffuses through neighbouring locations with effective diffusivity $D$, and because local processes $f(u)$ create, remove, amplify, or saturate it. This is a modelling grammar, not a claim that every urban process is literally chemical diffusion. It is useful because cities contain both spatial coupling and local feedback.

Two empirical facts set the ordinary-science boundary. First, many biological and infrastructural systems show allometric scaling: some quantities grow sublinearly with size, while others grow faster than size [@west1997general; @bettencourt2007growth]. In cities, infrastructure such as road length often scales more slowly than population, while measures tied to interaction, innovation, or disease can scale superlinearly. Second, urban outcomes are path-dependent. Street plans, zoning decisions, rail stations, rivers, hills, property law, and historical segregation remain visible in later patterns [@batty2013new]. Measurements are therefore commonly compared across neighbourhoods, across cities, and through time rather than read from one universal constant. Time of day and season also matter. The city is not a smooth gas of people. It is a constrained, heterogeneous medium with memory in its built form and institutions.

## The [T]-Theory reading

`interpretive`: The programme reads the society-city level as a response field on infrastructure and social encounter networks. An impulse such as a rumour, policy change, transport strike, or new practice enters a patterned medium; the observed spread is the Green's-function response shaped by neighbourhood boundaries, media coupling, and institutions. `derived-under-assumptions`: In the Atlas grammar, the level inherits dyadic coupling from below but averages away individual biographies into rates, channels, and local feedback functions. `interpretive`: The relevant paper title is *The Geographic Somatic Field: Scale-Invariant Wave Propagation in Human Landscapes*, where geographic propagation is proposed as a modelling analogy. `open-hypothesis`: Whether any particular urban variable deserves a soma-field interpretation must be tested case by case. `interpretive`: What is carried upward is not private experience but a pattern of response: repeated contact, shared symbols, and built constraints. The claim is not that a city has feelings, intentions, or a literal mind; collective memory means persistent norms, records, routes, and constraints.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** A night or daylight city map at kilometre scale: streets, blocks, transit lines, parks, river or rail boundaries, and moving dots for people or vehicles. Show one local disturbance spreading unevenly through connected streets and media nodes, with a scale bar of one kilometre.
- **Lens on:** Add a translucent propagator surface over the same map. Bright ridges follow high-contact corridors; dim zones sit behind rivers, zoning edges, or disconnected districts. A small impulse pulse enters one neighbourhood and becomes a delayed, anisotropic response field rather than a uniform circle.
