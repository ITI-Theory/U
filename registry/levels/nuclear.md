## In ordinary science

The nuclear level covers femtometre physics, roughly $10^{-15}$ m. Atomic nuclei are made of protons and neutrons; protons and neutrons are hadrons built mainly from up and down valence quarks plus gluons and sea quark-antiquark pairs. The underlying theory of the strong interaction is quantum chromodynamics, a non-Abelian gauge theory with colour charge and gluon self-interaction [@yangmills1954; @peskin1995qft]. The most important qualitative fact is confinement: isolated quarks are not observed at ordinary energies.

Inside nuclei, the residual strong force binds nucleons over a very short range. A useful ordinary approximation is the Yukawa form
$$G_Y(r)=\frac{e^{-mr}}{4\pi r}.$$
In words, $r$ is separation, $m$ is an effective exchanged-particle mass in natural units, and the exponential term makes the response fade rapidly. Yukawa introduced this idea to explain why the nuclear force is strong at about a femtometre but negligible at atomic distances [@yukawa1935]. It is not the full theory of QCD, but it remains an important scale-appropriate kernel.

Measured quantities at this level include nuclear masses, binding energies, decay half-lives, scattering cross-sections, reaction rates, spin, parity, and energy spectra. Energies are typically MeV for nuclear binding and excitations, while hadron rest masses are hundreds of MeV to GeV. Timescales vary enormously: strong-interaction scattering is extremely fast, but radioactive half-lives can range from fractions of a second to longer than the age of the universe because weak or electromagnetic processes may control the decay.

The nuclear level is the source of elemental stability and transmutation. Fusion changes light nuclei into heavier ones when conditions overcome Coulomb repulsion; fission splits heavy nuclei; radioactive decay changes nuclear composition. Ordinary material chemistry depends on electrons, but the list of possible elements is set by nuclear charge and nuclear stability. Stable ordinary matter uses nuclei made from protons and neutrons, not from stable strange-matter building blocks.

The level also links terrestrial laboratories with stars. Stellar nucleosynthesis, supernova ejecta, reactor physics, isotope dating, medical imaging, and particle detectors all depend on nuclear response. Yet most chemistry can ignore this substructure: an atom normally needs nuclear charge, mass, isotope, spin, and magnetic moment, not an explicit gluon field. That compression is the main reason the next atlas level can be atomic rather than fully subnuclear.

Several forces are present but not equally visible. The strong interaction binds quarks and, residually, nucleons; electromagnetism repels protons and shapes decay products; the weak interaction controls beta decay and neutrino processes; gravity is negligible for a single nucleus. Nuclear explanations therefore depend on which process is being measured. The same nucleus can be stable chemically, radioactive weakly, and excitable electromagnetically under gamma-ray or collision input in laboratory, reactor, or stellar settings.

## How it was found and measured

Rutherford's gold-foil scattering experiments showed that positive charge and most atomic mass occupy a tiny nucleus [@rutherford1911]. Chadwick identified the neutron in 1932, explaining isotopes and making nuclear structure far clearer [@chadwick1932]. Yukawa proposed a massive exchange particle for the short range of the nuclear force in 1935 [@yukawa1935]. Later electron scattering measured finite nuclear charge distributions, and deep inelastic scattering revealed quark substructure [@hofstadter1956; @pdg2024].

Quantum chromodynamics emerged in the 1970s when asymptotic freedom showed how quarks can behave nearly free at short distances while remaining confined at long distances [@gross1973; @politzer1973]. Today nuclear quantities are measured by accelerators, reactor experiments, mass spectrometry, gamma spectroscopy, neutrino detectors, and astrophysical observations. The field is empirical, but not simple: low-energy nuclei require many-body approximations, effective field theories, and numerical methods because exact QCD calculations for complex nuclei remain difficult.

Key instruments include cloud and bubble chambers historically, then wire chambers, calorimeters, time projection chambers, storage rings, radioactive-beam lines, and underground detectors. Precision mass tables and evaluated nuclear data are as important as spectacular collisions. They show that the nucleus is not a featureless point: it has size, deformation, shell structure, resonances, thresholds, reaction channels, and many experimentally measured decay pathways today.

## Objects at this level

### Proton and neutron

Protons and neutrons are baryons made from valence quarks, sea quarks, and gluons. Their masses, magnetic moments, and scattering form factors are measured quantities, while their internal dynamics are governed by QCD. In [T]-Theory they are local response carriers only `interpretive`: a perturbation can be represented through scattering amplitudes, but the hadron is not treated as a mind or organism.

### Atomic nucleus

An atomic nucleus is a bound many-nucleon system with charge, mass number, spin, parity, binding energy, and possible excited states. Shell models, liquid-drop terms, and ab initio methods describe different regimes. The [T]-Theory reading uses the nucleus as a compact short-range response basin `derived-under-assumptions`: many internal variables are hidden when the atom above needs only selected nuclear parameters.

### Meson and exchange picture

Mesons are quark-antiquark states; historically, pion exchange explained why residual nuclear forces are short-ranged. The Yukawa kernel captures the idea that a massive mediator produces exponential decay with distance. In [T]-Theory this becomes the clearest early example of a bounded kernel `interpretive`: a source matters strongly nearby, then fades before atomic length scales.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary open questions include confinement from first principles, the QCD phase diagram, neutron-star matter, rare decays, neutrino properties, and the exact origin of nuclear shell structure across unstable isotopes. Many are being tested by lattice QCD, radioactive-beam facilities, neutrino experiments, heavy-ion collisions, and multimessenger astronomy.

For [T]-Theory, the nuclear test is whether the short-range kernel is handled with discipline `open-hypothesis`. The atlas should predict that nuclear variables disappear rapidly from chemical-scale explanations except through charge, mass, isotope, spin, and stability. If later text smuggles detailed nuclear agency into molecular or biological levels without a measured coupling, the transition has failed. A valid extension must state which nuclear parameters survive and why `derived-under-assumptions`.

Refutation can be practical as well as philosophical. If an atlas example attributes a molecular effect to nuclear structure while isotope substitution, nuclear spin, or radiation cannot affect the process, the proposed bridge is unsupported. Conversely, isotope chemistry, nuclear magnetic resonance, and radiotracing are legitimate cases where selected nuclear variables survive.
