## In ordinary science

The nuclear level covers femtometre physics, roughly $10^{-15}$ m. Atomic nuclei are made of protons and neutrons; protons and neutrons are hadrons built mainly from up and down valence quarks plus gluons and sea quark-antiquark pairs. The underlying theory of the strong interaction is quantum chromodynamics, a non-Abelian gauge theory with colour charge and gluon self-interaction [@yangmills1954; @peskin1995qft]. The most important qualitative fact is confinement: isolated quarks are not observed at ordinary energies.

Inside nuclei, the residual strong force binds nucleons over a very short range. A useful ordinary approximation is the Yukawa form
$$G_Y(r)=\frac{e^{-mr}}{4\pi r}.$$
In words, $r$ is separation, $m$ is an effective exchanged-particle mass in natural units, and the exponential term makes the response fade rapidly. Yukawa introduced this idea to explain why the nuclear force is strong at about a femtometre but negligible at atomic distances [@yukawa1935]. It is not the full theory of QCD, but it remains an important scale-appropriate kernel.

Measured quantities at this level include nuclear masses, binding energies, decay half-lives, scattering cross-sections, reaction rates, spin, parity, and energy spectra. Energies are typically MeV for nuclear binding and excitations, while hadron rest masses are hundreds of MeV to GeV. Timescales vary enormously: strong-interaction scattering is extremely fast, but radioactive half-lives can range from fractions of a second to longer than the age of the universe because weak or electromagnetic processes may control the decay.

The nuclear level is the source of elemental stability and transmutation. Fusion changes light nuclei into heavier ones when conditions overcome Coulomb repulsion; fission splits heavy nuclei; radioactive decay changes nuclear composition. Ordinary material chemistry depends on electrons, but the list of possible elements is set by nuclear charge and nuclear stability. Stable ordinary matter uses nuclei made from protons and neutrons, not from stable strange-matter building blocks.
