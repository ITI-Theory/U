## In ordinary science

The atomic level is the angstrom scale, about $10^{-10}$ m. An atom consists of a compact positively charged nucleus and a surrounding electron state. The nucleus sets the element through its proton number $Z$; the electrons determine most chemical and optical behaviour. Quantum mechanics replaces planetary orbits with wavefunctions and orbitals: probability amplitudes whose squared magnitude gives the probability density for measurement outcomes [@griffiths2018qm].

For static electric response the registry uses the Coulomb Green's function,
$$G_C(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi r}.$$
In words, a point charge creates a potential whose strength falls as inverse distance in three spatial dimensions. Physical constants such as $1/\epsilon_0$ are suppressed in the registry form. Because the photon is massless, the electromagnetic interaction has long range, although real atoms are neutral overall and fields can be screened by surrounding charges [@jackson1999].

The quantum atomic problem is governed by a Hamiltonian. For hydrogen, the Schrödinger equation with a Coulomb potential gives discrete bound states and a continuum above ionisation. The Bohr radius, $a_0\approx5.29\times10^{-11}$ m, sets the size of the hydrogen ground state. Energy gaps are typically electron-volts, and photon emission or absorption occurs when the atom changes between allowed energy levels. Spectral lines are among the most precise measurements in physics and astronomy.

For many-electron atoms, electron-electron repulsion, spin, the Pauli principle, and relativistic corrections make the problem more complex. Nevertheless, shell structure explains the periodic table's broad organisation: noble gases have filled shells, alkali metals have a single outer electron, and transition metals involve partially filled $d$ orbitals. Atomic clocks, lasers, spectroscopy, plasma physics, and condensed-matter physics all rely on atomic transition frequencies and electromagnetic response.

This level is the bridge from nuclear identity to chemical behaviour. The nucleus supplies charge and mass; the electron cloud supplies size, polarisation, spectra, and bonding possibilities. The governing variables are therefore mixed: nuclear charge and isotope data from below, plus electron quantum numbers, spin, angular momentum, and electromagnetic selection rules at the atomic scale.

Atomic physics is also where matter becomes a precision clock. Caesium hyperfine transitions define the SI second, optical lattice clocks compare frequencies at parts in $10^{18}$, and astronomical spectra identify elements across the observable universe. Ionisation, excitation, recombination, and line broadening connect this level to plasmas, stars, lasers, and chemical reactions. The ordinary science is strongly measured; the interpretive risk is to forget that orbitals are probability amplitudes, not little paths.

Atoms can be neutral, ionised, trapped, excited, polarised, or embedded in solids and molecules. Their response depends on boundary conditions: vacuum, plasma, lattice, solvent, magnetic field, electric field, and radiation environment. That is why the same element can appear as a sharp clock transition in one setting and a broadened spectral line in a hot star. The atlas keeps the ideal atom and the contextual atom together.

## How it was found and measured

Nineteenth-century spectroscopy revealed regular spectral lines before atoms had a full quantum theory. Rutherford's scattering experiments placed the positive charge in a tiny nucleus, while Bohr's 1913 model explained hydrogen lines by quantised angular momentum [@rutherford1911; @bohr1913]. Schroedinger's wave equation then replaced Bohr orbits with wavefunctions and energy eigenstates [@schrodinger1926].

Atomic measurements now use spectroscopy, ion traps, atomic beams, lasers, interferometry, tunnelling microscopy, and precision frequency comparison. The hydrogen spectrum, Lamb shift, hyperfine splitting, Zeeman effect, and fine structure test quantum electrodynamics at high precision. In astronomy, absorption and emission lines reveal composition, velocity, temperature, magnetic field, and redshift. What is measured is not an orbital photograph but transition frequency, scattering pattern, ionisation energy, or probability density reconstructed through experiment.

Important dates also mark changing instruments: cathode-ray and scattering experiments separated electron from nucleus; diffraction and spectroscopy made structure quantitative; lasers made coherent control routine; scanning tunnelling microscopy later imaged surface electron density. Each method measures an interaction with the atom, not the atom as a miniature solar system. The evidence is therefore response evidence from the start, built from frequencies, deflections, currents, line intensities, lifetimes, linewidths, polarisation, recoil shifts, clock comparisons, isotope shifts, Stark shifts, Zeeman splitting, and transition probabilities.

## Objects at this level

### Hydrogen atom

Hydrogen is the simplest bound atom: one proton and one electron. Its Coulomb potential gives analytic energy levels in the non-relativistic approximation, and corrections test quantum electrodynamics. The [T]-Theory reading treats it as the cleanest atomic response grammar `derived-under-assumptions`: a charge source defines a kernel, while spectroscopy records allowed responses rather than mental content `interpretive`.

### Ion

An ion is an atom with unequal proton and electron counts. Its net charge changes how it responds to electric and magnetic fields, forms plasmas, and binds in salts or traps. Ordinary physics governs it through Coulomb forces, quantised states, and collisions. In [T]-Theory an ion is a boundary-altered response surface `interpretive`: changing electron number changes the kernel seen by neighbouring systems.

### Rydberg atom

A Rydberg atom has an electron in a very high principal quantum number. It can be large on atomic terms, highly polarisable, and sensitive to fields. Such atoms are used in precision and quantum-information experiments. The [T]-Theory reading treats the exaggerated scale as a visible response amplifier `interpretive`: small fields shift allowed states, but the system remains ordinary atomic physics.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary atomic science is mature, but open work remains in many-electron correlation, precision tests of fundamental constants, isotope-shift anomalies, highly charged ions, antimatter spectroscopy, and quantum-control limits. Complex atoms still require approximations, and ultra-precise measurements can expose physics beyond the Standard Model.

For [T]-Theory, this level tests whether the word "information" stays technical `open-hypothesis`. Atomic information must mean quantum state, occupation, transition, phase, or spectrum. If later levels use atomic language to imply proto-feeling or intention, the claim is unsupported. A useful extension would show how atomic response variables are compressed into molecular bond variables and where that compression breaks, for example in spectroscopy, photochemistry, or isotope effects `derived-under-assumptions`.

Possible falsifiers are straightforward. If an alleged cross-scale atomic analogy ignores selection rules, conservation laws, or environmental decoherence, it should be rejected. If it preserves these constraints and predicts which perturbations couple into molecular behaviour, it can remain as a labelled modelling bridge.
