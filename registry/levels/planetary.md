## In ordinary science

The planetary level treats a whole planet or large planetary body as a coupled physical system: solid interior, mantle, crust, surface terrain, ocean or volatile layer, atmosphere, cryosphere, ionosphere, magnetosphere, and space environment. Measurable variables include gravity, topography, heat flow, mantle viscosity, plate velocity, pressure, wind, temperature, humidity, salinity, sea level, magnetic field, albedo, greenhouse-gas concentration, radiative flux, tidal forcing, and impact history. Lengths run from hundreds to thousands of kilometres; times run from minutes for atmospheric or seismic waves, months to years for ocean climate modes, and millions of years for mantle and carbon-cycle feedbacks.

The registry equation, $\rho(D_t\mathbf u)=-\nabla P+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u+\rho\mathbf g$, is a Navier--Stokes-style momentum balance with gravity. In words, fluid or slowly deforming material changes velocity because pressure gradients push it, viscosity diffuses momentum, and gravity drives buoyancy and weight. Rotating planets add Coriolis terms, stratification, phase changes, radiation, chemistry, magnetic fields, and boundary conditions. The equation is therefore a doorway into a family of geophysical-fluid and mantle-flow models, not a complete climate model by itself [@vallis2017atmospheric; @holton2013introduction].

Planetary science includes familiar wave and feedback phenomena. Rossby waves organise large-scale weather and ocean circulation. ENSO couples tropical Pacific winds, sea-surface temperature, and thermocline depth. Internal waves move energy through stratified oceans. Mantle convection moves heat from the interior. Free oscillations after great earthquakes make a planet ring in normal modes. Magnetospheres respond to solar wind, and auroras show that coupling in visible light. Climate models combine fluid dynamics, radiation, clouds, aerosols, biogeochemistry, land surface, ice, and human forcing [@ipcc2021ar6wg1]. Tipping-element research asks when feedbacks can shift parts of the Earth system into new regimes [@lenton2008tipping].

The ordinary scientific boundary is explicit: planets regulate energy and material flows, but they do not need consciousness to do so. Stability, oscillation, and transition are physical system properties. Planetary measurements are multi-instrument: seismometers, satellites, landers, rovers, ocean floats, weather stations, magnetometers, telescopes, ice cores, impact craters, and geochemical archives each sample a different layer of the coupled system over deep planetary time.

The catalogue objects assigned here include terrestrial planets, gas giants, ice giants, dwarf planets, moons, atmospheres, cloud layers, oceans, auroras, and surface terrains. Some small irregular moons, rings, comets, and asteroid belts are better treated at orbital-system scale because their dominant display variable is orbit rather than whole-body interior or fluid response.

## How it was found and measured

Planetary science grew from astronomy, geodesy, geology, meteorology, oceanography, geomagnetism, and space exploration. Telescopes revealed phases, moons, rings, bands, spots, polar caps, and changing atmospheres. Geodesy measured Earth's shape and gravity. Seismology showed that a planet can be probed by waves; Earth's normal modes after large earthquakes gave a whole-planet spectrum [@aki2002quantitative]. Meteorology and oceanography added fluid measurements, while spacecraft made other worlds measurable through imaging, spectroscopy, gravity tracking, magnetometers, radar, and in situ sampling.

For Earth, satellites now measure temperature, clouds, sea level, ice, vegetation, gravity change, atmospheric composition, and radiation. Ocean floats profile temperature and salinity; weather stations and reanalyses track atmospheric state; palaeoclimate archives extend records beyond instruments. For other planets and moons, missions infer interiors from gravity, rotation, tides, topography, heat flow, magnetic fields, and surface chemistry. The same physical principles apply, but data quality differs sharply between Earth, Mars, Jupiter, exoplanets, and small icy moons.

## Objects at this level

### Terrestrial and surface worlds

Terrestrial planets and rocky surface worlds are differentiated bodies with crust, mantle, core, terrain, impact record, volcanism, and possible tectonics. Their ordinary dynamics involve heat transport, gravity, rheology, surface processes, and volatile inventory [@turcotte2014geodynamics]. `interpretive`: The Atlas reads them as layered response systems. Mineral and regolith details are integrated into terrain, lithosphere, and heat-flow variables.

### Gas and ice giants

Gas giants and ice giants are massive fluid planets governed by hydrostatic balance, convection, rotation, storms, atmospheric jets, magnetic dynamos, and radiation [@vallis2017atmospheric]. Gas giants are hydrogen-helium dominated; ice giants contain larger volatile-rich components. `interpretive`: Their banded atmospheres and magnetic fields are response channels. Cloud chemistry is not ignored, but it is averaged into flow, opacity, and interior-state variables at this scale.

### Dwarf planets and moons

Dwarf planets and regular moons are rounded bodies shaped by self-gravity, impacts, tides, volatile transport, differentiation, and orbital environment. Some moons host oceans or strong tidal heating. Ordinary models combine geophysics with celestial mechanics [@murray1999solar]. `interpretive`: The Atlas treats them as planetary when whole-body geology, tide, or volatile response dominates. Small irregular satellites remain mainly orbital objects when trajectory is the principal variable.

### Atmospheres and cloud layers

Planetary atmospheres are gaseous envelopes governed by hydrostatic balance, radiative transfer, chemistry, circulation, and weather. Cloud layers condense, scatter, absorb, and move with winds [@holton2013introduction]. `interpretive`: The response grammar treats stellar radiation, surface flux, rotation, and composition as sources. Individual molecules and droplets are integrated out into pressure, temperature, humidity, opacity, and wind fields.

### Oceans and ocean worlds

Surface and subsurface oceans are liquid layers governed by geophysical fluid dynamics, stratification, tides, heat, salinity, and boundary exchange [@vallis2017atmospheric]. On Earth they couple strongly to climate; on icy moons they may sit beneath ice shells. `interpretive`: The Atlas reads ocean layers as delayed response reservoirs. Heat, wind, and tides enter as impulses, while turbulence is averaged into circulation and mixing variables.

### Auroras and magnetospheres

Auroras occur when magnetospheric particles precipitate into upper atmospheres and excite emission lines. Their ordinary physics uses plasma motion, Lorentz forces, magnetic reconnection, field-aligned currents, and atmospheric radiation. `interpretive`: In the Atlas they are visible boundary responses to solar-wind forcing. Particle cascades are integrated out into luminous bands, not treated as evidence of planetary feeling.

## The [T]-Theory reading

`interpretive`: The programme reads the planetary level as a whole-system response field in which mantle, ocean, atmosphere, surface, and magnetic boundary conditions filter impulses. `derived-under-assumptions`: Geological stress histories are carried upward, but individual faults, folds, outcrops, and formations are integrated out into heat flux, convection, circulation, terrain, and boundary conditions. In *The Tensor: Formal Structure of the Universal Somatic Field*, planetary language functions as scale grammar, not proof of planetary sentience.

`interpretive`: The useful carry-over is nested regulation, delay, and feedback across layers and timescales. A solar-wind impulse can be filtered by a magnetosphere; an earthquake can excite normal modes; greenhouse forcing can alter radiative balance; interior heat can drive convection. These are ordinary physical responses first. `open-hypothesis`: Any analogy between planetary regulation and somatic regulation remains a proposed comparison requiring explicit variables, baselines, and discriminating tests before Atlas use. `interpretive`: No planetary consciousness, clinical mechanism, or climate prediction follows from the Atlas overlay.

`derived-under-assumptions`: What moves upward to later stellar levels is not a planet's local weather but the idea of a layered body responding through boundary conditions. The response field is multi-material, multi-rate, and measurable. `interpretive`: The overlay must remain compatible with lens-off science; it cannot overrule climate physics, geodesy, seismology, or spacecraft data.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary planetary science still asks how plate tectonics begins, how dynamos start and stop, how giant-planet interiors are layered, how clouds and aerosols affect climate sensitivity, how ocean worlds exchange heat and chemistry, and how exoplanet atmospheres should be interpreted from sparse spectra. Earth-system prediction also faces uncertainty in clouds, ice sheets, carbon-cycle feedbacks, and human forcing [@ipcc2021ar6wg1].

`open-hypothesis`: A [T]-Theory planetary reading could be tested by choosing a physical impulse and asking whether a stated layered-response model predicts timing, damping, or spatial pattern better than a simpler baseline. Free oscillations, solar-wind storms, ENSO teleconnections, volcanic aerosol forcing, and tidal heating are candidate domains. It would be weakened if it made claims about planetary feeling, ignored established geophysics, or failed to specify variables and falsifiable response metrics. `interpretive`: The strongest claim is disciplined analogy: planets are response systems without being minds.
