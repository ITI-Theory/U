## In ordinary science

The whole-brain scale covers coordinated neural activity across cortical layers, subcortical nuclei, white-matter tracts, cerebrospinal fluid, vasculature, and the skull boundary. The characteristic length is decimetres; the timescales range from millisecond spikes to seconds-long rhythms and minutes-long state changes. EEG and MEG measure population activity indirectly: synchronised postsynaptic currents produce electric potentials and magnetic fields that can be detected outside the head, although inverse reconstruction is ill-posed and spatially filtered [@nolte2004identifying]. The same neural event may appear differently in scalp EEG, MEG, intracranial electrodes, functional MRI, or behaviour because each instrument samples a different projection of the underlying activity.

The registry equation $(\nabla^2+k_{\mathrm{CEMI}}^2)G=\delta$ is a Helmholtz-style Green-function display. In words, the spatial curvature of a response $G$ plus a wavelength-setting term $k_{\mathrm{CEMI}}^2$ balances a point source $\delta$. This is a compact way to picture how a local neural source can contribute to a macroscopic electromagnetic pattern. Boundary conditions matter: skull, scalp, cerebrospinal fluid, cortical folding, and tissue conductivity all shape which fields are measured. The equation is not, by itself, proof that electromagnetic fields are the seat of consciousness.

Mainstream neuroscience already uses field language carefully. Local field potentials reflect summed transmembrane currents; coherence and phase coupling can mark communication between areas; and rhythms may regulate when signals are likely to affect downstream targets [@fries2005mechanism]. CEMI theory, associated especially with McFadden, is a stronger hypothesis: conscious information is proposed to be physically integrated in the brain's endogenous electromagnetic field [@mcfadden2002a; @mcfadden2002b]. That proposal remains debated. Evidence that extracellular fields can modulate neuronal activity supports the plausibility of field effects in principle, not the full CEMI theory [@anastassiou2011ephaptic; @jefferys1995nonsynaptic].

At whole-brain scale many microscopic facts are averaged away. One does not track each vesicle or each ion channel. The variables become source distributions, phase relations, frequency bands, coupling matrices, boundary conditions, network states, arousal, attention, and behavioural or report measures. Typical data come from EEG, MEG, intracranial recordings, stimulation studies, lesion evidence, imaging, autonomic measures, and task reports, each with different spatial and temporal limits. Consciousness theories such as global workspace and integrated information provide other frameworks for the same scale [@baars1988cognitive; @dehaene2014code; @tononi2004iit].

The scale is therefore both physical and inferential. The fields are physical consequences of currents, but the psychological variables associated with them are inferred through reports, tasks, physiology, and intervention. A robust whole-brain account must keep those layers separate: a rhythm may mark sleep, attention, seizure, or artefact depending on context, sensor placement, and source model.

## How it was found and measured

Whole-brain electrophysiology began with surface and exposed-cortex measurements before scalp EEG became a human instrument. Berger reported the human electroencephalogram in 1929, including rhythmic activity that later became central to clinical and cognitive EEG [@berger1929elektrenkephalogramm]. MEG emerged when Cohen measured magnetic fields associated with alpha rhythm currents in 1968, later improved by superconducting sensors and shielding [@cohen1968magnetoencephalography]. Intracranial EEG, electrocorticography, stimulation mapping, lesion studies, and neuroimaging added complementary views. Functional MRI measures haemodynamic consequences rather than electrical fields; diffusion imaging estimates white-matter structure; transcranial magnetic or electrical stimulation perturbs activity from outside the skull. Source reconstruction remains an inverse problem: many source configurations can explain the same external field. That limitation is not a defect of one instrument but a mathematical feature of measuring the brain through boundaries.

## Objects at this level

### EEG rhythm

An EEG rhythm is a scalp-measured voltage pattern, often described by frequency bands such as delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma. It is governed by synchronised postsynaptic currents, tissue conduction, sensor montage, state, and task. The [T]-Theory reading treats the rhythm as an empirical whole-brain field trace `empirical-result`; interpreting it as a conscious threshold is a separate `open-hypothesis`.

### MEG source field

An MEG source field is the magnetic expression of neural current flow, especially tangential cortical sources. It is governed by Maxwellian electromagnetism, tissue geometry, sensor sensitivity, and inverse modelling. In the programme it provides a disciplined example of source-in, field-response-out grammar `interpretive`. It does not make the inverse solution unique or make the measured field identical with experience `open-hypothesis`.

### Resting-state network

A resting-state network is a reproducible pattern of correlated activity measured when no explicit task is imposed. Its ordinary study uses fMRI, EEG, MEG, and graph analysis to describe coordination among regions. The [T]-Theory reading treats such networks as large-scale boundary and coupling patterns `interpretive`, while leaving cognitive or affective meaning to separately testable hypotheses `open-hypothesis`.

### Endogenous electromagnetic field

The endogenous electromagnetic field is the physical electric and magnetic field generated by brain currents. It is real as a measured phenomenon, but its causal role varies with strength, geometry, frequency, and tissue state. The CEMI reading treats this field as a candidate integration medium `open-hypothesis`; the safer claim is only that neural activity produces measurable fields `empirical-result`.

### Stimulation-defined state change

A stimulation-defined state change is a reproducible alteration in activity or behaviour following electrical, magnetic, sensory, or pharmacological perturbation. It is governed by source location, field strength, receptor state, network excitability, and safety limits. The programme reads such perturbations as possible tests of whole-brain response kernels `open-hypothesis`. It does not treat every stimulation effect as evidence for CEMI consciousness `open-hypothesis`.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The programme uses the CEMI layer as an `open-hypothesis`: a candidate brain-scale field in which local circuit responses are integrated into a whole-brain propagator. The substrate is neural tissue and its endogenous electromagnetic field; the field variable is a Green's-function response constrained by cortical boundaries, tissue conductivity, and source currents `interpretive`. The compact Helmholtz display is a `derived-under-assumptions` modelling surface, not a derivation of consciousness from Maxwell's equations.

In *The Missing Limbic Layer: A Somatic Field Extension of Hopfield Networks via the Correspondence Principle*, Hopfield attractors are read `interpretive`ly as emotional basins, not as established diagnoses. Threshold-consciousness claims remain `open-hypothesis`, even where the Lean surface contains `kernel-verified` real-number dichotomies. A formal dichotomy can prove a statement about a defined amplitude and threshold; it does not prove that a measured brain field has that amplitude or that consciousness has been located. This level carries up distributed phase, amplitude, coupling, source geometry, and boundary conditions `interpretive`. It integrates out the exact cellular route by which each source current was produced `derived-under-assumptions`. The level does not claim that CEMI is proved, that trauma therapy follows, that EEG alone reads a mind, or that a magnetic field alone is a person `open-hypothesis`.

## Open questions and tests

The unresolved ordinary questions are substantial: how large-scale dynamics relate to report, attention, sleep, anaesthesia, seizure, and behaviour; how to combine electrical, magnetic, haemodynamic, and anatomical data; and how much causal influence endogenous fields exert on neurons. The [T]-Theory CEMI reading would need discriminating tests against global-workspace, integrated-information, predictive-processing, and conventional neural-field accounts `open-hypothesis`. A strong test would perturb the electromagnetic field while controlling synaptic activity and ask whether predicted changes in reportable state or integration follow the proposed Green response `open-hypothesis`. The reading would be weakened if field variables add no predictive or causal power beyond source-local neural activity, connectivity, and arousal measures `open-hypothesis`.

A further test concerns boundaries. If skull, cerebrospinal fluid, cortical folding, and conductivity changes alter measured fields exactly as ordinary forward models predict, the CEMI layer must respect those constraints `derived-under-assumptions`. If the programme predicts field thresholds, it must specify how they are estimated from real sensors and how artefact, movement, muscle activity, and vascular signals are excluded `open-hypothesis`.
