## In ordinary science

Compact objects are the dense end states of stellar evolution: white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes. Their radii can be Earth-sized, city-sized, or defined by an event horizon, while their masses are comparable to stars. Ordinary matter physics breaks into degenerate-electron pressure for white dwarfs, nuclear-density matter and strong magnetic fields for neutron stars, and spacetime geometry for black holes [@shapiro1983black].

The registry equation, $G_{14}(t)\propto e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{ring}}}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{QNM}}t)$, is a ringdown response. A disturbed compact object emits a decaying oscillation: $\omega_{\mathrm{QNM}}$ is the quasi-normal-mode frequency, and $\tau_{\mathrm{ring}}$ is the damping time. In general relativity the black-hole ringdown is set mainly by mass and spin. For neutron stars, matter equations of state also shape oscillations, tidal deformability, and merger remnants.

Observations include pulsar timing, X-ray binaries, kilonovae, accretion discs, gravitational waves, and horizon-scale interferometry. GW150914 showed that merging black holes can be detected by measuring strains of order $10^{-21}$ on Earth [@abbott2016gw]. The Event Horizon Telescope imaged horizon-scale emission around M87*, confirming compact shadow-scale structure in a plasma environment [@eht2019m87]. Hawking radiation remains a theoretical prediction, negligible for astrophysical black holes but central to the information problem [@hawking1975].

This is strong-gravity physics, not ordinary vibration in a material bell. The bell metaphor is useful only because a perturbation can be decomposed into a finite set of damped modes.

Compact objects also force observers to infer structure indirectly. A pulsar clock tests gravity through timing residuals; an accretion disc maps the potential through temperature and line broadening; a merger waveform encodes masses, spins, tides, and distance. The variables are few compared with the microphysics, but those few variables are measured with extraordinary precision.

This compression is why compact objects anchor strong-field tests: the signal is sparse, but the allowed physical parameters are tightly constrained.

White dwarfs are supported by electron degeneracy pressure and have a limiting mass of order the Chandrasekhar mass. Neutron stars are supported by nuclear-density matter whose equation of state is still uncertain; pulsars reveal them as rotating magnetised clocks. Black holes are described externally by mass, spin, and charge in classical general relativity, although astrophysical charge is expected to be negligible. Around all three, ordinary gas can become extraordinary: accretion converts gravitational binding energy into X-rays, jets, winds, and relativistic particles.

## How it was found and measured

The compact-object level was assembled piece by piece. Sirius B made white dwarfs unavoidable when a faint companion was found to have stellar mass in an Earth-sized volume. Jocelyn Bell Burnell and Antony Hewish's 1967 discovery of pulsars revealed rotating neutron stars, and the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar later showed orbital decay consistent with gravitational-wave emission [@hulse1975binarypulsar]. X-ray astronomy identified accreting neutron stars and black-hole candidates through compact, variable high-energy emission.

The twenty-first century added direct strong-field probes. LIGO's detection of GW150914 measured a binary black-hole inspiral, merger, and ringdown [@abbott2016gw]. The Event Horizon Telescope resolved shadow-scale emission around M87* [@eht2019m87]. Multi-messenger observations of neutron-star mergers combine gravitational waves, gamma rays, kilonova light, and heavy-element production. These instruments do not photograph interiors; they infer compact parameters from timing, spectra, images, and waveforms.

## Objects at this level

### White dwarfs

White dwarfs are compact stellar remnants supported by electron degeneracy pressure. They cool over time, accrete in binaries, and can produce novae or Type Ia supernovae if conditions drive runaway thermonuclear burning. Their ordinary variables are mass, radius, temperature, composition, cooling age, and binary environment. The [T]-Theory reading treats them as compact boundary conditions with long thermal memory `interpretive`, not as subjects `interpretive`.

### Neutron stars, pulsars, and magnetars

Neutron stars compress more than a solar mass into a city-sized radius. Pulsars are rotating magnetised neutron stars whose beams sweep across Earth; magnetars have extreme magnetic fields and can flare in high-energy bursts. Classically they test dense matter, strong gravity, and plasma magnetospheres. The atlas reading treats their timing residuals and flares as measurable response outputs from a compact state `interpretive`.

### Pulsars

Pulsars deserve a separate catalogue entry because their clock-like timing makes compact physics measurable. Spin-down, glitches, binary delays, dispersion, and relativistic corrections turn a rotating neutron star into a precision instrument. The ordinary model is magnetic rotation plus magnetospheric plasma. The [T]-Theory reading treats pulse arrival times as response data `interpretive`: interior and magnetosphere are inferred from residuals, not personified.

### Black holes

Astrophysical black holes are regions where an event horizon bounds causal escape in general relativity. Their external classical parameters are mass and spin, while surrounding plasma supplies most electromagnetic light. Mergers produce gravitational-wave ringdowns set by quasi-normal modes. The [T]-Theory reading uses black holes as a strict response example: infer boundary parameters from the returned signal `interpretive`, without claiming interior experience `interpretive`.

### Accretion discs

Accretion discs are rotating gas flows that lose angular momentum and fall inward around compact objects or young stars. Viscosity, magnetic turbulence, radiation, and relativistic gravity convert potential energy into heat and jets. Their spectra and variability map the central potential. In the atlas reading, a disc is a sheared response surface: forcing becomes temperature gradients, lines, flicker, and inflow history `interpretive`.

### Relativistic jets

Relativistic jets are narrow outflows launched near compact objects and active galactic nuclei. Magnetic fields, rotation, accretion, and black-hole spin are leading ingredients, with details still under study [@blandford1977electromagnetic]. They carry energy across scales into lobes and shocks. The [T]-Theory reading treats a jet as collimated response grammar `interpretive`: stored rotational and magnetic structure becomes directed transport, not intention `interpretive`.

### Theoretical wormholes

Wormholes are mathematical solutions in relativistic theories that connect separated regions of spacetime. No observational evidence establishes an astrophysical wormhole, and traversable versions require exotic conditions not known to occur. They belong here only as theoretical compact-geometry possibilities. The atlas reading must therefore label them `open-hypothesis`: useful for testing boundary language, not for claiming detected objects or hidden passageways.

### Quasars and active galactic nuclei

Quasars and active galactic nuclei are powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes. Their luminosity comes from compact gravitational energy release, even though their jets, radiation, and host-galaxy effects extend far beyond the centre. Classical modelling combines general relativity, accretion physics, radiative transfer, and feedback. The [T]-Theory reading treats the central engine as a compact response source `interpretive`, with host detail integrated out.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The programme uses compact objects as the extreme case of response grammar: a sharp gravitational impulse leaves a ringdown kernel, so boundary conditions are read from the return signal `interpretive` [@johnson2026usf]. It carries up stellar mass, spin, charge limits, and merger history, while detailed plasma turbulence and unresolved microphysics are integrated out into effective mass, spin, mode frequency, and damping `derived-under-assumptions`.

This entry does not claim that black holes, neutron stars, or horizons have subjective states `interpretive`. It also does not solve the information paradox `open-hypothesis`. The [T]-Theory comparison is limited to propagator form and state reconstruction; any stronger M-theory or consciousness reading remains an `open-hypothesis`.

The evidence status is therefore deliberately narrow: observed ringdowns are empirical gravitational-wave results, while the cross-scale atlas use of ringdown as a response archetype is `interpretive`.

Compact objects also police the language of information. In physics, information may mean waveform parameters, entropy, quantum state, or recoverable boundary data. The atlas uses only the response sense unless a stronger claim is explicitly formalised `interpretive`. A quasi-normal mode is a damped return, not a thought. An accretion flare is plasma dynamics, not emotion. The programme's live question is whether response kernels at this extreme can be compared safely with kernels elsewhere without erasing their different substrates `open-hypothesis`.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary open questions include the neutron-star equation of state, black-hole information, jet launching, accretion turbulence, horizon-scale plasma modelling, and whether ringdown spectroscopy can reveal deviations from general relativity. Observations from gravitational-wave detectors, X-ray timing, radio pulsar arrays, neutrino searches, and very-long-baseline interferometry constrain these questions.

For [T]-Theory, a testable claim would need to say which compact-object observable changes under the programme: a mode spectrum, damping hierarchy, jet correlation, or information-accounting rule `open-hypothesis`. If it cannot alter or organise predictions beyond standard strong-gravity and plasma models, it remains an interpretive atlas analogy `interpretive`.
