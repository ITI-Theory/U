## In ordinary science

A human group is many separate people coupled through sight, sound, movement, shared tasks, rules, and place. The scale runs from a family at a table or a quartet on a small stage to a meeting, congregation, theatre audience, protest, stadium crowd, choir, band, or assembly. The ordinary object is not a single enlarged person. Each participant has their own body, history, attention, and reportable experience. The group-level question is how timing, attention, information, emotion, and norms propagate when many bounded persons respond to one another and to common sources.

The registry equation is the Kuramoto phase model,
$\dot\theta_i=\omega_i+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)$. In words, each participant or oscillator has a phase $\theta_i$, a preferred rate $\omega_i$, and a coupling term that pulls it toward the phases of others when coupling strength $K$ is large enough. This is a minimal model, not a full sociology. It is useful because group synchrony often begins as a timing problem: clapping locks into rhythm, singers align breath and pitch entrances, dancers follow pulse, a crowd wave travels around a stadium, and a meeting settles into turn-taking. Synchronisation theory gives a precise vocabulary for locking, detuning, noise, delay, and failure [@kuramoto1984chemical; @acebron2005kuramoto; @strogatz2003sync; @neda2000physics].

Groups also need variables that oscillator models do not contain. Roles, authority, status, kinship, ritual, seating, architecture, amplification, policing, rehearsal, shared repertoire, language, and institutional rules can decide which signals matter and whose signals are ignored. Crowd and pedestrian models show that density, desired direction, obstacles, and local interactions can produce lanes, jams, waves, and dangerous compressions [@helbing1995social]. Social theory adds norms, legitimacy, collective categories, and institutions: an assembly is not only many bodies in a room; it is many bodies in a recognised procedure [@durkheim1912elementary; @north1990institutions].

The relevant time scale ranges from seconds for a clap, shout, turn, or crowd wave to hours for a rehearsal, service, hearing, or performance. The relevant length scale ranges from a room to a square, station, arena, or online channel. Ordinary measurements include audio envelopes, video tracking, motion capture, attendance records, conversation analysis, surveys, network data, ethnography, and event logs. The group exists as a measurable coordination pattern only when its membership, boundary, task, medium, and variables are declared.

## How it was found and measured

Human groups were first studied through observation before they were modelled as dynamical systems. Ethnographers, sociologists, psychologists, music researchers, crowd engineers, and urban scientists all developed instruments for making the many-person pattern visible. Durkheim made ritual and collective life central to sociology, while later institutional analysis treated rules and routines as durable constraints on action [@durkheim1895rules; @durkheim1912elementary; @north1990institutions]. Crowd science added measured density, flow, lane formation, panic risk, and evacuation dynamics [@helbing1995social].

Synchrony became measurable through clocks, microphones, cameras, physiological sensors, and phase extraction. Rhythmic applause is a clean example because thousands of hands can move from noisy approval into a temporary common pulse [@neda2000physics]. Music research extends this to ensembles and audiences: pulse, metre, entrainment, expectation, and emotion can be logged as time-varying response rather than only as retrospective report [@large1999dynamics; @juslin2013]. Contemporary studies can combine wearable sensors, video keypoints, room acoustics, ticketing data, ethnographic notes, and social-network records. The safest measurements keep individual time series visible while estimating the group-level pattern.

## Objects at this level

### Families

A family group is governed by kinship, care, habit, space, obligation, and memory as well as moment-to-moment timing. Ordinary analysis may track conversation, routines, decision rules, resource flow, and emotional climate. The [T]-Theory reading treats family coordination as many-person forcing through repeated roles and memory kernels `interpretive`. It does not turn the family into one feeling subject `open-hypothesis`.

### Teams

A team is a task-bound group with roles, goals, training, communication channels, and feedback. Sport, surgery, emergency response, research, and logistics teams all coordinate under constraints. Classical variables include role clarity, timing, expertise, shared displays, error correction, and authority. In the programme, a team can be read as a temporary propagator that lowers coordination cost `interpretive`, provided legitimacy and power are not erased `open-hypothesis`.

### Audiences

An audience receives a common source while also sensing itself. Applause, laughter, silence, attention, coughing, standing, leaving, and call-response can propagate across the room. Ordinary accounts must include acoustics, sight lines, genre convention, ticketing, seating, and expectation. The [T]-Theory reading treats the audience as coupled response paths around a performance source `interpretive`, not as one listener `open-hypothesis`.

### Choirs and bands

Choirs and bands are disciplined synchrony machines. Breath, pulse, tuning, cueing, score, memory, conducting, and room acoustics constrain the timing field. The ordinary model can use coupled oscillators for entrances and phase locking, with musicians remaining distinct agents. The [T]-Theory overlay reads rehearsal and performance as controlled forcing through sound, gesture, and prediction `interpretive`.

### Congregations

A congregation is a group assembled around ritual, calendar, text, authority, place, and shared practice. Chant, prayer, posture, call-response, procession, and silence can coordinate bodies, but meaning and norm are not reducible to timing. The programme may model repeated ritual as a boundary condition and memory kernel `interpretive`; it must not claim that synchrony proves belief, care, or unity `open-hypothesis`.

### Crowds and crowd waves

A crowd is many bodies in shared space, often under strong density, visibility, and motion constraints. Crowd waves, queues, crush risks, and pedestrian lanes show how local responses can make a large pattern. The [T]-Theory reading treats a wave as a propagating response through coupled bodies `interpretive`. Safety, power, policing, architecture, and exit capacity remain ordinary variables, not decorative context `open-hypothesis`.

### Assemblies

An assembly is a group with a recognised procedure: meeting, council, court, parliament, union hall, citizens' forum, or public hearing. It coordinates speech rights, agenda, voting, record, authority, and appeal. Classical analysis belongs to institutional and political sociology as much as synchrony. The programme reads the assembly as a group field stabilised by rules and artefacts `interpretive`, not by shared consciousness `open-hypothesis`.

## The [T]-Theory reading

At the human-group level, the programme keeps the dyadic response grammar but replaces one link with many-to-many coupling `interpretive`. A person can still be modelled as a bounded organismal field, and a dyad can still be modelled as two coupled fields. A group adds a coupling matrix, shared sources, common rhythms, spatial boundaries, roles, and slow norm variables `derived-under-assumptions`. The registry's Kuramoto equation is the simplest timing slice of that larger picture, useful for clapping, choir entries, chanting, marching, or crowd waves when phase is the relevant observable `derived-under-assumptions`.

The level is also a boundary against overclaim. Each person feels; the group propagates and coordinates `interpretive`. A group can amplify fear, calm, attention, or tempo through visible and audible channels, but the atlas does not claim a group mind, a collective subject, or a proof of consciousness above the persons `open-hypothesis`. *The Geographic Somatic Field: Scale-Invariant Wave Propagation in Human Landscapes* supplies the social-kernel reading, while *Swarm Propagator: From Local Consensus to Precomputed Global Response in Distributed Agent Networks* supplies a coordination-cost analogy `derived-under-assumptions`. Both are mappings under declared variables, not licences to erase meaning, norm, history, or power `interpretive`.

What is carried up from the dyad is delay, coupling, response, and boundary discipline `interpretive`. What is integrated out is most person-by-person internal detail unless it changes measured group dynamics. What is added is membership, density, role, medium, rule, shared source, and collective timing `derived-under-assumptions`.

## Open questions and tests

The ordinary open question is when a group-level variable predicts better than a collection of individual variables. A useful test would compare models of the same event: individual baselines, dyadic coupling, common-source response, network contagion, and a declared group propagator `open-hypothesis`. A choir, applause episode, emergency drill, or public meeting could supply audio, video, seating, role, and survey data. The group model should predict onset, locking, wave travel, breakdown, or repair better than simpler alternatives `open-hypothesis`.

The [T]-Theory risk is psychologism at scale. If the model predicts synchrony but the relevant social variable is legitimacy, exclusion, coercion, or norm recognition, the operator is missing the point `open-hypothesis`. If the same kernel is used for a family, band, congregation, and crowd without changing state variables, it has become metaphor rather than model `interpretive`. The level is therefore refutable in practice: poor prediction, wrong boundaries, or participant accounts that contradict the chosen variables should force revision `open-hypothesis`.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** Show a room, hall, square, or stadium with many separate people. Some face a shared source, some interact locally, and visible cues mark roles: conductor, speaker, team member, audience, queue, or moving crowd. Use arrows or traces only for ordinary timing, sound, motion, and sight lines.
- **Lens on:** Add a many-to-many response overlay: phase rings for synchrony, delayed coupling links, shared-source waves, and slower norm or rule boundaries around the group. Keep individual bodies distinct. The overlay should show coordination and propagation, not a fused group aura or group mind.
