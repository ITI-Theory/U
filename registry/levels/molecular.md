## In ordinary science

The molecular level begins when atoms form stable arrangements through shared, transferred, or correlated electron density. Typical lengths are tenths of a nanometre for bonds and nanometres for small biomolecules. Bond energies are usually electron-volts, thermal energies at room temperature are about $k_BT\approx0.026$ eV, and vibrational motions often occur on femtosecond to picosecond timescales. Molecular shape matters because the electron distribution, nuclear positions, and surrounding environment jointly set the Hamiltonian [@atkins2014physicalchemistry].

The registry displays the compact quantum relation
$$H\psi=E\psi.$$
In words, $H$ is the Hamiltonian operator encoding kinetic energy and interactions, $\psi$ is the molecular wavefunction or approximate electronic state, and $E$ is an allowed energy. In practical chemistry, the Born-Oppenheimer approximation often separates fast electronic motion from slower nuclear motion, allowing chemists to compute potential-energy surfaces for molecular geometry [@bornoppenheimer1927].

Chemical bonds are not tiny rods. A covalent bond is a lower-energy arrangement of electron density shared between nuclei; ionic bonding is dominated by electrostatic attraction; hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions are weaker but crucial for liquids, crystals, proteins, and DNA. Molecular orbitals, density functional theory, spectroscopy, diffraction, cryo-electron microscopy, and molecular dynamics all measure or model different aspects of this level.

Molecules have conformations: distinct shapes separated by energy barriers. Butane rotates between staggered and eclipsed forms; proteins fold into metastable structures; retinal in rhodopsin isomerises after absorbing light. These states can store biochemical information in the ordinary sense that a shape changes binding, catalysis, or signalling. That is not consciousness. It is physical chemistry: stable or metastable minima on an energy surface, transitions driven by heat, photons, collisions, solvent, or enzymes.

This level is where atomic spectra become matter with function. The upper variables are bonds, angles, torsions, charge distributions, conformers, reaction coordinates, and rates. The lower variables--all individual electron and nuclear degrees of freedom--are still present, but most explanations use effective molecular coordinates.

Molecules are also the first atlas level where shape memory becomes ordinary science. A crystal defect, a protein fold, a ligand-bound receptor, or a cis-trans isomer can persist long enough to change later behaviour. That behaviour is physical chemistry, thermodynamics, and kinetics. It is not mental life. The level prepares the way for cellular biology because chemical systems can couple recognition, catalysis, storage, and motion without needing a nervous system.

The same molecule can have different relevant descriptions at different temperatures and environments. Gas-phase spectra emphasise rotations and vibrations; solution chemistry emphasises solvent, diffusion, and reaction barriers; solids emphasise lattice packing and collective modes; biochemistry emphasises binding, folding, and catalysis. Molecular science is therefore already multiscale, but its immediate substrate remains atoms and electron density arranged in space, time, and local chemical environment.

## How it was found and measured

Molecular science grew from stoichiometry, gas laws, structural chemistry, and spectroscopy. The quantum account of bonding developed after wave mechanics, with the Born-Oppenheimer approximation separating electronic and nuclear motion [@bornoppenheimer1927]. Pauling's work systematised the relation between quantum mechanics, resonance, hybridisation, electronegativity, and chemical structure [@pauling1960]. X-ray diffraction and later cryo-electron microscopy connected molecular models to measured density; the DNA double helix showed how molecular structure can carry biological information [@watson1953].

Modern measurement uses infrared, Raman, microwave, nuclear magnetic resonance, mass spectrometry, ultrafast laser spectroscopy, diffraction, scanning probes, calorimetry, and molecular simulation. Density functional theory is a central computational tool, with Kohn-Sham orbitals giving a practical route from electron density to molecular properties [@kohnsham1965]. No single method sees the whole molecule at once; structure is inferred by combining constraints, spectra, dynamics, and models.

Historically, the shift from formula to structure was decisive. A formula gives atom counts; a structure gives connectivity and geometry; a potential-energy surface gives possible motions and reactions. Modern chemistry measures all three where possible, often under controlled temperature, pressure, solvent, phase, purity, and composition. This is why molecules can be classified by composition, shape, symmetry, spectrum, and function without reducing every statement to the full electronic wavefunction.

## Objects at this level

### Diatomic molecule

A diatomic molecule such as hydrogen, oxygen, or carbon monoxide has two nuclei joined by an electronic state. Its rotations and vibrations give clean spectra, and its bond length and dissociation energy are measured quantities. The [T]-Theory reading treats it as the minimal bond-response surface `derived-under-assumptions`: a disturbance changes vibrational, rotational, or electronic state, with no claim of experience `interpretive`.

### Organic molecule

Organic molecules use carbon frameworks, functional groups, stereochemistry, and conformations to create many possible reactions and shapes. Their behaviour is governed by electron density, orbital symmetry, solvent, and thermal motion. In [T]-Theory they illustrate configurable response grammar `interpretive`: local substitution or excitation can alter the whole reaction path, while the ordinary explanation remains chemistry.

### Protein or nucleic acid

Proteins and nucleic acids are large molecules whose sequence, fold, and environment determine binding, catalysis, replication, or signalling. Their dynamics involve hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic effects, electrostatics, and solvent. The [T]-Theory reading may call a conformation "memory" only in the biochemical sense `interpretive`: shape constrains future response, but consciousness is not inferred.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The atlas reads the molecular level as a configurable response surface `interpretive`. The substrate is chemical bond and conformation; the field is electron-density response; an impulse such as a photon, collision, or ligand contact returns a propagated change through orbitals and geometry `derived-under-assumptions`.

What is carried up from atoms is quantised electromagnetic structure and state discreteness `interpretive`. What is integrated out is the full electronic many-body problem when a molecule is represented by bonds, torsions, partial charges, and conformational basins `derived-under-assumptions`. The programme may compare conformational memory with later attractor language, but that comparison is explicitly a model analogy, not evidence for molecular subjectivity `interpretive`.

This is the first level where the atlas can safely discuss function without psychology. A molecule can recognise a ligand, catalyse a reaction, store sequence, switch conformation, or absorb a photon `empirical-result`. Those are response capacities, not feelings. The [T]-Theory claim is that the same source-kernel-boundary grammar can describe how a molecular state is perturbed and relaxes `interpretive`. Any cross-scale claim that this grammar supports biological or somatic dynamics remains an open research proposal `open-hypothesis`.

The programme must also respect energy scales. A conformational change requires thermal, chemical, electrical, or photonic input of the right size `empirical-result`. The atlas may call the result a basin transition `interpretive`, but it should still name the physical driver: collision, solvent fluctuation, ligand binding, proton transfer, redox change, or photon absorption, plus the timescale, dissipation route, coupling strength, state change, and observable experimental spectroscopic output.

## Open questions and tests

Ordinary open questions include protein folding from sequence, enzyme catalysis in complex environments, excited-state dynamics, solvent effects, strong correlation, quantum coherence in some biological processes, and the limits of density functional approximations. Experiments test these with ultrafast spectroscopy, single-molecule methods, cryo-EM, mutagenesis, and high-performance simulation.

For [T]-Theory, the molecular level is a necessary constraint `open-hypothesis`. Later biological claims must pass through chemistry: a proposed cellular or somatic response should identify a molecular coupling, concentration, timescale, and energy scale where relevant. If the programme skips from molecule to mind without a mechanism or declared abstraction, it overclaims. A useful test is whether a molecular perturbation predicted to matter at a higher level can be measured, blocked, or varied `empirical-result`.

The strongest refutations would be ordinary biochemical ones. If blocking a proposed molecular pathway leaves the higher-level response unchanged, the claimed bridge weakens. If changing concentration, mutation, temperature, or light exposure changes the response in the predicted direction, the bridge gains empirical support at this level before any broader interpretation is added.
