## In ordinary science

A bird is a vertebrate body specialised for moving through air. The relevant ordinary substrate includes feathers, wings, bones, muscles, respiratory sacs, vestibular and visual systems, and the surrounding airflow. A bird is not a point particle: wing shape, body mass, feather condition, gusts, posture, and muscle power all change the forces that determine flight [@tobalske2007biomechanics; @pennycuick2008modelling].

The registry equation, $M_b\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{aero}}+\mathbf F_{\mathrm{gravity}}+\mathbf F_{\mathrm{muscle}}$, is Newton's second law for the centre-of-mass motion of the bird. In words, mass times acceleration is the sum of aerodynamic forces, gravity, and muscular forcing. Aerodynamic force includes lift, drag, thrust, and unsteady effects from flapping wings. Gravity supplies the downward load. Muscle force is internally generated but appears in the motion equation through wingbeats, posture, and control surfaces.

The characteristic scale is centimetres to metres. A small bird may flap many times per second; a larger bird may glide for long intervals and adjust wing shape more slowly. Energies are metabolic and mechanical: food powers muscle, muscle moves wings, and wings exchange momentum with air. Sensors close the loop. Vision estimates obstacles and flock-mates, vestibular organs detect rotation, and proprioception reports wing and body position. These sensorimotor loops are ordinary biology, not field mysticism [@openstax2019biology].

The bird level is placed on the side-path because it is a named solution rather than a universal scale plate. It prepares the move to flocking: the same bird that can be modelled as a body in air also becomes one active agent among many. Perching, landing, take-off, and turning add boundary conditions that a pure particle model would miss. Wind shear, obstacles, and terrain further constrain the available response. The level therefore needs variables for individual flight state--position, heading, speed, wingbeat phase, and aerodynamic forcing--before any flock equation can be written.

## The [T]-Theory reading

The programme treats the bird as a bounded organismal propagator in a bird-flock side-path `interpretive`. A gust, wingbeat, or neighbour cue is an impulse; the bird-body and air coupling produce a changed flight state `derived-under-assumptions`. The response grammar is inherited from the human organism level only at the abstract level of impulse-in, Green's-function response out `interpretive`. What is carried upward is bounded agency, position, heading, delay, and response to forcing `interpretive`. What is integrated out is the human-centred affect taxonomy and any claim about inner phenomenology `interpretive`. The named side-path is useful because it keeps ordinary avian mechanics visible while testing the zoom grammar `interpretive`. No claim is made that bird flight is a soma-field clinical model, that birds carry human-like affect modes, or that a single equation captures all avian behaviour `open-hypothesis`.

## The pictures

- **Lens off:** Show one bird in side view or three-quarter flight, with wing, body, tail, and airflow streamlines. Draw lift, drag, thrust or muscle, and gravity arrows, plus a metre-scale bar and a short wingbeat trace.
- **Lens on:** Add a transparent response kernel around the bird and air: gust or muscle impulse in, altered heading out. The overlay should show the bird as one bounded agent whose state will later couple to neighbours, not as a symbolic human.
