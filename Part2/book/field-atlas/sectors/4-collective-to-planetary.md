sector: IV
title: Collective to Planetary
levels: [society-city, regional-institutional, civilisational-solar, species-stellar, geological, planetary]
---

This sector covers the band where individual bodies have disappeared from the main display and durable collective, historical, geological, or planetary constraints take over. Its lower entries, society-city and regional-institutional, are ordinary social and geographic systems: streets, buildings, media channels, laws, archives, jurisdictions, watersheds, routes, hospitals, utilities, courts, and service networks. Their modelling vocabulary is concrete even when it is not simple mechanics. Diffusion, congestion, barriers, feedback, bottlenecks, path dependence, and memory stored in infrastructure or procedure all have ordinary uses in urban science, regional science, and institutional analysis [@batty2013new; @hagerstrand1967innovation; @north1990institutions]. `interpretive`: In the [T]-Theory reading, these levels keep the impulse-response grammar while replacing named persons with encounter rates, channels, capacities, and boundary conditions.

The first transition in this sector is therefore an ethical and mathematical change of display. A dyad is still made of persons; a city or region is not a larger person. What survives from smaller scales is not private experience but repeated response: imitation, repair, convention, schedule, law, and built affordance. What is averaged away is biography. A rumour, epidemic warning, rent shock, transport failure, or new practice moves through patterned media. `derived-under-assumptions`: The Atlas can compare that movement with a propagator only when the relevant network, boundary, and response variable are declared. `interpretive`: The aim is to make collective response legible without turning a city, region, or institution into a mind.

The middle entries, civilisational-solar and species-stellar, are deliberately hybrid. The registry preserves two ladder branches that do not reduce to one ordinary science. Civilisations and species require historical, institutional, demographic, ecological, and evolutionary variables. Solar systems, stars, and stellar neighbourhoods require gravity, radiation, plasma, oscillation, chemistry, and orbital variables [@murray1999solar; @aerts2010asteroseismology]. `interpretive`: The Atlas compares persistence under constraint, not substance. `open-hypothesis`: Any stronger cross-branch identification would need explicit variables, predictions, and tests. The sector therefore uses hybrid labels as signposts, not as claims that culture is gravity, that evolution is stellar physics, or that stars have social memory.

The hybrid entries are useful precisely because they force the boundary to be named. A civilisation can carry records, laws, energy systems, and transport standards across centuries; a solar system carries gravitational resonances and orbital histories across astronomical time. A species carries descent, selection, drift, and reproductive isolation; a stellar neighbourhood carries radiation, motion, composition, and oscillatory traces. `interpretive`: The response grammar can ask how slow variables filter perturbations, but it cannot replace the branch-specific science. `derived-under-assumptions`: The same visual ladder can show persistence, branching, and basin structure only by integrating out the variables from the previous level and adding the variables required by the new one.

The upper entries, geological and planetary, return to physical Earth and planet science. Rocks store stress, faults release it, folds preserve deformation, basins archive subsidence and sediment, and seismic waves make the response measurable [@aki2002quantitative; @turcotte2014geodynamics]. Planets add mantle convection, rotation, ocean and atmospheric circulation, radiative balance, magnetic coupling, volatile cycles, tides, impact history, and climate feedbacks [@vallis2017atmospheric; @ipcc2021ar6wg1]. `empirical-result`: These are standard geophysical and climate-system domains, with measurements from field geology, laboratory rheology, seismology, geodesy, satellites, ocean floats, magnetometers, telescopes, landers, and models. `interpretive`: The [T]-Theory overlay uses them as clean examples of memory, propagation, delay, and scale-dependent averaging.

The sector's main open question is how far one response grammar can travel without becoming empty metaphor. The answer adopted here is conservative. Ordinary science comes first at each level, and the hybrid levels carry warning labels rather than hidden identity claims. `derived-under-assumptions`: The shared Atlas grammar may organise transitions when its variables are stated, its averaging is explicit, and its evidence labels remain visible. `open-hypothesis`: It does not prove consciousness, clinical mechanism, planetary feeling, civilisational destiny, or a universal social physics. It can be useful only if it helps compare how impulses are filtered by durable constraints: street grids, jurisdictions, archives, lineages, rock fabrics, oceans, atmospheres, and magnetic boundaries.

The pictures for this sector should therefore become less anthropocentric as the scale rises. Lens off, the viewer should see maps, institutions, archives, orbital diagrams, phylogenies, stellar fields, faults, cross-sections, oceans, atmospheres, and magnetospheres. Lens on, the overlay should not add faces, intentions, or mystical unity. It should add channels, basins, delays, modes, reflections, damping, feedbacks, and integration boundaries. `interpretive`: Sector IV is the part of the Atlas where "memory" must be read most carefully: sometimes it means an archive, sometimes a lineage, sometimes a stress field, sometimes an ocean heat reservoir. The word is shared; the mechanism is not.
