# How to read this atlas

An atlas of scales is a reference work for changing size without losing the thread. A geographical atlas does not claim that a street, a river basin, and a hemisphere are the same object. It gives a disciplined way to move among maps, legends, projections, and scales. This Field Atlas does the same for wave, field, and response patterns. Each spread asks: what is the ordinary physical system at this size, what variables describe it well, what must be averaged away to move up or down, and what the [T]-Theory lens adds as an interpretation.

The ladder has thirty levels. It begins at quantum foam and the string or Planck boundary, passes through nuclear, atomic, molecular, cellular, neural, organismal, social, institutional, geological, planetary, stellar, galactic, and cosmological scales, and ends at the observable universe. The levels are arranged in five broad sectors: microphysical substrates; biological and neural networks; organismal and collective systems; geological and planetary systems; and astrophysical or cosmological systems. Alongside the main ladder is one named side-path: bird, flock, and colony or roost. That path is not an extra claim about birds as privileged beings. It is a worked example of how one organism-scale node can be followed through flight, coordination, and roosting into a collective field.

The atlas does not use global scale numbers, because a number can imply a false precision or a single privileged path. Stable level names do the work instead. Paths can order the same named levels differently when a model needs a different route.

Every level is presented as a spread. The first picture is the lens-off view: ordinary physics, chemistry, biology, social science, geophysics, or astronomy only. It should make sense to a reader who never accepts the [T]-Theory reading. The second picture is the lens-on view: the same scene with response fields, propagators, regulation layers, or higher-dimensional overlays restored where the registry allows them. Lens-on does not turn interpretation into proof. It shows the model's proposed way of reading the same scene.

The data panel is generated from the shared registry used by the Soma Machine app. It gives the level label, the characteristic length scale, the substrate, the field or operator, the governing equation, the sources, and the evidence badges. The prose in the spread explains the panel in sentences: first the ordinary science, then the [T]-Theory reading, then the picture instructions. The same explanation can be opened inside the app as the level's information panel.

Three badges keep the claims honest. FORMAL means that a named formal statement has been checked by the Lean kernel, subject to its definitions and axioms. SOURCED means that the ordinary-science claim is grounded in cited literature, published programme papers, data, or a declared model calculation. INTERPRETIVE means that the claim is a reading, analogy, co-identification, or open hypothesis. These badges are not decoration; they tell the reader what kind of trust is being requested.

All pictures in this edition come from the Soma Machine app or from original figures made for the programme. A reader can open the same view at https://www.t-theory.org/ by choosing the level, the path, and the lens state. In print, the atlas fixes a view. In the app, the same registry can be explored: lens off, lens on, or with individual layers added.

One equation recurs because it is the atlas's response grammar: $(\nabla^2 + k^2)G = \delta$. In plain words, a point-like impulse is applied to a medium, and the Green's function $G$ tells how that medium responds everywhere else. The Laplacian $\nabla^2$ measures spatial spreading; $k$ sets the characteristic wavelength or inverse length; $\delta$ marks the local poke. Different levels have different substrates and parameters, but the question is often the same: if this system is struck here, what pattern of response can travel, resonate, decay, or reorganise there?
