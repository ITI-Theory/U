# Fields, Waves, and Scale

A field assigns a value to every place where the model says a value can be asked for. Temperature in a room is a scalar field, wind above a valley is a vector field, and stress inside rock is a tensor field. A wave is a field pattern whose present shape changes in time because local rules connect neighboring places. This opening chapter treats those ideas as ordinary physics first, then uses them to read the [T]-Theory Field Atlas as a labelled comparison rather than as an assumed identity.

::: {.learning-objectives}
By the end of this chapter, students should be able to distinguish fields from particles, describe wavelength and frequency with units, explain why scale changes the useful variables, compute the spacing of a 31-level atlas, and identify which [T]-Theory claims are sourced, interpretive, or derived under assumptions.
:::

## 1.1 What a field says

Classical mechanics can begin with one particle at one position. Field theory begins with a value spread across a domain: pressure at every point in air, voltage along a membrane, or gravitational potential around a star. The field description is useful when many local parts act together and when a disturbance at one place changes what happens nearby. It also makes boundary conditions explicit. A plucked string, a canyon, a cell membrane, and a galaxy cluster all require different boundaries before equations can predict anything.

Waves add timing to the field picture. For a sinusoidal wave, speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength, $v=f\lambda$. If a sound wave travels at $343\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and has frequency $440\,\mathrm{Hz}$, its wavelength is $343/440=0.780\,\mathrm{m}$. That arithmetic is not a metaphor; it is a measurement rule. In the Atlas, wave language is only safe when the variables, units, and medium are named with the same care.

::: {.example title="Example 1.1 Spacing a 31-level wall chart"}
An A3 landscape page has a usable horizontal width of about $39\,\mathrm{cm}$ after margins. If all 31 registry levels are placed at equal marks, there are 30 intervals. The spacing is therefore $39/30=1.30\,\mathrm{cm}$ per interval. The calculation shows why labels must be short and why the HTML version can carry more detail than the print ladder.
:::

::: {.check-your-learning}
A wave on water moves at $2.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and has wavelength $0.80\,\mathrm{m}$. Its frequency is $3.0\,\mathrm{Hz}$ because $f=v/\lambda$.
:::

## 1.2 The zoom operator

A zoom operator is a bookkeeping move: it changes the scale at which a system is described and asks what must be preserved. At atomic scale, the variables may be quantum amplitudes and energy levels. At cellular scale, voltage, conductance, and diffusion become more useful. At human scale, the model may track a small number of state variables, noise, and forcing terms. At cosmological scale, density parameters and expansion rate replace the vocabulary of individual atoms.

The Field Atlas registry uses 31 named levels from quantum foam to cosmic web. Those levels are not a claim that all systems are made of one psychological substance. They are an index of modelling regimes. A responsible zoom keeps equations attached to their own domain and records where a proposed [T]-Theory connection is a class C cross-scale unification, a class D reinterpretation, or an open hypothesis from the Differences Ledger.

The app view for scale comparison can be opened with `#level=atomic&lens=on&dim=11` and then changed to `#level=cosmic-web&lens=on&dim=11`. The lens should be read as a navigation aid, not as evidence by itself.
:::

## 1.3 Baseline first, [T]-Theory second

Modern open textbooks separate observation, model, and interpretation. This edition follows the same order. First it teaches the baseline equation or phenomenon using standard units. Next it gives a worked numerical example. Only after that does it state the [T]-Theory reading with an evidence label such as `derived-under-assumptions`, `simulated`, `interpretive`, or `open-hypothesis` from the theory-status ledger.

For chapter 1, the [T]-Theory reading is modest. The zoom grammar says that many systems can be examined by asking four questions: what is the field, what is the source, what boundary shapes the response, and what observable would falsify the mapping? That grammar is `derived-under-assumptions` as a research-programme convention. Any stronger statement of physical identity would require a domain-specific proof or measurement.

::: {.key-terms}
**Field:** value assigned over a domain. **Wave:** time-changing field disturbance. **Boundary condition:** rule imposed at an edge or interface. **Zoom operator:** registry move between modelling scales. **Evidence label:** explicit statement of proof or measurement status.
:::

::: {.key-equations}
$v=f\lambda$ relates wave speed, frequency, and wavelength. The generic field question is written schematically as $L\phi=J$, where $L$ is an operator, $\phi$ is the field, and $J$ is a source.
:::

::: {.summary}
Fields describe distributed values, waves describe changing field patterns, and scale determines which variables can be measured usefully. The Atlas uses 31 levels as a registry and requires every cross-scale statement to carry an evidence label.
:::

::: {.review-questions}
1. Why does a field model need boundary conditions? 2. What changes when a model zooms from atoms to cells? 3. Which evidence label fits a conceptual comparison that has not yet been tested?
:::
