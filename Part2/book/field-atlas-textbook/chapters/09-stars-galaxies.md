# Stars and Galaxies

Astronomy turns weak light into physical history. Stars reveal temperature, composition, mass, and age through spectra and luminosity. Galaxies reveal gravity, gas, star formation, and dark matter through motion and light distribution. This chapter introduces stellar scaling and galactic fields before the final cosmology chapter treats the dark sectors.

::: {.learning-objectives}
Students should be able to connect luminosity, distance, and flux; describe hydrostatic balance in stars; explain why main-sequence scaling is steep; summarize rotation-curve evidence for unseen mass; and separate standard astronomy from [T]-Theory reinterpretation.
:::

## 9.1 Stars as balanced fields

A main-sequence star is a self-gravitating plasma in approximate hydrostatic balance. Gravity pulls inward, pressure gradients push outward, and nuclear fusion supplies energy that eventually escapes as light. The baseline equation is $dP/dr=-Gm(r)\rho(r)/r^2$. This is a local statement about pressure change with radius, not a metaphor for emotional pressure.

Stellar spectra make the model observable. Absorption lines identify elements and ionization states. Luminosity and temperature place stars on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. Massive stars burn brightly and live briefly; low-mass stars conserve fuel and live much longer. Because luminosity scales steeply with mass, a star ten times the Sun's mass can be thousands of times more luminous.

::: {.example title="Example 9.1 Solar flux at ten parsecs"}
The Sun's luminosity is $3.828\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}$. At $10\,\mathrm{pc}=3.086\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{m}$, flux is $F=L/(4\pi d^2)$. Substitution gives $F=3.20\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Astronomers convert such fluxes into magnitudes, but the inverse-square law is the core arithmetic.
:::

::: {.check-your-learning}
If distance doubles while luminosity stays fixed, flux falls by a factor of four because the same power spreads over four times the area.
:::

## 9.2 Galaxies and gravitational evidence

A galaxy is a stellar, gaseous, dusty, and dark-matter system bound by gravity. Spiral galaxies have discs, bulges, gas, and halos; elliptical galaxies have different orbital structures and gas content. Motion provides mass estimates. If visible matter alone cannot account for observed rotation speeds or lensing, the baseline inference is additional gravitating mass or a modified gravitational law.

Dark matter entered astronomy through such gravitational discrepancies, not through the [T]-Theory programme. Rotation curves, cluster dynamics, gravitational lensing, the cosmic microwave background, and large-scale structure all constrain the standard picture. Any alternative overlay must respect that evidence. A model-derived density fraction can be interesting only if it faces the same observations.

## 9.3 Atlas levels from stars to galaxies

The registry includes stellar, compact-object, stellar-cluster, galactic-disc, galactic-halo, and galaxy-cluster levels. Many local equations are class E in the Differences Ledger: ordinary gravity and stellar physics are not replaced. The Atlas contribution is a level map and a response vocabulary that compares hydrostatic balance, orbital motion, and density perturbation.

For compact objects, the caution is stronger because general relativity is essential. Black-hole ringdown, Hawking temperature, and neutron-star structure are not psychological analogies. They are precise physical subjects. A [T]-Theory statement about them should either reduce to the standard equation in the local limit or state a discriminating prediction. Otherwise it remains interpretive art-language.

::: {.key-terms}
**Luminosity:** total power emitted by an object. **Flux:** power received per unit area. **Hydrostatic balance:** pressure-gradient support against gravity. **Rotation curve:** orbital speed as a function of galactic radius. **Gravitational lensing:** bending of light by mass.
:::

::: {.key-equations}
$F=L/(4\pi d^2)$ gives inverse-square flux. Stellar balance uses $dP/dr=-Gm(r)\rho(r)/r^2$. Newtonian orbital speed satisfies $v^2/r=GM(r)/r^2$.
:::
