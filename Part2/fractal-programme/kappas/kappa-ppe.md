# Introduction: The Same Field Governs Mind, Market, and Mandate

Oxford PPE — Philosophy, Politics, and Economics — is an unusual curriculum. It was assembled in 1920 with the explicit intention that the three disciplines illuminate each other: that philosophical rigour improves political reasoning, that economic analysis sharpens political choices, that political reality disciplines philosophical abstraction, that philosophical foundations constrain economic modelling. In practice, the three disciplines have increasingly gone their separate ways. Philosophy of mind has little contact with macroeconomics. Political theory debates justice in near-total isolation from game theory. Economists build equilibrium models without engaging the philosophical foundations of rational choice. The integrative promise of PPE has been partially fulfilled in applied ethics and political economy, but the deepest integration — a single framework that governs all three domains from first principles — has not been achieved.

This book proposes that the Universal Somatic Field (USF) provides exactly that framework. The same master equation that describes the dynamics of felt experience also describes Nash equilibrium selection in strategic games, the stability of legal and political institutions, and the dynamics of market systems. This is not a claim that philosophy, politics, and economics are *reducible* to physics. It is the claim that they all instantiate the same *mathematical structure* — a structure that the USF framework makes explicit and whose implications can be derived, not merely asserted.

## Philosophy: Mind and the Field

The philosophical pillar of PPE begins with questions of mind, agency, and value. What is a preference? What makes an action rational? What is the good life, and what grounds its value? These are questions that standard economics treats as primitives — preference orderings are given, rationality is defined axiomatically, utility functions are postulated — and that philosophy finds the economic treatment unsatisfying precisely because it is circular: it defines rationality as preference-satisfaction and preferences as what rational agents have.

The USF framework grounds preferences in field dynamics: a preference is a gradient in the somatic field energy landscape. What you prefer is what your somatic field moves toward; what you disprefer is what it moves away from. Rationality, in this framework, is not an axiom but a property: a rational agent is one whose field dynamics track the gradient of a consistent energy landscape. Inconsistent preferences (the preference reversals documented by Kahneman and Tversky, the intransitivities observed in experimental economics) are not anomalies to be explained away; they are signatures of a landscape that has been modified by context — different attractors are accessible in different framings.

This gives the philosophy of mind a connection to the economics of decision-making that neither has had before. The phenomenology of choosing — the felt quality of desire, aversion, deliberation, regret — is the inside view of a field navigating its energy landscape. The normative question (what should one prefer?) is the question of what attractor landscape is worth having — a question that the framework makes well-posed even if it does not answer it.

## Politics: Governance as Field Architecture

The political pillar of PPE addresses the organisation of collective life: justice, legitimacy, authority, democracy, rights. The USF framework connects directly to the accounts developed in the companion law volume: political institutions are constraint fields that modify the somatic field energy landscape; rights are topological invariants; the rule of law is ergodicity; legitimacy is the condition under which the constraint field is accepted as a modification of the landscape rather than resisted as an external imposition.

The specifically political question that the framework illuminates is the **design problem**: given a desired collective outcome (a fair distribution, a stable democracy, a sustainable commons), what institutional architecture — what pattern of attractors, barriers, and channels in the social somatic field — will produce that outcome as a stable equilibrium? This is a field-design problem, and the USF framework provides the tools to address it: the WKB formula for minimum intervention strength, the topological stability analysis for rights, the ergodicity criterion for the rule of law.

The framework also addresses the pathological cases. Authoritarian capture — the concentration of power in the hands of a single actor who modifies the landscape in their own interest — corresponds to the non-ergodic case: the powerful actor's field is protected from accountability by a deep, artificially maintained basin, while others are exposed to the full field dynamics. The field-theoretic description makes clear why authoritarian systems are unstable in the long run: they require continuous energy input to maintain the non-ergodic structure, and they become increasingly fragile to exogenous perturbations.

## Economics: The Field as Market

The economic pillar connects directly to the companion economics volume: markets are somatic field systems, Nash equilibria are Hopfield minima, market crashes are phase transitions, and optimal regulation is a WKB problem. The distinctive contribution of the PPE synthesis is the connection between the economic and political/philosophical accounts.

Standard welfare economics evaluates market outcomes by their efficiency (Pareto optimality) and sometimes by their fairness (various social welfare functions). The USF framework adds a third criterion: **landscape robustness** — the degree to which the market attractor landscape is topologically stable against perturbations. An efficient-but-fragile market that crashes at the first shock may be worse, on landscape-robustness grounds, than a less efficient market with deep, stable attractors.

This connects economic analysis to the political design problem: the goal of economic regulation is not just to achieve efficient equilibria but to design a landscape that is topologically stable (rights-preserving), ergodic (rule-of-law satisfying), and efficient (welfare-maximising). The three criteria can conflict — there are trade-offs — but the framework provides a common vocabulary for navigating them.

## The Patient Perspective

The patient perspective paper included in this volume serves a specific function in the PPE synthesis: it grounds the abstraction in a concrete human life. The field-theoretic account of trauma, recovery, and therapeutic intervention is not merely an application of the physics; it is a reminder of what the stakes of the inquiry are. Political institutions, economic systems, and legal frameworks all act on the somatic fields of real people. When they fail — when rights are violated, when markets crash and destroy livelihoods, when legal uncertainty leaves people unable to plan their lives — the failure has a phenomenal correlate: a somatic field driven into a trauma well, a field operating non-ergodically, a landscape fragmented by uncertainty.

The patient perspective paper makes this concrete. It is deliberately placed as the final chapter before the conclusion: the theory comes first, the lived experience comes last, and the juxtaposition is intentional.

## What This Book Offers the PPE Student or Scholar

This volume is designed as the integrative text that the PPE curriculum has always needed but never had. Each chapter connects to the philosophical, political, or economic tradition it addresses; the field-theoretic framework is introduced through familiar PPE concepts rather than unfamiliar physics.

Chapter 2 (soma-field-synthesis) develops the field dynamics in terms accessible to the philosophically-trained reader. Chapter 3 (soma-game-theory, anchor paper) develops the economics and game theory. Chapter 4 (soma-law, anchor paper) develops the political and legal applications. Chapter 5 (soma-field-patient-pov) grounds the synthesis in lived experience. The final chapter addresses the integrative research agenda: what PPE questions the framework opens, and what interdisciplinary work is needed to pursue them.

Mind, market, mandate. The same equation, three vocabularies.
