# The Green Propagator

**G-ID:** *Trauma-Well Resolution — escape probability from a somatic attractor basin* (`open-hypothesis`). In this book the Green propagator is read at the scale of the person: nervous system, body, memory, social field, and therapeutic time. A propagator is not a treatment. It is the mathematical object that asks how a perturbation at one point of a field changes the field elsewhere. The clinical translation is deliberately cautious: if traumatic experience behaves like a constrained affective landscape, then a present trigger may propagate through prior coupling and memory rather than through present meaning alone (`interpretive`).

The working scale is $\sigma \approx 8$: the embodied person in relational and developmental context. Lower scales enter through autonomic physiology, interoception, electromagnetic and network models; higher scales enter through family, institution, culture, and policy. The zoom note for clinicians is simple: this volume works at the scale where testimony, symptom, bodily state, attachment context, and model variable can be held together without pretending that one reduces cleanly to the other (`open-hypothesis`).

# Introduction: A Map That Must Not Become a Protocol

This book is the safety-critical volume of the [T]-Theory domain series. The reason is not that the mathematics is more dangerous than in physics or computing. It is that the reader may be close to practice: a clinician, supervisor, researcher, service designer, trainee, or person whose work affects traumatised lives. A speculative model can be useful in such a setting only if it refuses to masquerade as instruction. The field language introduced here is therefore a research and educational vocabulary. It does not tell a therapist what to do in a session. It does not tell a client what their history means. It does not supply a new diagnosis, a new indication for intervention, or a reason to abandon established care. Its claims about clinical mechanism are marked as `open-hypothesis` unless they are merely restatements of published programme definitions or simulations.

Any phrase that makes this volume sound like a practice manual needs immediate qualification. What follows is not a clinical manual in the professional sense. It is a model manual: a guide to the terms, equations, analogies, and research questions by which Soma Field Theory (SFT) tries to describe traumatic stuckness. A clinician may find it a useful map of possible mechanisms, especially where trauma theory already speaks of regulation, window, memory, bodily activation, and dissociation. But a map of mechanisms is not a protocol, and a protocol is not an evidence base. This distinction is not a legal footnote; it is part of the scientific argument (`interpretive`).

Trauma science already has a rich vocabulary. Clinicians speak of arousal, avoidance, intrusive re-experiencing, dissociation, attachment, shame, procedural memory, defensive response, and relational repair. Developmental clinicians speak of right-brain maturation, mentalisation, disorganised attachment, and the long afterlife of early adversity [@schore2001]. Somatic traditions speak of bodily tracking, movement, orienting, titration, pendulation, and the felt sense [@levine2010; @ogden2006]. Polyvagal theory has given many practitioners a vivid language of ventral, sympathetic, and dorsal states [@porges2011], while critics have challenged its anatomical and phylogenetic claims, including the interpretation of respiratory sinus arrhythmia as a direct vagal index, and urged a more cautious reading [@grossman2007toward]. Cognitive and behavioural traditions retain the discipline of explicit formulation, exposure, skills, and outcome measurement. EMDR, trauma-focused CBT, narrative approaches, psychodynamic work, phase-based care, and body-oriented therapies already form a heterogeneous field. SFT enters this field not as a replacement but as a proposed cross-language model (`open-hypothesis`).

The proposal is that the familiar word "stuck" can be made mathematically sharper. In SFT, an affective state is represented by a finite vector $e(t)$ moving in an energy landscape,

$$
H(e) = -\tfrac12 e^\top W e - b^\top e,
$$

with noisy dynamics of the general Langevin form

$$
\gamma\dot e = -\nabla H(e) + \sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t) + J(t).
$$

Here $W$ encodes coupling among affective dimensions, $b$ encodes bias, $D/\gamma$ acts like an effective temperature, $\xi(t)$ represents fluctuation, and $J(t)$ is an external or volitional perturbation (`derived-under-assumptions`). For clinical readers, the point is not the equation itself. The point is the kind of question it makes possible. Does a person return to the same state because of meaning, memory, habit, autonomic constraint, relational expectation, physiological load, or some coupled mixture? What counts as a local skill, what counts as a landscape change, and what would longitudinal evidence look like?

The trauma-well image is the most accessible entry point. A trauma well is a deep attractor basin in a model landscape. A trigger is not simply an idea that causes distress; it is a perturbation that may push the system into a basin whose exit costs more energy than the person can recruit at that moment (`interpretive`). This is close to ordinary clinical wisdom. Many clients describe being "back there", "swallowed", "gone", or "unable to choose". The field model tries to translate those reports into a research vocabulary: basin depth, basin width, curvature, barrier height, memory kernel, coupling, and temperature (`open-hypothesis`).

The model's promise is also its risk. Once a metaphor is equipped with equations, it can sound more established than it is. A trauma well is not visible on a scan. A coupling matrix is not measured in routine assessment. Effective temperature is not a validated clinical biomarker. The proposed relationship between field temperature and arousal has analogies to window-of-tolerance language, but it is not identical to that clinical construct and has not been calibrated against it. The field model is therefore best read as a set of operational hypotheses: if the variables can be measured, if the dynamics improve prediction, if the predictions survive disconfirmation, then the model may earn a place in clinical science (`open-hypothesis`).

The same caution applies to the author's testimony. Johnson's lived experience is central to the programme's context of discovery. It explains why certain questions were asked: why sub-perceptual emotion mattered, why existing maps felt insufficient, why alexithymia and bodily signal could not be dismissed as side issues, and why the N=1 case was written at all. But testimony is not a treatment effect, not a normative trajectory, and not an epidemiological claim. The author's case can supply phenomenological constraints and generate hypotheses; it cannot establish the prevalence, cause, prognosis, or best intervention for any diagnostic group (`interpretive`).

For trauma researchers, the book's value lies in whether the model can connect currently separate lines of work. One line studies autonomic regulation and physiological flexibility. Another studies memory updating and reconsolidation. Another studies developmental trauma and C-PTSD. Another studies body-based approaches and interoception. Another studies computational psychiatry and attractor dynamics. SFT says these may be different projections of a common landscape formalism (`open-hypothesis`). That is a strong claim, but it is not an instruction to collapse the differences. The right question is: which parts of the landscape formalism survive contact with each literature?

Take polyvagal theory first. Its clinical influence is immense: safety, co-regulation, mobilisation, shutdown, and social engagement have become common language. Yet the scientific debate is real. Some aspects of Porges's account are empirically and anatomically contested; respiratory sinus arrhythmia, vagal tone, and social behaviour are not interchangeable terms; phylogenetic narratives require careful treatment. SFT should therefore not borrow polyvagal language as if it were settled mechanism. It can instead use the language as a clinically familiar phase-map: regulated engagement, mobilisation, and immobilisation are candidate attractor families, while the precise autonomic interpretation remains open (`interpretive`).

The window of tolerance is similarly useful and underspecified. As a clinical teaching model, it helps practitioners describe the range within which affect can be processed without flooding or collapse [@ogden2006]. As a measurement model, it needs operational criteria [@siegel2012developing]. SFT's effective temperature $T_{\text{eff}}=D/\gamma$ offers a possible formal analogue: too low and the system may be rigid; too high and it may be volatile. But the analogy must remain an analogy until studies map physiological markers, subjective report, behavioural flexibility, and outcome trajectories onto the same parameter (`open-hypothesis`).

Memory reconsolidation offers a third bridge. Contemporary reconsolidation theory holds that retrieved memories can become labile under certain conditions and then restabilise, sometimes with altered emotional force [@nader2000fear; @ecker2012unlocking]. Clinical translation remains debated: it is easy to overstate the extent to which any therapy has "erased" a memory, and difficult to specify what changed when symptoms improve. SFT's memory kernel gives a formal way to ask whether a past perturbation continues to contribute to present dynamics through a decaying or recurrent term, but it does not prove reconsolidation or prescribe how to induce it (`derived-under-assumptions`).

C-PTSD in ICD-11 is especially important for this book. ICD-11 distinguishes PTSD symptoms from the disturbances in self-organisation associated with C-PTSD: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational disturbance, with functional impairment [@worldhealthorganization2024international; @cloitre2013evidence; @maercker2013proposals]. The SFT claim that C-PTSD can be modelled as an operator modification rather than a single symptom cluster is a research proposal, not a diagnostic amendment. It may be clinically attractive because it treats dysregulation, shame, and relational expectation as coupled dynamics rather than as separate checklist items. But only careful empirical work can decide whether the operator language adds predictive value beyond existing formulation (`open-hypothesis`).

The book is organised accordingly. The first chapter sets SFT beside established trauma science and asks what kind of model it is. The second chapter develops trauma topology as a computational metaphor with formal aspirations: basins, barriers, temperature, memory kernels, and coupling. The third chapter treats developmental time, testimony, and the pre-verbal layer, with special attention to the ethical handling of N=1 evidence. The fourth chapter asks how to test the model without turning it into premature clinical advice. The reading sections introduce the embedded papers and tell clinicians how to read each one safely. The conclusion returns to the central discipline: if the field model is to matter clinically, it must become more falsifiable, not more inspirational (`interpretive`).

This discipline also changes the tone. The strongest version of this book is not the version that promises a new way to heal trauma. It is the version that gives trauma researchers a sharper way to fail. A model that can say in advance what would count against it is more useful than a model that can explain everything afterwards. If trauma wells exist as measurable attractor structures, if field temperature improves treatment-matching prediction, if somatic perturbation has a differential effect under specific coupling conditions, then those claims should be testable. If they fail, the clinical field loses little; it keeps its existing evidence-based practices. If they partially survive, SFT may become a useful research scaffold (`open-hypothesis`).

# Clinical Science Before Field Metaphysics

The clinical reader is entitled to ask why a field model is needed at all. Trauma science is not short of theories. It has fear-conditioning models, cognitive models, attachment models, dissociation models, developmental neurobiology, predictive processing, interpersonal neurobiology, psychodynamic formulations, and an expanding computational psychiatry literature. A new framework must justify its burden. SFT's answer is not that existing models are wrong. Its answer is that many of them speak about different projections of the same phenomenon without a common grammar (`interpretive`).

Consider a common presentation in complex trauma research: a person may show physiological arousal, relational mistrust, attentional narrowing, bodily numbness, shame, intrusive memory, and abrupt state shifts. A cognitive formulation may privilege appraisals and beliefs. An attachment formulation may privilege internal working models and relational expectation. A somatic formulation may privilege defensive activation and interoceptive signal. A psychiatric classification may privilege symptom clusters. These are not mutually exclusive. The difficulty is that they often lack a shared mathematical object. SFT proposes the affective field as that object (`open-hypothesis`).

The field object is intentionally abstract. It is not the autonomic nervous system, although autonomic variables may be among its observables. It is not the immune system, although inflammatory history may shape developmental trajectories. It is not the narrative self, although narrative meaning may change bias terms. It is not the therapeutic relationship, although the relationship may supply perturbations and boundary conditions. It is a model state: a way to describe how bodily, neural, affective, and relational variables move together (`derived-under-assumptions`).

This abstraction is clinically useful only if it preserves the difference between construct and measure. A construct such as field temperature may be estimated through heart-rate variability, skin conductance, respiratory pattern, movement variability, vocal prosody, startle response, self-report, or task flexibility. None of those is the field temperature. Each is a possible read-out under specified assumptions (`open-hypothesis`). Good research would compare candidate read-outs and ask which combination predicts state transitions better than existing measures.

The same distinction matters for "trauma well" language. A person is not their basin. A basin is not their diagnosis. A deep attractor is not a moral failing, a fixed identity, or a hidden essence. It is a model of recurrent state capture. The governing invariant for this volume is simple: the person is distinct from the attractor landscape they traverse. That sentence should remain at the centre of any clinical use of the framework (`interpretive`).

Clinical psychology already knows that explanation can harm when it reifies. A client told that they have a "deep trauma well" might hear inevitability. A clinician impressed by the metaphor might overinterpret ordinary relapse as topological fate. A supervisor might convert a speculative model into a treatment plan. The ethical correction is to keep the language conditional: "we can model this as", "one hypothesis is", "a possible measurement programme would ask". The language should widen inquiry, not narrow agency (`interpretive`).

Polyvagal theory offers a useful cautionary example. Its clinical metaphors can be humane: safety, connection, shutdown, mobilisation. They can reduce shame by locating responses in bodily defence rather than character. But the clinical popularity of a model can outrun anatomical consensus. SFT should learn from that history. It can borrow the clinical insight that bodily state matters while refusing to make disputed physiology bear more weight than it can carry (`interpretive`).

The window-of-tolerance concept offers a second caution. It is widely useful because it is phenomenologically recognisable. People know the difference between workable distress, flooding, and collapse. Yet its boundaries are often taught as if they were obvious, when in practice they are inferred from multiple signs and negotiated in a relationship. SFT's temperature parameter may sharpen the idea, but only if it accepts that temperature will be a latent construct, probabilistic and person-specific, not a number read directly from a monitor (`open-hypothesis`).

Memory reconsolidation offers a third caution. It is tempting to claim that therapy works by rewriting memory. Sometimes that language may capture a real process; sometimes it may oversimplify learning, extinction, meaning change, relational repair, and behavioural practice. SFT's memory kernel can represent the continuing influence of prior events, but it must not collapse all therapeutic change into one mechanism. A kernel may weaken, become context-dependent, be counterweighted by new attractors, or remain while the system learns a new route around it (`open-hypothesis`).

ICD-11 C-PTSD offers a fourth caution. The diagnosis recognises that chronic interpersonal and developmental trauma can involve self-organisation, not only fear-memory symptoms. That is a major conceptual advance. SFT's operator language may help formalise self-organisation as coupled dynamics, but it must not imply that C-PTSD is a single hidden mathematical object waiting to be measured. Diagnostic categories are administrative, clinical, and research constructs; a field model may cut across them, but it cannot abolish their purposes by assertion (`interpretive`).

The model is strongest when it supports pluralism. In real services, qualified assessment may identify needs involving safety, medical care, psychological treatment, medication, peer support, housing, legal advocacy, occupational accommodation, creative practice, or time. A mathematical model cannot decide or prioritise these needs. It can, at best, help researchers ask why some sequences of support alter state-transition probabilities and others do not. In that sense, the field model belongs to clinical science before it belongs to clinical practice (`open-hypothesis`).

A fair clinical science reading of SFT has five layers. First, there is phenomenology: testimony about what it is like to have affect present below language. Second, there is construct modelling: variables such as $e(t)$, $W$, $T_{\text{eff}}$, $K(t)$, and $J(t)$. Third, there is formal consistency: whether the equations and Lean definitions do what the prose says. Fourth, there is simulation: whether toy systems show predicted transitions. Fifth, there is empirical validation: whether human data support the constructs in a way that changes prediction (`interpretive`). Most of the programme currently lives in the first four layers. Clinical authority would require the fifth.

This layering prevents two opposite errors. The sceptical error is to dismiss the field model because it begins with testimony and analogy. Many useful models begin in phenomenology and analogy. The enthusiastic error is to treat formal elegance as clinical evidence. Mathematics can organise hypotheses; it cannot substitute for outcome data, safety monitoring, cultural competence, or patient choice. A good SFT research programme should be ambitious in modelling and conservative in claims (`interpretive`).

Clinical readers may also ask how the model relates to evidence-based practice. The answer is that it should be evaluated under the same norms: reliability of measurement, pre-registration, comparison groups, effect sizes, adverse-event monitoring, replicability, mechanism specificity, and incremental validity. A field-temperature measure that does not predict outcome beyond baseline severity, therapeutic alliance, and established physiological markers would have limited value. A trauma-well metric that cannot be rated reliably would fail before intervention testing. A somatic-injection hypothesis that cannot specify for whom and when it predicts change would remain metaphor (`open-hypothesis`).

The research challenge is therefore not to prove that SFT is true in the large. It is to make small claims fragile enough to test. Does a candidate temperature metric predict session rupture, dissociation, or dropout? Does a state-space model fitted to repeated measures show attractor deepening after triggers? Does successful therapy correspond to reduced return probability to a particular state, independent of expectancy? Do body-oriented perturbations differ from verbal interventions under conditions of low emotion-cognition coupling? These are answerable questions, and they are safer than slogans (`open-hypothesis`).

The discipline of labels is central. In this book, `kernel-verified` means a named Lean theorem compiles without `sorry` in the named proof surface. It does not mean the clinical interpretation is proved. `derived-under-assumptions` means a result follows from stated definitions, axioms, or model choices. `simulated` means a computational model produced the stated output. `empirical-result` means a documented measurement or experiment produced a result. `interpretive` means a conceptual mapping or explanatory reading. `open-hypothesis` means a claim with a path to discrimination but no settled evidence. These labels are not decoration. They are the book's safety system.

# Trauma Topology: Basins, Barriers, Temperature, Memory

Topology enters trauma language because ordinary linear cause-and-effect often fails to describe clinical recurrence. A small cue can produce a large state shift; a large insight can produce little change; a person may know they are safe and still feel captured; repeated practice may hold for months and then fail under specific relational conditions. Clinicians already describe these phenomena as "parts", "states", "schemas", "modes", "procedural memories", or "defence responses". SFT says one way to unify them is to model affective life as motion over a landscape (`interpretive`).

In a simple landscape, the system rolls downhill into a nearby minimum. In a trauma landscape, certain minima may be unusually deep, wide, or isolated. A state such as immobilised shame, threat mobilisation, appeasing compliance, or detached numbness may become easy to enter and difficult to leave. In SFT, this is an attractor basin. The word "basin" matters because it shifts explanation from blame to dynamics. A person does not choose the gradient; the system follows it unless enough perturbation, noise, support, or structural change alters the trajectory (`derived-under-assumptions`).

Barrier height is the next idea. If a basin is separated from another by a high barrier, ordinary small interventions may not move the system across. This resembles a clinical observation: reassurance may not reach panic, insight may not reach procedural terror, and cognitive reframing may not reach an early bodily state. But the resemblance is not proof. Barrier height must be operationalised. It might be estimated from transition probabilities among repeated state ratings, physiological flexibility under challenge, or the amount of perturbation required to shift state in a laboratory task (`open-hypothesis`).

Effective temperature is the model's term for the degree of exploratory variability. In statistical mechanics, temperature is not "warmth" in the everyday sense; it controls the probability of leaving a local minimum. In the SFT clinical analogy, low temperature corresponds to rigidity, narrowing, or freeze-like capture; high temperature corresponds to volatility, flooding, or unstable switching. The analogy is useful because it treats both hypoarousal and hyperarousal as problems of navigability rather than as opposite moral states (`interpretive`).

The God-Knob is the programme's vivid name for a parameter that changes this effective temperature. In this book the term should be treated as laboratory slang, not clinical instruction. It does not mean that a therapist has a knob to turn in another person's nervous system. It means that any intervention, context, or bodily state that changes variability and transition probability may be modelled as changing a temperature-like parameter (`open-hypothesis`). Stabilisation, movement, sleep, medication, social contact, breath, threat, pain, music, and fatigue might all affect such a parameter in different directions.

Somatic injection is the term for perturbing the field through bodily channels: movement, posture, sensation, breath, proprioception, rhythm, load, orienting, or touch where ethically and professionally appropriate. The model's hypothesis is that bodily perturbations may sometimes reach state variables that language reaches only indirectly (`open-hypothesis`). This is compatible with body-oriented therapy traditions [@levine2010; @ogden2006], but it is not evidence that any specific technique is effective for any specific person. It is a mechanism hypothesis that demands comparative testing.

Memory kernels introduce time. Trauma is not only a stored content; it may be a patterned influence of the past on the present. A kernel $K(t-t')$ describes how a past perturbation at time $t'$ contributes to current state at time $t$. In the programme's notation, a response can be written as

$$
\phi(t)=\int K(t-t')J(t')\,dt'.
$$

For clinical readers, the equation says that present response may depend on a weighted history of prior events, not only on present appraisal (`derived-under-assumptions`). Different kernels imply different clinical pictures: rapid decay, long tail, recurrent reactivation, context-specific amplification.

C-PTSD fits naturally into this language because it involves persistent dysregulation, self-organisation disturbance, and relational expectation across time. The field model represents such persistence with altered coupling and memory terms (`open-hypothesis`). This is not a claim that ICD-11 C-PTSD "is" a kernel. It is a claim that kernels may help model why certain histories continue to organise present state even when explicit memory, belief, and current safety have changed.

Asymmetry also matters. Classical Hopfield networks often use symmetric weight matrices, which guarantee descent to energy minima. Clinical life is rarely so tidy. A cue may push a person from shame to threat faster than from threat to repair. Relief may not retrace the path of activation. Repair after rupture may require a different route from the route into rupture. SFT uses asymmetric coupling and non-equilibrium dynamics to represent such path dependence (`derived-under-assumptions`). This is clinically plausible but empirically unsettled.

The topological claim is more ambitious. Topology studies properties preserved under continuous deformation: holes, loops, connectivity, separations. A topological trauma barrier would be a constraint that cannot be crossed by ordinary smooth change in the current coordinates. In the programme's more speculative language, some transitions require a perturbation of a different type, not just more of the same effort (`open-hypothesis`). Clinically, this may correspond to the experience that repeated insight does not alter a bodily pattern until a relational, somatic, pharmacological, or contextual condition changes. But the topological interpretation is not established.

The same caution applies to the programme's THERAPY-2 claim. In the formal source it is an axiom about non-zero winding number and smooth intervention, not an empirical theorem about psychotherapy. It must therefore be read as a modelling postulate: if a trauma state has the specified topological charge, then smooth operators cannot remove it under the stated definitions (`derived-under-assumptions`). That does not establish that any actual client has such a winding number, that any intervention is topological, or that EMDR, medication-assisted therapy, somatic work, or any other modality should be chosen on that basis.

The field model can also clarify why "relapse" is often a misleading word. If a person returns to a trauma basin under high load, that may reflect landscape geometry rather than failure. Skills may reduce dwell time, widen alternative basins, or increase transition probability without eliminating the old basin. A later return does not prove that therapy failed; nor does it prove that the model is correct. It suggests that longitudinal state dynamics are a better research target than binary recovered/not-recovered language (`interpretive`).

This is where established clinical concepts remain indispensable. Phase-based trauma treatment emphasises stabilisation before intensive processing. Window-of-tolerance work emphasises pacing. Somatic approaches emphasise titration and pendulation. Attachment-informed work emphasises relational safety. Cognitive approaches emphasise meaning and appraisal. SFT does not replace these. It asks whether each can be understood as operating on landscape parameters: temperature, barrier height, bias, coupling, memory, and boundary conditions (`open-hypothesis`).

The model should be allowed to fail at specific points. If physiological variability does not relate to transition probability, the temperature analogy weakens. If repeated measures show no attractor structure, the basin language weakens. If somatic perturbations do not outperform matched verbal perturbations in low-coupling conditions, the somatic-injection hypothesis weakens. If C-PTSD symptom patterns are better predicted by simpler constructs, the operator model weakens. This is not a problem; it is the point of the model (`interpretive`).

Researchers might begin with ecological momentary assessment. Participants could provide repeated brief reports of state, bodily sensation, context, and trigger exposure, paired with wearable physiological data. State-space models could then estimate whether some states function as attractors and whether transitions follow predicted gradients. Such studies would not require changing therapy. They would test whether the language of landscape adds predictive structure to naturalistic trauma dynamics (`open-hypothesis`).

Another route is laboratory perturbation. Carefully screened participants could engage in ethically mild tasks involving orienting, paced breathing, imagery, movement, or cognitive reappraisal while physiological and subjective responses are tracked. The goal would not be to provoke traumatic material but to estimate state flexibility and hysteresis. Does the route into arousal differ from the route out? Does bodily perturbation alter transition probability differently from verbal perturbation? Can models predict individual differences? (`open-hypothesis`)

A third route is secondary analysis of existing trials. If data include repeated symptom, physiological, or session-level measures, researchers could fit landscape models retrospectively and ask whether estimated parameters predict response, dropout, or adverse events. This is less clean than pre-registration but safer and faster. It could show whether SFT variables have incremental validity over baseline severity, alliance, treatment type, and comorbidity (`open-hypothesis`).

Throughout, the model must respect the client's meaning. Topological language can sound impersonal, as if the person were a particle in a well. Trauma work requires the opposite: recognition, dignity, consent, culture, narrative, and agency. The field model is acceptable only if it reduces shame and improves understanding without erasing subjectivity. A basin is a model of constraint; it is not a replacement for the person's account (`interpretive`).

# Developmental Time, Testimony, and the Pre-Verbal Layer

Trauma theory has always struggled with time. Some memories are explicit and narratable. Some are procedural, bodily, or affective. Some early events occur before autobiographical memory and yet appear to shape later regulation, attachment, movement, sensation, or expectation. Clinicians often meet the adult form of these patterns without access to the developmental data that would make causal claims secure. The field model's pre-verbal manifold is an attempt to describe this temporal problem without pretending that the early past is directly readable (`open-hypothesis`).

The pre-verbal manifold is proposed as a developmental coupling structure laid down before language becomes the dominant organiser of experience. It is not a literal anatomical layer. It is a model object representing the fact that early bodily and relational events may shape later state space before the child can encode them narratively (`derived-under-assumptions`). Such a model resonates with developmental trauma literature, right-brain affect-regulation accounts [@schore2001], attachment theory, interoception research, and the clinical observation that some material resists verbal formulation.

The risk is retrospective certainty. Adult suffering can make early events look causally obvious. A dramatic infancy event, family adversity, speech delay, later neurodivergent diagnosis, and adult crisis can be woven into a compelling story. But a compelling story is not a cohort result. The pre-verbal manifold paper is explicit that the case is N=1. This book preserves that boundary. Johnson's case is testimony and theory-generation material, not evidence that a given early insult causes autism, ADHD, C-PTSD, or any later trajectory (`interpretive`).

This boundary is especially important for neurodevelopment. Autism and ADHD are not trauma diagnoses. Developmental adversity can shape presentation, support needs, co-occurring anxiety or trauma symptoms, and diagnostic timing; it does not license casual claims that autism is acquired in general. The pre-verbal paper explores a high-stakes edge case in which early physical trauma, developmental timing, family context, and later diagnoses are read through one manifold. That reading is `open-hypothesis`, and it requires cohort design, careful differential diagnosis, genetic moderation, and independent clinical governance before any general claim could be made.

The clinical psychology book therefore differs from the psychiatry/neurodivergence volume. Here the focus is trauma modelling, not a general account of autism or ADHD. Neurodivergence appears because the author's testimony includes it and because field dynamics may be modified by sensory, interoceptive, attentional, and social-processing differences. The ethical rule is that neurodivergent profiles are structural realities, not motivational failures; but structural reality does not mean a new unvalidated biomarker or a cause story imposed from outside (`interpretive`).

Testimony has a legitimate role. First-person and patient-researcher accounts can reveal phenomena that standard instruments miss: sub-perceptual affect, mismatch between bodily load and named emotion, distrust of purely cognitive language, and the experience of being overexplained by clinicians. Such testimony is not merely anecdote if it functions as a source of hypotheses and constraints. It becomes unsafe only when it is treated as independent validation of those hypotheses (`interpretive`).

The SFT patient-perspective paper is therefore best read as a phenomenological and modelling document. It says: existing maps did not capture this lived structure; here is a formal language that might. The correct response is neither dismissal nor adoption. It is translation into research questions. Can alexithymic reports be modelled as high thresholds between field activity and conscious labelling? Can interoceptive differences alter projection from bodily field to narrative report? Can co-occurring C-PTSD alter memory kernels? These are testable only with appropriate designs (`open-hypothesis`).

The AI-generated self-case analysis requires even stronger caveating. It is a generated interpretation of Johnson's biography through Johnson's model. It may be rhetorically illuminating, but it is not an independent clinical assessment, not a therapist's formulation, and not external corroboration. It belongs in the archive as a demonstration of explanatory style and as a warning about how easily a model can sound precise when applied to a life (`interpretive`). This book treats it as commentary, not evidence.

Developmental timing also intersects with C-PTSD. ICD-11 C-PTSD recognises disturbances in self-organisation; developmental trauma researchers have long argued that chronic early adversity may shape affect regulation, self-concept, and relationships beyond fear conditioning. The field model's contribution is to ask whether those disturbances can be represented as coupled landscape changes rather than independent symptom clusters (`open-hypothesis`). That representation could support better measurement, but only if it improves prediction and does not erase social context.

Social context matters because trauma is not only inside bodies. Poverty, racism, disability discrimination, medical dismissal, educational exclusion, migration, family systems, and institutional power can all shape the landscape. A field model that focuses only on internal dynamics would be ethically incomplete. Boundary conditions in SFT must include social conditions: safety, recognition, resources, legal status, housing, relational support, and stigma (`interpretive`). Clinical psychology cannot reduce those to parameters without losing their moral force.

The pre-verbal manifold also raises a question about proof. Lean can define a manifold-like object, prove facts about predicates, or check arithmetic relationships. It cannot prove what happened in infancy, what a child's body encoded, or what an adult's symptoms mean. Formalisation may discipline the model by exposing assumptions; it does not supply developmental evidence (`kernel-verified` for formal statements only). This is one of the most important lessons for trauma researchers reading a mathematically ambitious programme.

What would good developmental evidence look like? One path is prospective cohort work following infants with severe early medical trauma, immobilisation, pain, or separation, alongside matched controls, with later measures of speech, sensory processing, attachment, interoception, autonomic regulation, neurodevelopmental profile, and trauma symptoms. Another path is retrospective but independently documented case series, using medical records, developmental records, school records, and blinded clinical assessment. A third path is mechanistic animal or computational modelling. All would require ethical oversight and careful avoidance of deterministic language (`open-hypothesis`).

The author's testimony can help design these studies. It can indicate what variables standard designs might miss: bodily asymmetry, pain imprint, speech delay, medical immobilisation, racialisation, school policy, maternal availability, and later creative reorganisation. It can remind researchers that adult narratives are often assembled from fragments, records, and bodily recognitions. But it cannot decide causal order alone. That humility is not a weakness; it is what allows testimony to become science rather than mythology (`interpretive`).

Clinicians reading this material should also resist a common temptation: to search for the single origin. Trauma work often benefits from understanding developmental roots, but it can also become trapped in origin stories. The field model suggests a different question: what current landscape is maintained by which couplings, memories, and boundary conditions? Early causes matter, but present navigability matters too. Even if an original $W_0$ never existed or cannot be recovered, new basins and routes may still be constructed (`interpretive`).

That last claim must be phrased with care. It is not a promise of recovery, nor a claim about any particular therapy. It is a model stance: the relevant object is not only the past configuration but the current transition structure. In research terms, we ask whether interventions change probabilities of state capture, dwell time, physiological flexibility, and relational behaviour over time. In clinical-human terms, we preserve hope without converting it into guarantee (`open-hypothesis`).

# From Model to Measurement Without Treatment Advice

A clinically responsible field model must pass through measurement before it passes into practice. This chapter therefore asks what one would have to measure to make SFT useful in trauma research. It deliberately avoids recommending interventions. The question is not "how should a therapist use the God-Knob?" The question is "what observable pattern would support or weaken the hypothesis that a temperature-like parameter governs state flexibility?" (`open-hypothesis`).

The first measurement domain is state. Trauma research already uses self-report scales, clinician ratings, behavioural tasks, and physiological measures. SFT would need repeated state measures fine-grained enough to detect transitions. A single pre/post symptom score cannot reveal a landscape. Researchers would need time series: momentary affect, bodily sensation, context, perceived safety, dissociation, intrusive material, avoidance, interpersonal rupture and repair, sleep, pain, and medication changes. The burden is high because attractor claims are dynamic claims (`open-hypothesis`).

The second domain is physiology. Candidate proxies include heart-rate variability, respiratory sinus arrhythmia, electrodermal activity, heart rate, movement variability, posture, facial expression, vocal prosody, startle, sleep, and perhaps EEG/MEG measures in laboratory studies. Each has limitations. HRV is affected by age, fitness, medication, respiration, illness, and measurement conditions. Skin conductance is nonspecific. Wearables are noisy. None should be treated as a direct field meter (`interpretive`).

The third domain is meaning and relationship. SFT cannot ignore narrative, alliance, rupture, repair, culture, or social power. These may enter the model as boundary conditions or perturbations, but they must still be measured with instruments and qualitative methods appropriate to their nature. A purely physiological field model would be a category error in psychotherapy research (`interpretive`).

The fourth domain is safety. Trauma studies risk destabilisation, shame, and overinterpretation. If SFT-inspired research asks participants to track bodily states, researchers must consider whether tracking increases rumination, dissociation, or health anxiety for some groups. Adverse-event monitoring, opt-out procedures, grounding resources, and clinical governance are not optional. The model's ambition makes safety more important, not less (`interpretive`).

A minimal observational study might recruit adults with trauma histories and collect two weeks of ecological momentary assessment plus wearable physiology. It would not change treatment. It would ask whether state transitions show attractor-like recurrence and whether candidate temperature metrics predict transition frequency, dwell time, or subjective window. Such a study could fail cleanly. If no attractor structure appears, SFT weakens. If simpler models predict as well, SFT adds little (`open-hypothesis`).

A second study might examine psychotherapy process data without altering care. Repeated session measures could estimate whether symptom improvement corresponds to shallower basins, reduced dwell time in threat states, increased access to regulated states, or changed coupling between bodily and narrative report. This would still not prove mechanism, but it would test whether the landscape vocabulary tracks clinical change (`open-hypothesis`).

A third study could compare perturbation classes in a controlled, non-treatment setting: verbal reappraisal, orienting, movement, paced breathing, music, imagery, or neutral control tasks. The hypothesis would be differential state-transition effects, not therapeutic benefit. Participants would be screened; tasks would avoid traumatic exposure; outcomes would be immediate and reversible. Such work could test somatic-injection claims ethically before any clinical translation (`open-hypothesis`).

A fourth study could focus on C-PTSD. It might test whether disturbances in self-organisation correspond to stronger coupling among shame, relational threat, bodily collapse, and negative self-appraisal than in PTSD without DSO. It might use network analysis, dynamic systems modelling, and physiological measures. If SFT's operator language predicts subgroup differences beyond ICD-11 categories, it gains value. If not, the operator language remains poetic (`open-hypothesis`).

A fifth study could examine memory reconsolidation claims. The model might predict that a destabilised memory trace plus mismatch experience changes kernel amplitude or decay. But reconsolidation is difficult to demonstrate clinically, and the study would need conservative wording. The outcome could be reduced reactivation under controlled cue exposure, changed physiological response, or altered expectancy, not a claim that memory was erased (`open-hypothesis`).

The Lean proof surface has a role in this measurement programme, but it is limited. It can ensure that formal claims are internally consistent, distinguish axioms from theorems, and prevent some category mistakes. For example, `FieldAxioms.lean` is an axiom registry, not a theorem file proving therapy. QUANT-EXP-1 is an exact 8-qubit statevector simulation, not hardware, not clinical therapy, and not evidence that humans tunnel out of trauma wells (`simulated`). These boundaries should appear in every clinical research proposal using the programme.

The experimental-validation paper is useful here because it demonstrates the programme's preferred style: define a benchmark, run code, connect it to theorem surfaces, and say what has not been established. Its rhetoric sometimes exceeds what clinical readers should adopt, but its structure is right. Trauma research needs the same structure: benchmark tasks, pre-specified predictions, negative controls, and a statement of what would count as failure (`interpretive`).

Clinical training is a separate matter. It may be tempting to teach SFT immediately as a new formulation tool. This book advises restraint. Trainees can learn the model as philosophy of science, computational metaphor, and research literacy. They should not be taught to infer a client's basin depth, field temperature, or coupling matrix in a way that affects care unless those constructs have been validated and embedded in professional standards (`interpretive`).

The model may still have educational value before validation. It can help clinicians see why clients should not be blamed for state capture. It can help researchers formulate dynamic questions. It can help interdisciplinary teams communicate about body, memory, and meaning. It can help patients recognise that a model is a model and not an identity. These are educational uses, not treatment claims (`interpretive`).

The strongest ethical version of SFT is therefore modest in application and demanding in evidence. It says: here is a way to organise hypotheses about trauma dynamics. Here are labels showing what is formal, simulated, interpretive, or open. Here are studies that could support or disconfirm parts of the model. Here is the testimony that motivated the search. Here are the limits. Anything beyond that belongs to future research, not current clinical instruction (`interpretive`).

A useful test of readiness is the language a project uses when it writes its consent form. If the form says that SFT will identify the participant's trauma topology, the project is not ready. If it says that the study will test whether repeated state and physiology data can be modelled using attractor terms, and that the model may be wrong, the project has a defensible starting point. Consent language is a scientific instrument because it exposes whether the investigators know the status of their claims (`interpretive`).

Another readiness test is whether the model can accommodate cultural variation. The bodily and narrative expression of distress is culturally mediated. A field model that treats one set of Western therapy categories as universal would reproduce the very reduction it tries to avoid. Cross-cultural work would need local idioms of distress, language, family structure, religion, migration history, and medical trust. The formal state space may be abstract; its observables are always situated (`open-hypothesis`).

A third test is whether the model can speak with service users rather than about them. Patient and survivor researchers should be involved in study design, interpretation, and dissemination. The author's testimony is not enough; indeed, reliance on one testimony would undermine the programme's own ambition. A plural testimony base would help identify which constructs are useful, alienating, shaming, or missing (`interpretive`).

The fourth test is parsimony. Dynamic systems models can fit almost anything if they are flexible enough. SFT must show not only that it can describe trauma trajectories but that it predicts something simpler models miss. The right comparison is not against no model but against existing clinical predictors, network models, and computational psychiatry approaches. Incremental validity is the standard (`open-hypothesis`).

The fifth test is failure publication. If an SFT-inspired measure fails, that failure should be written up. If the model works only for a small subgroup, the subgroup should be named and the boundary respected. If somatic perturbation does not differ from verbal perturbation when expectation is matched, that result matters. A programme that cannot publish negative results cannot safely approach practice (`interpretive`).

# Established Trauma Science Through a Field Lens

The field model is most credible when it clarifies rather than replaces established trauma science. This chapter therefore revisits four established areas: autonomic state, tolerance window, memory change, and complex trauma. Each is already a large literature. SFT's role is to ask whether a shared dynamical vocabulary can connect them without erasing their differences (`interpretive`).

## Autonomic state and the polyvagal debate

Polyvagal theory has been clinically influential because it gives practitioners and clients an accessible state map: social engagement, mobilisation, and shutdown. Many clinicians have found that map humane, especially when it reframes defensive responses as adaptive rather than wilful. SFT can use that clinical map as a description of candidate attractor families. Regulated engagement resembles a broad, flexible basin; mobilisation resembles a high-energy threat basin; immobilisation resembles a low-mobility basin with high exit cost (`interpretive`).

The debate concerns mechanism. Porges's specific claims about vagal pathways, phylogeny, and social behaviour are not all settled, and some are contested. A field model should not settle the debate by borrowing the language. Instead, it can separate three levels: the phenomenological state family, the autonomic measurements, and the mathematical attractor description. Those levels may correlate without being identical. A person can describe shutdown; a researcher can measure HRV and respiration; a model can fit a basin. None alone proves the others (`open-hypothesis`).

This separation is clinically protective. It lets practitioners use accessible state language without pretending that a single physiological variable explains the whole person. It also lets researchers test whether autonomic measures improve the field model. If regulated states do not correspond to greater flexibility or safer transition probabilities, the model must change. If vagal measures add little beyond self-report and context, the model must say so. The point is not to defend polyvagal theory; it is to make state language measurable and revisable (`interpretive`).

## Window of tolerance and effective temperature

The window of tolerance is a teaching model for the range in which affect can be experienced without overwhelming or shutting down. In SFT, effective temperature is a candidate formal cousin. Low effective temperature can mean rigidity, fixedness, or freeze-like capture. High effective temperature can mean volatility, rapid switching, or flooding. Mid-range temperature may support exploration and integration (`open-hypothesis`).

The analogy is attractive because it gives a single mathematical dimension to two clinical problems: too little movement and too much movement. But clinical windows are not one-dimensional. A person may tolerate anger but not tenderness, bodily sensation but not eye contact, cognitive discussion but not silence. Tolerance depends on relationship, culture, context, sleep, medication, pain, and meaning. Therefore SFT should treat temperature as one parameter among many, not as the master variable (`interpretive`).

A research version of window work would ask whether different emotional domains have different thresholds and whether those thresholds shift with context. It might model a person's state space as a landscape with local temperatures or anisotropic mobility: easy movement in one direction, difficult movement in another. That would be more complex than popular window diagrams, but it may better fit clinical reality (`open-hypothesis`).

## Memory reconsolidation, extinction, and kernel change

Memory reconsolidation is often invoked in psychotherapy discussions, sometimes too quickly. A retrieved memory can become labile under certain conditions in laboratory paradigms, but clinical change may involve reconsolidation, extinction, new learning, relational experience, narrative integration, behavioural exposure, or simple reduction in avoidance. SFT's kernel language can help by refusing to treat "memory" as a single object. A past event may contribute to present dynamics through several channels: sensory cueing, bodily expectation, relational prediction, shame, narrative meaning, and defensive readiness (`interpretive`).

A memory kernel can represent the time profile of one such contribution. Does the response decay quickly after a cue, persist for hours, reappear in sleep, or recur around anniversaries? Does therapy change the amplitude of the response, the decay time, or the cue classes that activate it? These questions are compatible with reconsolidation research but do not depend on claiming reconsolidation in every case (`open-hypothesis`).

The field model also helps avoid the language of erasure. Trauma recovery rarely means the past has vanished. A kernel may weaken; competing attractors may strengthen; cues may lose generalisation; the person may gain routes out; shame may be recoded; social safety may change boundary conditions. These are different mechanisms. SFT should preserve those distinctions rather than compress them into a single claim that therapy rewrites memory (`interpretive`).

## EMDR and evidence without mechanism inflation

EMDR belongs in this volume because it is an established trauma-focused psychological treatment in major guidelines for PTSD, including NICE guidance for adults, while the evidence base for complex presentations still requires careful adaptation and further comparative work [@nationalinstitute2018post; @worldhealthorganization2013guidelines; @shapiro2018eye]. A fair SFT reading is therefore double: EMDR is not fringe, and EMDR is not evidence for the SFT topology. Its efficacy claims stand or fall in the psychotherapy-outcomes literature; its mechanism remains debated. The field model may ask whether bilateral stimulation, exposure, expectancy, working-memory load, therapist factors, or reconsolidation-like processes alter state-transition probabilities, but it must not treat any of these as settled explanation (`interpretive`).

## ICD-11 C-PTSD and disturbances in self-organisation

ICD-11 C-PTSD matters because it names something broader than fear-memory PTSD: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational disturbance. Those domains are naturally coupled. Shame alters relationship; relationship alters arousal; arousal alters meaning; meaning alters bodily state. SFT can model such coupling with $W$, asymmetry, and memory kernels (`derived-under-assumptions`).

The operator language is useful only if it clarifies this coupling. A C-PTSD operator should not be a new label pasted over an existing diagnosis. It should specify predictions: perhaps stronger coupling between shame and relational threat, slower return from rupture, wider trigger generalisation, or greater mismatch between cognitive safety and bodily safety. Each prediction could be tested against PTSD without DSO, depression, anxiety, and non-clinical stress groups (`open-hypothesis`).

This approach may also help with heterogeneity. Two people can meet C-PTSD criteria for different reasons. One may be dominated by hyperarousal and relational threat; another by collapse and negative self-concept; another by dissociation and bodily disconnection. A field model could, in principle, describe subtypes dynamically rather than only categorically. But that is a future empirical task, not a current clinical classification (`open-hypothesis`).

## Phase-based care and landscape sequencing

Phase-based trauma care often distinguishes stabilisation, processing, and integration. SFT can read this as landscape sequencing: first increase navigability and safety, then approach high-barrier material, then consolidate alternative basins and routes (`interpretive`). That reading is useful because it explains why sequence matters. A perturbation that might be helpful in a stable region may be destabilising near a steep basin. A memory cue that can be integrated under one set of boundary conditions may flood under another.

Again, the model must not become instruction. Phase-based care has its own evidence debates, and some treatments do not follow a simple staged pattern. The field reading should generate research questions: do state-space parameters predict who benefits from which sequence? Do some people need more work on temperature-like flexibility before exposure? Do others improve when meaning changes first? The model should support adaptive empirical design, not one universal order (`open-hypothesis`).

## Dissociation and access thresholds

Dissociation is a broad term covering depersonalisation, derealisation, amnesia, compartmentalisation, absorption, shutdown, and more. SFT's threshold language may help with one slice of this territory: activity may occur in bodily or neural systems without becoming available as integrated report. A person may be affected without knowing what they feel. This maps to the perception-threshold claim in the Soma-Field model (`derived-under-assumptions`).

But dissociation cannot be reduced to threshold. It involves attention, memory, self-model, defensive response, social context, and sometimes severe fragmentation of experience. The field model should therefore use threshold language narrowly. It can ask whether some dissociative phenomena involve altered access from field activity to conscious labelling; it cannot explain all dissociation by amplitude alone (`open-hypothesis`).

## Therapeutic relationship as boundary condition

Most psychotherapy research recognises that relationship matters, even when theories disagree about why. SFT can describe the therapeutic relationship as a boundary condition and source of perturbations. A regulated, attuned relationship may alter transition probabilities; rupture may push the system toward old basins; repair may create new routes. This is `interpretive` and close to common clinical language.

The research challenge is to measure relationship dynamically. Alliance ratings, rupture-repair coding, synchrony measures, vocal rhythm, turn-taking, and subjective safety might enter a model. But relationship is not merely a variable. It has ethical and personal meaning. A mathematical model should not make the therapist into a regulator of the client as object. Co-regulation language must preserve reciprocity, consent, and power awareness (`interpretive`).

## Pharmacology, sleep, and the body outside therapy

A field model also reminds trauma researchers that therapy is not the only perturbation. Sleep, pain, hormones, medication, illness, exercise, nutrition, and environment can alter state dynamics. Heavy bodily load in the self-case analysis is one example, but it should not be romanticised. Exercise can stabilise, destabilise, dissociate, or injure depending on person and context. Medication can alter temperature-like parameters, memory reactivity, sleep, and access to affect. These factors belong in models that claim to describe trauma dynamics (`interpretive`).

Research that ignores these variables may misattribute change to therapy or meaning. Conversely, research that reduces change to physiology may miss relationship and narrative. The field approach is valuable if it keeps both in view: body conditions shape the landscape; meaning and relationship shape the landscape; social context shapes the landscape. The difficulty is not choosing one level but modelling their coupling responsibly (`open-hypothesis`).

## What SFT adds if it adds anything

After these caveats, the reader may ask what SFT adds. Its possible contribution is not a new therapy. It is a common dynamical grammar for phenomena that trauma science already recognises: state capture, window, bodily access, memory persistence, developmental timing, dissociation, relational safety, and heterogeneity. If that grammar improves measurement and prediction, it adds something. If it merely renames existing wisdom, it should be treated as a poetic synthesis and nothing more (`open-hypothesis`).

This is a high bar, but a fair one. Clinical psychology does not need more metaphors unless they organise evidence. SFT's metaphors are disciplined by equations and labels; now they must be disciplined by data. The established trauma sciences are not obstacles to the programme. They are the tests the programme must pass (`interpretive`).
# Research Vignettes: How the Model Can Be Used Without Advising Practice

A research model becomes safer when it is tried first on imaginary or retrospective vignettes rather than on live clinical decisions. The vignettes in this chapter are not cases and not recommendations. They are examples of how a trauma researcher might translate familiar clinical patterns into SFT variables, then immediately ask what would have to be measured. Each vignette therefore ends not with an intervention but with a testable question (`interpretive`).

## Vignette A: The rapid return after apparent progress

A client in a study reports several weeks of improved sleep, reduced intrusive memory, and more social contact. A minor workplace cue then produces a sudden collapse into shame, isolation, and bodily heaviness. A conventional formulation might speak of relapse, schema activation, threat response, or attachment rupture. The field translation would say: the system approached a healthier basin but remained within the attraction domain of a deep shame or immobilisation well; the cue supplied enough perturbation to cross the separatrix back into that basin (`open-hypothesis`).

The research question is not how the clinician should respond. It is whether repeated state data before and after the cue support the landscape interpretation. Did the person's state variables move gradually toward the prior basin before the conscious report changed? Did physiology show narrowing or rigidity? Was the return probability unusually high after similar cues? Could a simpler explanation, such as sleep loss or direct social threat, explain the event better? The field model earns value only if it predicts features that ordinary relapse language does not (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette B: Knowing safety without feeling safe

A person says, "I know I am safe, but my body does not." This sentence is common enough that it can sound ordinary, yet it is theoretically rich. It separates propositional belief from bodily state. In SFT terms, the cognitive channel may encode present safety while the somatic-neural field remains close to a threat attractor or under a memory kernel from prior danger (`interpretive`). The model's language of decoupling may help formalise the split between knowledge and felt safety.

The measurement question is whether the split can be observed. Does explicit appraisal change before physiological state? Do bodily markers remain threat-like after safety statements? Does the gap close over time, or does it widen under social evaluation? Does a model with separate cognitive and somatic components predict distress better than a single self-report measure? These questions respect the person's report without turning it into a treatment plan. They also avoid the error of saying that the body "knows" literally in a mystical sense; the claim is about coupled variables and report channels (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette C: The client who floods under helpful language

A therapist offers a formulation that is compassionate and accurate, but the client becomes more distressed. A field reading would not assume that the formulation was wrong. It might ask whether the verbal input increased field temperature beyond the workable range, widened access to traumatic material faster than regulation could contain, or activated a relational expectation that made being understood itself threatening (`interpretive`). In ordinary clinical language, this is a pacing problem; in SFT, it is a transition problem.

The research version would collect session-level markers of formulation moments, arousal, dissociation, alliance, and later outcome. It would ask whether certain classes of meaning input reliably precede state destabilisation in some participants. It would compare those effects with nonverbal, relational, or neutral inputs. The model would fail if it merely redescribed "the client became upset" without predicting when accurate language helps and when it destabilises (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette D: Numbness with high load

Another participant reports numbness, but wearable and behavioural data suggest high physiological load: poor sleep, elevated startle, shallow breathing, muscle tension, and reduced variability. The field model distinguishes conscious feeling from sub-threshold field activity. It might describe numbness as high activity below access threshold, or as decoupling between bodily signal and emotional labelling (`derived-under-assumptions`). This is a hypothesis, not a correction of the person's report. The person is not "really" feeling something they deny; the report is part of the data.

A study could test whether people with high alexithymia or dissociation scores show larger gaps between physiological perturbation and named emotion, and whether those gaps predict later state shifts. It could also test alternative explanations: medication, fatigue, chronic pain, social desirability, measurement artefact. The field model must be humble here because bodily measures are nonspecific. High load is not hidden truth; it is a signal requiring interpretation (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette E: The apparently disproportionate trigger

A small cue produces an intense response. Standard trauma theory already explains this through associative learning, appraisal, threat detection, and memory. SFT adds only if it can model why some cues have broad capture power while others do not. Basin width is the relevant term: a wide basin has many entry routes. The hypothesis would be that some trauma configurations produce broad attraction domains, so superficially diverse cues converge on the same state (`open-hypothesis`).

Testing this would require cue sampling. Researchers might collect many everyday triggers and code their sensory, relational, temporal, and semantic features. They could ask whether diverse cues lead to a small number of recurrent state clusters, and whether those clusters have physiological signatures. If the same response follows only semantically similar cues, ordinary associative models may suffice. If heterogeneous cues converge dynamically, the basin-width language may add value (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette F: Progress in one register, blockage in another

A person may become more articulate about trauma without feeling bodily change, or show bodily flexibility without being able to narrate meaning. SFT's body-neural tensor language offers one way to model this: different projections of the field may change at different rates (`derived-under-assumptions`). Clinical traditions already recognise this mismatch. The research contribution would be to measure cross-register coupling directly: do changes in language, autonomic flexibility, movement, and relational behaviour cohere or dissociate over time?

The danger is to privilege one register as the true one. A field model should not say that the body is always deeper than words or that narrative is secondary. In some cases language reorganises bodily state; in others bodily safety enables language; in others social context dominates both. The model should compare routes rather than rank them (`interpretive`).

## Vignette G: Cultural meaning as boundary condition

A person's state shifts around an anniversary, religious ritual, immigration event, racialised encounter, or family obligation. A narrow physiological model may miss why the cue matters. SFT can include cultural meaning as a boundary condition or perturbation, but only if researchers refuse to reduce culture to a scalar. The field state is embodied and social (`interpretive`).

A study might combine qualitative interviews with time-series modelling. It could ask whether culturally meaningful events predict state transitions beyond generic stress ratings. It could also ask whether the same physiological pattern has different meaning in different contexts. The field model would be strengthened if it can hold formal dynamics and thick description together; it would be weakened if it flattens culture into noise (`open-hypothesis`).

## Vignette H: The stable symptom that protects function

Some trauma patterns persist because they solve a problem. Numbing may preserve work function; mistrust may reduce exposure to harm; hypervigilance may support caregiving in unsafe environments. The energy landscape should therefore not be read as pathology alone. An attractor may be costly and protective at once (`interpretive`). This point matters ethically: researchers must not define movement out of a basin as improvement unless the person's context and values support that interpretation.

A field study would therefore need outcome measures beyond symptom reduction: functioning, agency, safety, values, cultural fit, and adverse consequences. A model that treats all movement toward a researcher-defined calm basin as good would be clinically naive. The landscape has to include environment, not only organism (`open-hypothesis`).

Taken together, these vignettes show the right use of SFT at this stage. The model can sharpen questions: What state returned? What route did it take? Which variables were coupled? What was the barrier? What was the boundary condition? What would count against the interpretation? It should not answer: what should the clinician do now? That answer belongs to established practice, professional judgement, consent, supervision, and evidence, not to an unvalidated field model (`interpretive`).
# A Clinician's Glossary of the Field Terms

This glossary is included to prevent the most common misreadings. Each term is a research construct unless the context says otherwise. None is an assessment instruction.

**Affect vector $e(t)$.** A finite representation of the person's current affective configuration. It may include dimensions such as fear, anger, grief, calm, shame, interest, or bodily activation depending on the model. It is not the person's real inner life; it is a coordinate choice (`derived-under-assumptions`).

**Coupling matrix $W$.** A model of how affective dimensions influence one another. Positive coupling means co-activation in the model; negative coupling means inhibition. In clinical language, $W$ can represent why shame amplifies withdrawal, why fear inhibits curiosity, or why calm supports social contact. In research language, $W$ must be estimated from data and may not be identifiable in simple designs (`open-hypothesis`).

**Bias $b$.** A baseline tilt in the landscape. Social context, temperament, pain, sleep, medication, developmental history, and current threat may all contribute to bias terms. The concept helps avoid treating every state as a free choice. It should not be used to imply destiny (`interpretive`).

**Attractor.** A recurrent state or state family toward which the system tends to move. Attractors can be helpful, harmful, protective, costly, or mixed. Regulated calm can be an attractor; so can shame, vigilance, collapse, or compulsive appeasement. Naming an attractor does not explain it; it marks a pattern to investigate (`open-hypothesis`).

**Trauma well.** A proposed attractor basin associated with traumatic capture. The word "well" suggests depth and exit cost. It should be used only as model language unless data demonstrate recurrent capture, transition asymmetry, and measurable return probability (`open-hypothesis`).

**Barrier.** A constraint on transition from one state to another. In clinical terms, a barrier may appear as inability to access calm, grief, agency, anger, or social trust despite explicit desire. Barriers can be cognitive, bodily, relational, social, pharmacological, or model-internal. The field model does not decide which without evidence (`interpretive`).

**Effective temperature.** A parameter for variability and exploration. Low temperature suggests rigidity or capture; high temperature suggests volatility or flooding. It is not body temperature and not a diagnosis. It is a candidate latent variable that must be calibrated against behaviour, physiology, and report (`open-hypothesis`).

**God-Knob.** Informal programme slang for a source term or parameter that changes effective temperature. In a clinical book, the term should be handled cautiously because it can imply control. It is better read as a reminder that state flexibility may be modulated by many factors, not as a tool (`interpretive`).

**Somatic injection.** A perturbation entering through bodily channels. Movement, orienting, load, rhythm, breath, posture, and sensation may be examples in research contexts. The term does not recommend any technique and does not bypass consent, scope of practice, or evidence (`open-hypothesis`).

**Memory kernel $K(t-t')$.** A function describing how past perturbations continue to affect present state. It is a formal analogue for temporal influence, not a claim that traumatic memory is literally a kernel in the body. It can help distinguish initial reactivity, persistence, recurrence, and decay (`derived-under-assumptions`).

**Pre-verbal manifold.** A proposed model object for coupling patterns shaped before language. It is a way to ask developmental questions, not a licence to infer infancy from adult symptoms. Its empirical future depends on cohort data, records, and independent assessment (`open-hypothesis`).

**Operator modification.** A change in the rules of the system rather than a mere change in a state. The programme uses this language for neurodivergence and C-PTSD. Clinically, it should mean only that some profiles may alter dynamics globally. It must not become a hidden severity ranking (`interpretive`).

**Projection.** A partial read-out of a higher-dimensional or richer state. Self-report, physiology, movement, narrative, and relationship are projections, not the whole field. The term encourages humility: disagreement among projections may be data, not error (`interpretive`).

**Hysteresis.** Path dependence: the route into a state differs from the route out. Clinically, this resembles the fact that calming after activation may require different conditions from those that prevented activation. Hysteresis can be tested in controlled state-transition studies (`open-hypothesis`).

**Co-identification.** The programme's method for saying two structures share enough formal type to import results under assumptions. In this book, co-identification should be treated as stronger than metaphor but weaker than empirical identity unless measured data support it (`derived-under-assumptions`).

**Testimony.** First-person or patient-researcher evidence that constrains and motivates modelling. Testimony can reveal phenomena that instruments miss. It does not by itself establish general clinical mechanism. Johnson's N=1 material remains testimony throughout this volume (`interpretive`).
# Falsification, Ethics, and Governance

A theory that enters trauma research must say not only what would support it but what would stop it. This chapter makes the failure conditions explicit. The point is not adversarial scepticism for its own sake. It is clinical humility. A model that cannot be wrong will eventually be used to make vulnerable people wrong. The protection against that is a falsification culture built into the model from the beginning (`interpretive`).

## When the trauma-well claim fails

The trauma-well claim fails if repeated data do not show recurrent state capture beyond what ordinary symptom severity predicts. If a person reports distress in ways that vary with context but do not cluster into stable basins, then basin language may be unnecessary. If state transitions are better predicted by immediate social threat, sleep, substance use, pain, or economic stress than by estimated landscape parameters, then the model should defer to those explanations (`open-hypothesis`).

It also fails if basin metrics cannot be estimated reliably. Two researchers fitting the same data should not produce entirely different wells unless they can explain why. Measures of basin depth, width, and curvature must have confidence intervals. They must be robust to sampling frequency and missing data. They must not become impressionistic labels disguised as mathematics (`open-hypothesis`).

## When the temperature claim fails

The temperature claim fails if candidate variability measures do not predict flexibility, volatility, or transition probability. It fails if high-temperature and low-temperature descriptions merely rename hyperarousal and hypoarousal without adding prediction. It fails if the same parameter is stretched to explain both rigidity and flooding after the fact. A temperature-like construct must earn its place by discriminating cases and moments prospectively (`open-hypothesis`).

It may also fail person by person. One person's increased physiological variability may signal adaptive flexibility; another's may signal destabilisation; another's may reflect caffeine, illness, medication, or exercise. A good model should allow individual calibration. A bad model will treat one physiological number as universally meaningful. Trauma research has learned repeatedly that context changes interpretation; SFT must not forget that lesson (`interpretive`).

## When the somatic-injection claim fails

The somatic-injection claim fails if bodily perturbations do not produce parameter-specific effects after expectation, alliance, attention, and demand characteristics are controlled. It fails if verbal, relational, musical, pharmacological, or environmental perturbations produce the same effects under the same conditions. It also fails if bodily perturbations work only through meaning, expectancy, or social permission rather than through the proposed field channel. That would not make body-based therapies useless; it would weaken this mechanism (`open-hypothesis`).

The claim must also be tested for harms. A bodily focus can help some people and destabilise others. It can increase agency or increase surveillance of symptoms. It can support grounding or trigger dissociation. A model that predicts benefit but not adverse response is incomplete. Therefore any somatic-injection research must include worsening, dropout, dissociation, shame, and loss of trust as outcomes, not merely improvement (`interpretive`).

## When the memory-kernel claim fails

The memory-kernel claim fails if past perturbations do not improve prediction after current context and meaning are modelled. It fails if the kernel can be adjusted to fit any temporal pattern. It fails if proposed decay parameters contradict known memory, learning, sleep, or developmental mechanisms. It also fails if the model ignores that people reinterpret the past: the meaning of an event changes, and that meaning can become part of the current boundary condition (`open-hypothesis`).

A strong kernel model would make narrow predictions. For example, it might predict that certain cue classes produce response curves with a long tail, or that successful therapy changes recurrence amplitude but not initial reactivity, or that anniversary reactions have a different temporal profile from relational rupture. Narrow predictions can be wrong. That is why they are useful (`open-hypothesis`).

## When the pre-verbal manifold claim fails

The pre-verbal manifold claim fails if cohort studies do not find the predicted relation between early bodily trauma, developmental timing, later projections, and adult regulation. It fails if genetic, family, social, or diagnostic factors explain the trajectory more parsimoniously. It fails if the proposed projections cannot be rated independently. It fails if the model is used retrospectively to make every adult difficulty traceable to an early event (`open-hypothesis`).

The claim also requires careful language around autism. If research does not distinguish autistic development, trauma-related adaptation, deprivation effects, sensory processing differences, and diagnostic overshadowing, the model will harm more than help. Any study in this area needs neurodivergent governance, developmental expertise, and strict avoidance of cure rhetoric. The aim is understanding support needs and trajectories, not explaining away neurodivergent identity (`interpretive`).

## Governance before translation

Before any SFT-informed clinical decision support is built, there should be governance at five levels. First, scientific governance: preregistered hypotheses, independent statistics, and open negative results. Second, clinical governance: professional oversight, adverse-event procedures, and clear separation from treatment recommendation. Third, service-user governance: people with trauma histories and neurodivergent people involved in design and interpretation. Fourth, cultural governance: attention to race, class, migration, disability, gender, and institutional power. Fifth, formal governance: a claim ledger that states which claims are axioms, simulations, interpretations, and open hypotheses (`interpretive`).

The claim ledger should be public in any research context. If a study mentions Lean, it should say which theorem and what it proves. If it mentions QUANT-EXP-1, it should say simulation. If it mentions C-PTSD operators, it should say model parameters, not biomarkers. If it mentions the author's case, it should say testimony. These statements should not be hidden in appendices; they are part of informed consent and scientific transparency (`interpretive`).

## Data ethics

Field modelling invites dense data: wearables, diaries, audio, movement, session process, physiological streams, and perhaps passive sensing. Such data are intimate. They can reveal habits, relationships, crises, sleep, substance use, and location. Trauma research already carries confidentiality obligations; field research amplifies them. Minimal data collection, local processing where possible, encryption, deletion rights, and clear limits on secondary use should be considered baseline requirements (`interpretive`).

The model also raises interpretation risks. A participant might be told, or infer, that their field is rigid, fragmented, low-temperature, or trapped. Such labels could shame or frighten. Research feedback should therefore be carefully designed. In early studies, it may be safer not to return individual field parameters at all, or to return only general educational material. If individual feedback is ever offered, it must be validated, contextual, and clinically supported (`interpretive`).

## Algorithmic humility

If SFT variables are eventually estimated by software, algorithmic humility becomes essential. A model fitted to data is not an oracle. It may be biased by sensor quality, missing data, cultural assumptions, diagnosis mix, and the research setting. It may mistake poverty, discrimination, pain, or unsafe housing for an internal attractor. It may overfit one population and fail in another. It may be most confident where it is least humane (`interpretive`).

For this reason, any computational tool should be decision-support for research first, never autonomous assessment. It should present uncertainty, alternatives, and reasons. It should allow participants and clinicians to reject interpretations. It should be audited for differential error across groups. It should record when simpler explanations fit better. A field model that cannot say "I do not know" should not enter trauma work (`interpretive`).

## What survives if the clinical mechanism fails

Even if SFT fails as a clinical mechanism, some things may survive. The insistence on evidence labels is useful. The distinction between testimony, simulation, proof, and clinical validation is useful. The idea that repeated state dynamics matter is compatible with broader computational psychiatry. The critique of blaming people for state capture is humane. The call for dynamic, embodied, and relational measurement is scientifically reasonable. A failed theory can still leave better questions behind (`interpretive`).

This is a good outcome. The purpose of a research scaffold is not to be preserved at all costs. It is to build a bridge to evidence. If the bridge cannot carry weight, it should be marked, repaired, or dismantled. Trauma research does not need another totalising theory. It needs tools that are precise enough to help and honest enough to stop (`interpretive`).
# Supervisory and Research Use

Supervisors, lecturers, and research leads may find this volume useful as a teaching text about evidence boundaries. Its safest classroom use is not to ask trainees to "apply SFT" to clients, but to ask them to classify claims. Which sentences describe established trauma science? Which are analogies? Which are formal consequences of definitions? Which are simulations? Which are live clinical hypotheses? This exercise is itself clinically valuable because it trains resistance to attractive overclaim (`interpretive`).

In supervision, the model can be discussed only at the level of reflective formulation. A supervisor might say that a recurring pattern resembles an attractor, or that a client's movement between flooding and shutdown suggests a window problem. But those are ordinary clinical reflections using a research vocabulary, not SFT assessment. They should never replace risk assessment, safeguarding, cultural formulation, differential diagnosis, or the modality's own standards (`interpretive`).

For doctoral and postdoctoral research, the model is a source of design problems. How do we estimate a landscape from sparse data? How do we distinguish basin depth from reporting habit? How do we model relational boundary conditions? How do we avoid making physiological data appear more objective than testimony? How do we compare SFT against network analysis, dynamic systems models, predictive processing, and established trauma measures? These are appropriate research questions because they preserve uncertainty (`open-hypothesis`).

For trauma services, the model is not ready to guide pathway design. It may, however, encourage services to collect more dynamic data: session-by-session change, dropout reasons, adverse responses, sleep and pain context, rupture-repair markers, and service-user accounts of bodily state. Such data would improve trauma research whether or not SFT survives. A good theory should leave useful measurement practices behind even if its own language is later abandoned (`interpretive`).

For lived-experience researchers, the book offers both recognition and warning. It recognises that insider testimony can identify gaps in professional maps. It warns that one testimony can become overcentral if a programme is not pluralised. Future work should invite multiple lived-experience accounts, including accounts that do not fit SFT well. Disagreement is not a threat to the model; it is a way to discover its boundary (`interpretive`).

For readers who are themselves clinicians with trauma histories, the same safety rule applies: the model is not a self-treatment framework. It may give language for research, writing, or discussion with appropriate supports. It should not be used to intensify self-monitoring, reinterpret all experience as topology, or replace care. The book's central ethical sentence remains: the person is distinct from the attractor landscape they traverse (`interpretive`).

The paper matters because it lets clinicians hear the inside of a modelling problem. It should not be used to infer another person's inside.

# Reading *The Soma-Field*

# Conclusion: Research Discipline for a Field-Theoretic Trauma Model

This volume has argued for a careful middle position. SFT should not be dismissed merely because it uses physics and topology in a clinical domain. Trauma already demands cross-level thinking: physiology, memory, attachment, culture, language, development, and bodily experience. A field model is one disciplined way to hold those levels together. At the same time, SFT should not be adopted as a clinical framework merely because its metaphors are powerful or its equations are elegant. The clinical threshold is higher. Human evidence, safety, and professional accountability decide whether a model can guide practice (`interpretive`).

The book's central image is the trauma well. It is useful because it explains stuckness without blame. A person captured by a state is not failing to apply insight; they may be moving through a landscape whose gradients make some returns highly probable. That image can reduce shame and support research into state dynamics. But it can also reify suffering if used carelessly. A person is not a basin. A diagnosis is not a topology. A clinical life is not exhausted by an energy function (`interpretive`).

The same ambivalence applies to the God-Knob. A temperature-like parameter is a promising way to formalise window, arousal, rigidity, volatility, and flexibility. It may help distinguish frozen states from flooded states without moralising either. Yet the name is dangerous if it suggests control. Clinicians do not turn another person's field temperature. Researchers may estimate variables that change transition probabilities under specific conditions. The difference between those sentences is the difference between science and overreach (`open-hypothesis`).

Somatic injection is similarly promising and limited. Many trauma clinicians know that bodily work can access material that talk alone does not reach [@levine2010; @ogden2006]. The field model gives a possible reason: bodily perturbations may act closer to certain state variables than language does. But this remains a mechanism hypothesis. It does not validate a technique, authorise touch, rank therapies, or explain every improvement. The ethical use is to design studies that compare perturbation classes under controlled, consented, and safe conditions (`open-hypothesis`).

The memory-kernel language may be the most clinically fertile part of the model. Trauma is time made active: a past event continues to shape present readiness, threat, shame, and bodily expectation. A kernel formalises that continuing influence without reducing it to explicit memory. It can represent decay, persistence, recurrence, and context dependence. It can also be wrong. Human memory is reconstructed, relational, embodied, and culturally mediated. A kernel is a model of one aspect of temporal influence, not an ontology of memory (`derived-under-assumptions`).

The engagement with C-PTSD must remain particularly careful. ICD-11's distinction between PTSD and C-PTSD is a clinical and research achievement because it recognises disturbances in self-organisation. SFT may help model those disturbances as coupled dynamics. It may also tempt theorists to rename existing clinical insight in mathematical language without adding predictive value. The burden is on SFT to show that operator language improves measurement, subgroup identification, mechanism testing, or outcome prediction (`open-hypothesis`).

The engagement with polyvagal theory is equally instructive. Polyvagal language has helped many clinicians talk about safety and defence, but scientific debate remains. SFT should avoid building an unvalidated model on another contested model as if both were settled. A safer route is to treat polyvagal categories as clinically familiar descriptions of state families while leaving the precise autonomic mapping open. The field model can then be tested against physiology rather than inheriting physiology by association (`interpretive`).

The pre-verbal manifold brings the book back to testimony. Johnson's N=1 case is moving, detailed, and theoretically generative. It should be treated neither as confession nor proof. It is testimony: a first-person and records-informed account that motivates formal hypotheses about early bodily trauma, developmental timing, neurodivergence, attachment, institution, crisis, and reconstruction. Trauma research needs such accounts because instruments miss things. It must also protect such accounts from being overgeneralised (`interpretive`).

The AI-generated self-case analysis sharpens this lesson. AI can make a model sound fluent. It can map biography to equations with impressive speed. That fluency is not evidence. In a clinical context, AI-generated formulation must be held at a distance unless there is human clinical accountability, consent, and validation. In this volume the generated analysis is useful because it displays the model's explanatory temptation. It is a mirror of what must be resisted (`interpretive`).

Formal proof has its own proper place. Lean can check that a theorem follows from definitions and axioms. It can expose whether a claim is a theorem, an axiom, a placeholder, or arithmetic. It cannot prove that a client is dissociated, that a therapy changed a coupling matrix, or that consciousness is a threshold crossing. The programme's proof surface is valuable precisely because it forces distinctions. Clinical readers should demand those distinctions wherever SFT prose sounds confident (`kernel-verified` only within formal scope).

Simulation also has a proper place. QUANT-EXP-1 matters because it turns reachability language into a finite computational experiment: cold classical dynamics do not reach the target basin in the reported runs, while a quantum-inspired path does (`simulated`). It does not show quantum therapy, clinical escape, or human mechanism. Its lesson is methodological: make a toy model, pre-specify a test, harden controls, report limits. Trauma research needs that discipline more than it needs the quantum imagery.

If the model is to move forward, the next step is not a branded intervention. It is a measurement programme. The field needs candidate observables, reliability studies, repeated measures, state-space modelling, preregistered predictions, negative controls, cultural adaptation, and patient involvement. It needs studies that can say no. It needs researchers who are willing to discover that a beautiful term such as trauma well predicts nothing beyond existing constructs. Only then can the terms that survive be trusted (`open-hypothesis`).

A first empirical milestone would be descriptive: show that repeated trauma-related state data exhibit attractor-like structure and that candidate temperature variables predict transitions. A second would be comparative: show that SFT parameters add predictive value beyond established symptom, alliance, and physiological measures. A third would be mechanistic: show that specific perturbation classes change specific parameters in predicted ways. A fourth would be clinical: only after the earlier steps, test whether parameter-informed decision support improves outcomes without increasing harm (`open-hypothesis`).

This sequencing matters. Clinical history is full of models that helped people make sense of suffering before they were adequately tested. Some became useful. Some became dogma. Some harmed by making patients fit the theory. SFT can avoid that path only by keeping its labels visible and its hypotheses fragile. The model should remain easier to falsify than to market (`interpretive`).

The benefit of the field vocabulary, if it survives, would be integration. It could give trauma researchers a shared grammar for bodily state, memory, affect, and relation. It could help clinicians from different traditions say more precisely what they think is changing. It could help patients understand why knowing is not always enough, why the body may return before the story does, and why repeated capture is not failure. These are educational hopes, not clinical claims (`open-hypothesis`).

The final caution is dignity. Traumatised people have often been overexplained: by families, institutions, clinicians, courts, schools, and cultures. A mathematical model can become another overexplanation if it speaks over testimony. The only acceptable field model is one that listens first. The equations are secondary. The person is primary. The terrain of therapy, if that phrase is used at all, is not an abstract manifold; it is a lived life in which science must earn permission to be helpful (`interpretive`).

The final invitation is therefore research, not adoption. Take the trauma-well metaphor and try to measure it. Take the temperature analogy and try to falsify it. Take the memory kernel and compare it with reconsolidation, extinction, and narrative change. Take the pre-verbal manifold and design a cohort that could disconfirm it. Take the author's testimony as a serious source of hypotheses and a reminder of stakes. Then let the evidence decide which parts of the field model deserve to remain (`open-hypothesis`).


A final way to state the book's position is this: SFT belongs, for now, in the seminar room, the methods appendix, the preregistration, the simulation notebook, and the reflective research meeting. It does not belong in a consent-free interpretation of a client's body. It may help clinicians and researchers notice that state change has geometry, that testimony can precede measurement, and that formal models need moral restraint. That is already a serious contribution if handled carefully. The next scientific task is to let the model encounter data that are strong enough to disappoint it (`interpretive`).

One practical consequence follows for readers of the embedded papers. When a paper says that a field mechanism explains a clinical phenomenon, translate the sentence into a research question before carrying it further. "Explains" becomes "could predict under these measurements". "Therapeutic" becomes "relevant to a future trial if safety and mechanism are established". "Formal" becomes "true of the definitions used". This translation is not hostile to the programme; it is how the programme can remain credible in the most ethically exposed domain it addresses (`interpretive`).

That discipline also protects clinicians from false novelty. Much of what SFT values is already present in good trauma work: pacing, consent, bodily attention, relational safety, humility about memory, and respect for protective adaptation. The model's contribution, if any, is to make these dynamics explicit enough to test across traditions. It should make experienced clinicians more precise, not make them forget what their field already knows (`interpretive`).

If future evidence contradicts the field vocabulary, the ethical response is not to defend the vocabulary but to keep the improved habits of measurement, labelling, and listening (`interpretive`).

This is the minimum standard for a trauma-facing theory in clinical research (`interpretive`).
