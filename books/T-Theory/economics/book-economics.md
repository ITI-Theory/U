# The Green Propagator

**G-ID:** *The Nash Attractor Resolvent -- $(H-\lambda)^{-1}$ determining market equilibrium*, with evidence label `open-hypothesis` for markets in general and `derived-under-assumptions` where the game is an exact potential game with a specified Hopfield energy. In economics the propagator should be read as a resolvent of adjustment: a way of asking how a perturbation to preferences, payoffs, information, liquidity, or regulation is transmitted through a coupled strategic system. It is not a magic equilibrium oracle. It is a compact notation for the response of an interaction landscape.

The zoom note is Scale $\sigma \approx 13$: civilisational economic landscapes, market microstructure, institutional coordination, and game-theoretic populations. The field variable is not the private inner state of any trader; it is an aggregate state vector whose components may be strategies, positions, order imbalances, beliefs, risk exposures, or institutional constraints, depending on the model. The book asks what follows if those variables are coupled by an energy-like object and driven by noisy adjustment.

# Introduction: The Equilibrium Is Not Where You Think

Economists know that equilibrium is a solution concept, not a story of arrival. A Nash equilibrium says that no player can profitably deviate unilaterally, given the others' strategies. It does not say that the equilibrium is unique, selected, stable under learning, welfare-maximising, computationally accessible, or empirically occupied. The most interesting economic questions often begin where the equilibrium definition stops: why this equilibrium rather than that one; why a market remains liquid for years and then freezes in hours; why institutions can make cooperation robust in some settings and brittle in others; why agents with ordinary cognitive limits nevertheless coordinate on conventions, prices, and expectations. This volume begins from that gap. `interpretive`

The generated framing for this book used the slogan "Nash equilibrium = Hopfield minimum". The slogan is useful only after repair. In an exact potential game, there exists a scalar potential $\Phi(s)$ such that each player's unilateral payoff change equals the corresponding change in $\Phi$. Pure Nash equilibria are precisely the strategy profiles that are local maxima of $\Phi$ with respect to unilateral deviations. If we define a Hopfield-style energy $H(s)=-\Phi(s)$, those equilibria become local minima of $H$ under the same neighbourhood relation. In differentiable potential games, the correspondence becomes a statement about gradients and boundary conditions: $\nabla_i u_i=\nabla_i\Phi$, plus KKT conditions on the strategy sets. Under those assumptions the mapping is mathematically exact. Outside them it is not. Monderer and Shapley's finite exact-potential framework is the standard reference point here. [@monderer1996potential] `derived-under-assumptions`

Hopfield networks enter because their stable memories are local minima of an energy function. Hopfield's 1982 model gave a precise dynamical picture: states update under a symmetric coupling matrix and descend an energy landscape [@hopfield1982]. Modern Hopfield networks and attention-like associative memories enlarge the class of such systems [@ramsauer2020hopfield]. When a game's incentives can be written as a compatible potential, the potential supplies the economic energy landscape. When the couplings are asymmetric, cyclic, strategic, informationally incomplete, or institutionally non-local, the Hopfield picture may still be a useful modelling analogy, but the equivalence no longer follows. That distinction is one of the main disciplines of this book. `derived-under-assumptions`

The same distinction matters in finance. Econophysics has long imported statistical-mechanics language into markets: Ising-like herding, spin glasses, heavy tails, scaling laws, critical phenomena, random matrices, and network cascades. Mantegna and Stanley's work helped name the field; Sornette's crash models made critical precursors and log-periodic signatures central to one research stream. [@mantegna1999introduction; @sornette2003why] These traditions are not settled finance theory. They are families of models with mixed empirical status. [T]-Theory enters them as an additional discipline: label which claims are exact mathematical transfers, which are fitted analogies, which are simulations, and which remain hypotheses. `interpretive`

The Green's-function language is valuable because economic disturbances propagate. A central-bank announcement, a clearing-house margin change, a defaults cascade, a sudden liquidity withdrawal, a regulatory shock, a viral belief, or a supply-chain interruption does not remain local. It travels through balance sheets, expectations, collateral chains, inventories, and communication networks. Economists already model such propagation with input-output matrices, network multipliers, impulse-response functions, rational-expectations systems, and stochastic discount factors. The Soma-field vocabulary does not replace those methods. It asks whether, in some models, they share a common response kernel: an operator that maps an impulse $J(t)$ to a system response $G*J$. `interpretive`

For game theorists, this reframes equilibrium selection as a landscape problem. Multiple equilibria are no longer merely a nuisance for comparative statics. They are basins of attraction separated by barriers. Risk dominance, payoff dominance, stochastic stability, and basin size become geometrical questions. Harsanyi and Selten's equilibrium-selection programme, Kandori--Mailath--Rob's stochastic evolution, Young's convention formation, and Schelling's segregation dynamics are already landscape-like in spirit. [@harsanyi1988general; @kandori1993learning; @young1993evolution; @schelling1978micromotives] The contribution here is to make the landscape vocabulary explicit, connect it to Hopfield energy where lawful, and use the programme's evidence labels to prevent the metaphor from hardening into an unsupported identity. `interpretive`

For financial economists, the corresponding question is not whether a crash is literally a thermodynamic phase transition. It is whether the mathematics of critical slowing down, correlation-length growth, susceptibility, hysteresis, and barrier crossing provides testable leading indicators of loss of market resilience. Market correlations often rise in stress; liquidity can become one-sided; leverage can turn small price movements into forced sales; order books can thin; dealer balance sheets can become capacity constraints. A field model would need to turn such observables into a state vector, a coupling matrix, a noise level, and a transition rule. Until that is done, "market crash as phase transition" is an `open-hypothesis`, not an established law. `open-hypothesis`

For behavioural economists, the field language is equally double-edged. Loss aversion, reference dependence, framing, present bias, status quo bias, and limited attention already have rigorous models. Prospect theory, hyperbolic discounting, rational inattention, and dual-self models do not need Soma Field Theory to exist. [@kahneman1979prospect; @laibson1997golden] The useful question is whether some behavioural regularities can be read as features of a dynamic valuation landscape: asymmetric basins around gains and losses, barriers around the status quo, context-dependent deformation of payoff surfaces, and path-dependent memory kernels. That is a modelling proposal. It must compete with existing behavioural models on prediction, parsimony, and measurement. `open-hypothesis`

The volume therefore proceeds by separation. First, it identifies the exact mathematical cases: exact potential games, symmetric pairwise-interaction games, and Hopfield networks with Lyapunov energy. Second, it discusses analogous but non-exact cases: general games, markets with strategic feedback, financial networks with endogenous leverage, and heterogeneous-agent dynamics. Third, it asks how the programme's simulation and proof surfaces bear on economics. The answer is cautious. Lean theorems about complexity, WKB reachability, and formal encodings are real facts about their definitions; QUANT-EXP-1 is an exact statevector simulation; the economic claims in this volume are mostly derived under assumptions or open hypotheses. `interpretive`

That caution does not make the exercise toothless. Economics is full of models whose value lies in disciplined simplification. The Arrow--Debreu model is not a street market, the Black--Scholes equation is not a trading desk, and a mean-field game is not a city. Yet each can reveal a structure that empirical work can then refine. Black--Scholes is especially relevant because the mathematical co-identification paper treats it as a precedent: option pricing is governed by a parabolic PDE co-identifiable with the heat equation, so analytical machinery travels across domains [@blackscholes1973]. The lesson is not that all imports are legitimate. It is that legitimate imports require exact attention to assumptions. `interpretive`

The intended reader of this book may be sceptical. That scepticism is welcome. The field has seen many seductive analogies: markets as organisms, rationality as physics, preferences as energy, society as thermodynamics. Some were fruitful; others blurred explanation with metaphor. This volume's method is to keep the audit trail visible. Where the mapping is exact, the text says so and states the assumptions. Where it is an analogy, it says analogy. Where it is a programme claim waiting for data, it says open hypothesis. No reader should leave thinking that a Lean file has proved the Efficient Market Hypothesis false, or that a propagator tells one when to buy or sell an asset. It does not. `interpretive`

Nor is this a policy manual. The phrase "minimum regulatory intervention strength" is a model term, not a recommendation to deploy any actual intervention. Real regulatory policy involves distribution, legal authority, legitimacy, information, political economy, uncertainty, moral hazard, and unintended consequences. A barrier-height calculation, even if empirically calibrated, would be one input among many. The book's claim is narrower: if a market or institutional system can be represented as a coupled landscape with barriers, then interventions below barrier scale may perturb without changing regime, while targeted changes near saddles may be efficient in the model. `open-hypothesis`

The economics volume is thus an exercise in disciplined translation. It borrows the USF's language of fields, energies, propagators, thresholds, and attractors, but subjects each borrowed term to economic grammar. A price is not an emotion. A market is not a nervous system. A regulatory agency is not a therapist. Yet systems of agents can possess collective states, memory, coupling, lock-in, hysteresis, and cascades. Economics already studies these phenomena. [T]-Theory asks whether one formal language can help align them without erasing their differences. `interpretive`


## What Counts as an Economic Field?

An economic field, in this book, is not a substance. It is a modelling stance: choose a state space, define couplings among its components, specify how impulses enter, and study the response. A price vector can be a field variable if the model asks how price pressure at one node propagates through substitutes, complements, portfolios, funding links, and expectations. A strategy profile can be a field variable if unilateral deviations are local moves in a landscape. A belief distribution can be a field variable if public information changes many beliefs at once. The label `interpretive` is essential: the field is a formal representation of coupled economic response, not a claim that the economy has biological interiority.

This stance is close to ordinary economics in several places. Input-output economics already studies how sectoral shocks propagate through production networks. Macroeconomic impulse-response analysis already asks how a shock changes trajectories over time. Financial network models already treat obligations as edges across which distress travels. Spatial economics already studies diffusion, agglomeration, and congestion. Game theory already treats unilateral deviations as local moves in a strategic space. The [T]-Theory addition is the insistence that these can sometimes be read with a common operator grammar: $J$ for source, $G$ for response, $H$ for landscape, $K$ for memory, and $T_{\text{eff}}$ for noise or bounded adjustment. `interpretive`

The danger is that common grammar can conceal different mechanisms. A production-network multiplier is not a Green's function in the same sense as a Helmholtz propagator unless the operator, boundary conditions, and domain match. A Keynesian multiplier, a Leontief inverse, and a market microstructure propagator are all response objects, but they are not interchangeable. A responsible economics translation therefore begins every model with four questions: what is the state variable; what is the operator; what is the admissible shock; and what observation would falsify the proposed response law? `derived-under-assumptions`

The equilibrium question then changes shape. In static comparative statics one asks how a parameter change moves equilibrium. In a field picture one also asks how the shock travels before the new equilibrium, whether transient overshooting matters, whether memory changes the future landscape, whether agents coordinate on the transition, and whether the old basin remains metastable. These are familiar concerns in dynamic macroeconomics, industrial organisation, finance, and learning in games. The field language's value is to keep them in one picture without erasing disciplinary detail. `interpretive`

A minimal economic field model should therefore state its evidential status in advance. If the model is a theorem about exact potential games, it can use `derived-under-assumptions`. If it is a Lean theorem about a named formal object, it can use `kernel-verified` only for that object. If it is an agent-based or statevector experiment, it is `simulated`. If it is estimated from real market or laboratory data, it may be `empirical-result`. If it is a conceptual reading, it is `interpretive`. If it proposes a measurable but untested claim, it is `open-hypothesis`. This is not bureaucratic decoration. It is the difference between interdisciplinary science and metaphor drift.

The economist may also ask why the word "somatic" belongs here at all. In this volume it functions historically and methodologically: the economics book is one domain translation of a programme whose scientific engine began with somatic and neural fields. It does not mean that an economy has a body. At most, economic agents are embodied and institutions aggregate their choices. Many economic models can ignore embodiment entirely. Behavioural and financial-crisis models may need it, because fear, trust, salience, and arousal alter risk-taking and coordination. The model decides the relevance, not the brand name. `interpretive`

The same care applies to rationality. A field model should not caricature rational choice as static and itself as dynamic. Economic theory contains dynamic programming, stochastic games, adaptive learning, recursive equilibrium, search, mechanism design, and information design. The better claim is comparative: where existing models produce multiple equilibria, fragile coordination, hysteresis, or path-dependent switching, energy-landscape language may help unify selection and transition analysis. If it cannot outperform or clarify existing tools, it should be discarded for that problem. `open-hypothesis`
## Relation to Existing Economic Dynamics

The field account should be placed beside, not above, existing dynamic economics. In macroeconomics, dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models already specify shocks, state variables, policy rules, and adjustment paths. In industrial organisation, dynamic games model investment, entry, exit, and learning. In finance, no-arbitrage models, market microstructure, and stochastic volatility models already use continuous-time dynamics. In evolutionary game theory, learning and selection already move populations across strategy space. The Soma-field vocabulary earns its place only when it clarifies common structure across these existing tools. `interpretive`

One possible contribution is the common treatment of metastability. A DSGE model may have occasionally binding constraints; an industrial-organisation model may have entry deterrence; a finance model may have liquidity spirals; a game-learning model may have convention lock-in. Each involves persistence that is not simply equilibrium. Energy landscapes, barriers, and noise provide a cross-domain way to discuss why systems remain where they are and what scale of perturbation changes them. But the underlying equations remain domain-specific. `interpretive`

A second contribution is memory. Standard Markovian models deliberately compress history into the current state. Many economic systems remember more than that: credit histories, reputations, habits, installed bases, durable capital, legal precedent, trauma-like institutional memory after crises, and model risk after losses. The programme's kernel notation $K(t-t')$ invites economists to ask when memory kernels are necessary and how they decay. This is an `open-hypothesis` until linked to data, but it names an important modelling choice. `open-hypothesis`

A third contribution is scale. Micro choices, firm networks, sectoral production, financial markets, national policy, and global supply chains interact. Economics has tools for each level, but cross-scale translation is hard. A Green's-function architecture encourages explicit scale separation: what is local, what is aggregated, what is transmitted, and what is lost in projection? The answer will differ across applications. The value is not a universal scale law; it is a disciplined way to ask scale questions. `interpretive`

A fourth contribution is auditability. Interdisciplinary economics often suffers from beautiful analogies that cannot be checked. The evidence-label system is a practical remedy. It forces the author to say whether a claim is a theorem, a model derivation, a simulation, a measurement, an interpretation, or an open hypothesis. For economists used to assumptions and identification strategies, this should feel familiar. It is a claim-identification strategy for theory transfer. `interpretive`

# Exact Where Exact: Nash Equilibrium, Potential Games, and Hopfield Energy

The first task is to protect the strongest claim from overreach. A Nash equilibrium is a fixed point in strategic incentives: given everyone else's strategy, no player wishes to deviate. A Hopfield minimum is a local minimum of an energy function under the network's allowed state updates. These are not the same definition. They become the same structure only when the strategic incentives are integrable into a single scalar potential and when the local neighbourhood used for deviation matches the local neighbourhood used for energy descent. `derived-under-assumptions`

In a finite exact potential game, for every player $i$, every profile of others' strategies $s_{-i}$, and every pair of strategies $a_i,b_i$, the payoff change satisfies

$$u_i(a_i,s_{-i})-u_i(b_i,s_{-i})=\Phi(a_i,s_{-i})-\Phi(b_i,s_{-i}).$$

A pure profile is a Nash equilibrium exactly when no unilateral move raises any player's payoff. By the defining equality, that is exactly when no unilateral move raises $\Phi$. Set $H=-\Phi$. Then the same profile is a local minimum of $H$ under unilateral moves. The statement is exact, elementary, and important. It is also narrower than "all Nash equilibria are Hopfield minima". `derived-under-assumptions`

Rosenthal's congestion games are the canonical finite example: each player's route choice imposes costs on shared resources, and the Rosenthal potential sums the marginal congestion costs encountered as users are added to each resource. [@rosenthal1973class] Network congestion, routing, facility location, club goods, and some public-goods games can often be written in potential form. In such games, energy language is not merely ornamental. With the cost-potential sign convention, local minima are equilibria; with a utility potential, $H=-\Phi$ gives the corresponding energy minima. Barriers are coordination costs, and stochastic perturbations can select among equilibria. `derived-under-assumptions`

A symmetric pairwise binary-action game gives the Hopfield connection in its most concrete form. Let $s_i\in\{-1,+1\}$ and suppose the interaction payoff can be represented by symmetric weights $W_{ij}=W_{ji}$ and biases $b_i$. Define

$$H(s)=-\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}s_is_j-\sum_i b_is_i.$$

The actual number, depth, and position of the equilibria are set by the coupling weights $W_{ij}$ and biases $b_i$, which this general form leaves unspecified; the real game is over discrete $s_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, not this continuous coordinate.

A single spin flip changes $H$ by the negative of the corresponding potential change. If each agent updates by a myopic best response and the weights are symmetric, asynchronous updates descend the energy until a local minimum is reached. This is the classic Hopfield Lyapunov property in economic dress [@hopfield1982]. `derived-under-assumptions`

Continuous differentiable games require more care. If each player controls $s_i\in S_i\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_i}$ and there is a differentiable potential $\Phi$ such that $\nabla_{s_i}u_i=\nabla_{s_i}\Phi$, interior stationary Nash candidates satisfy $\nabla_{s_i}\Phi=0$ for all $i$. On constrained sets, KKT conditions replace simple gradients. Local maxima of $\Phi$ subject to feasible unilateral deviations correspond to local Nash equilibria; $H=-\Phi$ again gives local energy minima. But concavity, constraint qualification, and boundary conditions decide whether stationary points are equilibria. `derived-under-assumptions`

The common overstatement is to start from a payoff matrix, symmetrise it, and call the result a Hopfield energy. That may produce a model, but it changes the game. In an asymmetric game, unilateral payoff changes need not be integrable. Rock--paper--scissors is the simplest warning: it has cyclic incentives rather than a scalar potential. Zero-sum games likewise resist energy-minimisation readings; their natural geometry is saddle-point or Hamiltonian-like, not Lyapunov descent. A Hopfield embedding may approximate some learning dynamics, but it is not the same game unless the lost antisymmetric component is irrelevant to the question. `interpretive`

This matters because economic conflict often lives in the antisymmetric component. Bargaining, market making, auctions, contest games, arms races, and predator--prey industrial dynamics include strategic cycling, signalling, and non-potential incentives. The potential-game subset is rich, but it is not the whole of economics. The [T]-Theory economics programme is strongest when it treats potential games as the exact core and other games as extensions whose fidelity must be tested. `interpretive`

There is a useful decomposition. Any sufficiently regular game can be analysed for its potential component, harmonic or cyclic component, and non-strategic component in game-decomposition frameworks. [@candogan2011flows] The potential component supplies a landscape; the harmonic component supplies cycles. A market may have both: inventory and risk constraints pulling toward equilibria, while strategic anticipation and liquidity provision generate cycles. A Soma-field translation should therefore not force all dynamics into gradient descent. It should ask which part of the game is landscape and which part is rotation. `open-hypothesis`

In population games, the same distinction appears as the difference between potential dynamics and evolutionary cycling. Replicator dynamics in a potential game tend to climb the potential under appropriate metrics; in anti-coordination or cyclic games they may orbit. Mean-field games add another layer: agents optimise against a distribution of others, and the equilibrium is a fixed point between individual control and population law. A Hopfield analogy may still be instructive, but the exact energy mapping needs a potential. `interpretive`

The phrase "Nash Attractor Resolvent" should therefore be read as a modelling proposal for classes of games with an energy representation. In linear response, a resolvent $(H-\lambda)^{-1}$ tells how a system responds near the spectrum of an operator. In game terms, the question is how a strategic landscape responds to perturbations: a tax, subsidy, information shock, enforcement change, technological innovation, or norm shift. If the landscape is potential, the resolvent language can be tied to Hessians, spectral gaps, and susceptibility. If the landscape is not potential, one must use a more general dynamical operator. `open-hypothesis`

Spectral gaps are another place where precision matters. In a Hopfield network or reversible Markov process, a gap often controls convergence time and metastability. In coordination games, a small gap in the Hessian around a minimum may indicate a flat direction: the system is easy to move along one collective mode. In financial correlation networks, an eigenvalue concentration can indicate market-wide common movement. These are related intuitions, not identical measurements. The book uses "gap" as a family resemblance unless a particular matrix and theorem are specified. `interpretive`

Risk dominance and basin size provide a bridge to classical game theory. In a two-equilibrium coordination game, one equilibrium may be payoff dominant while another is risk dominant. Stochastic perturbations select according to basins and barriers. The field picture renders this visually: the selected equilibrium is not simply the highest welfare point, but the attractor most likely reached under the adjustment process. In potential games, that statement can be formalised through perturbed best-response dynamics and stationary distributions. `derived-under-assumptions`

Logit quantal response equilibrium also belongs here. Agents choose better responses with higher probability but make mistakes whose frequency depends on a rationality parameter. Along a selected QRE branch, limit points as the rationality parameter rises are Nash equilibria, but the path need not be a simple Gibbs measure over pure profiles. This is close to a thermal landscape picture: the rationality parameter acts like inverse temperature. But logit QRE is a behavioural choice model; Hopfield temperature is a dynamical noise parameter. They can be linked in models, but they are not the same by definition. [@mckelvey1995quantal] `interpretive`

Bounded rationality is therefore not simply "noise". It may include cognitive costs, limited attention, misspecified beliefs, social learning, institutional frictions, and affective salience. A thermal model compresses these into one or a few parameters. That compression may be useful if it predicts convergence, lock-in, or escape. It becomes misleading if it hides the institutional sources of error. Economists should treat $T_{\text{eff}}$ as a reduced-form construct until linked to measurable frictions. `open-hypothesis`

The Prisoner's Dilemma shows why exact language matters. In the one-shot standard Prisoner's Dilemma, mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium; mutual cooperation is not a Nash equilibrium, because either player can gain by defecting. Cooperation is the social optimum, not a local incentive optimum. Calling cooperation a local energy minimum in the original game is wrong. It can become a minimum in a transformed game: repeated interaction, reputation, punishment, kin selection, network reciprocity, or institutional enforcement changes the payoffs and may create a cooperative equilibrium. `derived-under-assumptions`

Thus the topological reading of the Prisoner's Dilemma should be repaired as follows. In the one-shot game, cooperation is a desirable point lying uphill from individual incentives: a saddle or unstable point in the unmodified landscape. Institutions alter the landscape by changing payoffs, information, continuation values, or enforcement, thereby deepening the cooperative basin or raising the barrier against defection. The topological obstruction is not that cooperation is already a shallower attractor. It is that the socially preferred point is not incentive-stable until the game is changed. `interpretive`

Mechanism design can then be read as landscape engineering. A mechanism changes the game form so that individually optimal behaviour implements a desired social choice. In energy terms, it shapes the landscape so that target profiles become stable basins. Vickrey--Clarke--Groves mechanisms, matching mechanisms, auctions, and market rules all do this in different ways. The field language may help discuss robustness: not only whether truthful reporting is an equilibrium, but how steeply deviations are penalised, how shocks move the system, and whether undesirable equilibria remain nearby. `interpretive`

There is a normative warning. A stable attractor is not automatically good. Oppression, collusion, monopoly, discrimination, corruption, and inefficient conventions can be highly stable. Economics already knows this: equilibrium is not welfare. [T]-Theory must not smuggle welfare into stability. A landscape can describe why a bad equilibrium persists; it cannot by itself say that the equilibrium is just, efficient, or legitimate. Welfare economics, social choice, political economy, and law remain necessary. `interpretive`

The exact potential-game core gives a clean research agenda. Start with laboratory games whose payoff matrices are known. Identify whether each game is exact potential, ordinal potential, weighted potential, or non-potential. Compute the potential where it exists. Define $H=-\Phi$. Run subjects or simulated learning agents under controlled noise. Estimate transition rates between basins. Compare observed equilibrium selection with barrier heights, basin volumes, and stochastic-stability predictions. This would test the Hopfield-Nash claim without pretending it already covers all games. `open-hypothesis`

A second agenda concerns computational complexity. Finding equilibria can be hard. Potential games guarantee pure equilibria under finite improvement paths, but convergence may be slow; local search can become trapped; global optima may be computationally difficult. Hopfield networks are also prone to local minima. The analogy therefore does not make equilibrium computation easy. It makes the difficulty geometrical. Barriers, saddles, and metastability become the computational objects to study. `interpretive`

A third agenda concerns institutional design. If a regulator, platform, or protocol designer can estimate the landscape, then intervention can target the smallest modification that changes stability. But this requires data: observed strategies, payoff proxies, beliefs, network structure, and response dynamics. Without those, the WKB threshold is rhetoric. With them, it becomes a testable optimal-control problem. `open-hypothesis`

The discipline of this chapter is simple: exact potential games license exact energy language; general games do not. This does not weaken the economics volume. It gives it a spine. A strong interdisciplinary programme is not one that declares every economic phenomenon to be the same field. It is one that knows when an identity is exact, when a model is useful, and when the analogy should stop. `interpretive`


## Worked Examples: From Congestion to Cycles

A road congestion game is the clean case. Players choose routes; each route is a set of links; each link has a cost increasing in the number of users. A unilateral route change changes the player's cost by exactly the marginal change recorded in Rosenthal's potential. If one defines welfare with the sign convention of utility rather than cost, the potential's local maxima are the pure equilibria; with $H=-\Phi$ they become minima. A field reading can then ask which route equilibrium is selected from a given starting distribution, how tolls deform the landscape, and how stochastic experimentation lets traffic escape a bad convention. `derived-under-assumptions`

A local public-goods game is less tidy but still often potential-like. Suppose agents on a graph contribute to a local good whose benefits spill over symmetrically to neighbours. If the spillovers are symmetric and payoffs are separable in the right way, the total potential aggregates marginal contribution incentives. If spillovers are asymmetric -- for example, upstream pollution harms downstream neighbours more than the reverse -- the potential may fail. The asymmetry is economically meaningful. Replacing it by a symmetric matrix would erase the direction of harm. `interpretive`

A Cournot oligopoly with homogeneous goods can sometimes be written as a potential game under restrictive demand and cost conditions; a differentiated-products market with strategic complements and substitutes may or may not admit a potential. Here the landscape test is useful. If a potential exists, comparative statics can be supplemented by barrier analysis: do firms get trapped in high-output or low-output conventions, and how do capacity constraints move basins? If no potential exists, the model may exhibit cycles, limit behaviour, or strategic rotation. That is not failure; it is information about the strategic structure. `open-hypothesis`

Auctions show the danger of overextension. A sealed-bid auction has equilibria defined by beliefs, information, risk attitudes, and payment rules. The object of interest may be a Bayesian Nash equilibrium, not a pure profile in a finite complete-information game. A Hopfield energy over bid vectors would be an additional model, not a direct representation of the auction. It might approximate learning in repeated auctions or collusive bidding dynamics, but it cannot replace Bayesian incentive analysis without proof. `interpretive`

Matching markets provide another boundary. Stable matchings are local in the sense of blocking pairs, not necessarily in the sense of unilateral payoff gradients. A blocking pair is a two-agent deviation. The landscape neighbourhood is therefore different from unilateral Nash. A potential may exist for some matching variants, but the natural mathematical structure often involves lattices of stable matchings, deferred acceptance, priorities, and strategy-proofness. Energy language may visualise transitions among matchings, but it should not be imposed as the primitive. `interpretive`

Rock--paper--scissors is the negative control every economics-field model should pass. The incentive field curls. There is no scalar potential whose gradient captures the cycle. Replicator dynamics may circle the interior equilibrium rather than descend to a minimum. If a proposed Hopfield translation turns rock--paper--scissors into a static basin without preserving the cycle, the translation has failed. This is valuable because it tells us what the model cannot be. `derived-under-assumptions`

A zero-sum security game gives a policy-relevant version of the same point. Defender and attacker incentives are opposed; the equilibrium is a saddle of the value function, not a common minimum. The right mathematics is minimax, convex-concave optimisation, and sometimes stochastic games. A field-theoretic representation could use Hamiltonian or saddle dynamics, but the word "energy minimum" would mislead. [T]-Theory economics should allow non-gradient operators rather than forcing all strategic life into Hopfield form. `interpretive`

Potential-game exactness also clarifies mixed strategies. Nash's existence theorem for finite games relies on mixed strategies, but a Hopfield energy over pure profiles does not automatically represent mixed equilibria. One can sometimes define an energy over distributions, logit responses, entropy-regularised objectives, or mean-field population states. Those are new mathematical objects with their own assumptions. A mixed Nash equilibrium is not a local minimum of the pure-profile potential simply because it is a Nash equilibrium. `derived-under-assumptions`

Boundary conditions matter in continuous strategy spaces. A first-order condition may vanish at an interior optimum, but many economic equilibria lie on constraints: capacity limits, non-negativity, budgets, borrowing constraints, collateral requirements, reserve requirements. KKT multipliers are not optional technicalities; they are the economics. A landscape picture should show walls, corners, and reflecting or absorbing boundaries, not just smooth bowls. `derived-under-assumptions`

This gives a practical classification table for future work. Class A: exact potential games, where Hopfield energy is exact after sign change. Class B: near-potential games, where the potential component dominates but cyclic components may matter. Class C: non-potential games, where energy language is analogy or decomposition only. Class D: saddle and Hamiltonian strategic systems, where a different field operator is needed. Class E: institutional mechanisms that intentionally transform a game from one class to another. The economics volume's strongest claims live in Class A and carefully labelled parts of Class B. `interpretive`
## Equilibrium Concepts the Mapping Must Not Confuse

Economists use many equilibrium notions because different informational and institutional settings require them. A pure Nash equilibrium, mixed Nash equilibrium, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium, sequential equilibrium, correlated equilibrium, rational-expectations equilibrium, competitive equilibrium, and stable matching are not interchangeable. A field model that speaks simply of "the equilibrium" risks flattening this taxonomy. The Hopfield mapping is clearest for pure equilibria of exact potential games. It does not automatically cover beliefs, histories, off-path threats, information partitions, or market-clearing price systems. `interpretive`

Correlated equilibrium is especially instructive. A mediator can recommend actions according to a joint distribution such that no player wants to deviate after receiving their recommendation. This is a distributional object, not a single strategy profile. It may be represented in a larger state space, but the energy, if any, lives over distributions and incentive constraints. Likewise rational-expectations equilibrium requires consistency between beliefs and the law of motion generated by those beliefs. A landscape metaphor can visualise fixed points of expectations, but the fixed point is epistemic as well as strategic. `derived-under-assumptions`

Subgame perfection and sequential equilibrium add time and credibility. A strategy profile may be Nash but rely on non-credible threats. Hopfield energy over terminal action profiles would miss the tree structure that makes the threat non-credible. To translate dynamic games, the state must include histories, beliefs, continuation values, and information sets. The energy landscape becomes a landscape over policies or value functions, not simply over realised actions. This is possible in principle but much more demanding than the slogan suggests. `open-hypothesis`

General equilibrium adds another boundary. Competitive equilibrium is defined by price vectors, allocations, endowments, technologies, and market clearing. The welfare theorems depend on convexity, completeness, price-taking, and absence of externalities. A Hopfield minimum over excess demand is not automatically a competitive equilibrium, and a competitive equilibrium is not automatically a local minimum of social energy. If [T]-Theory wants a general-equilibrium version, it must state which functional is minimised and how individual optimisation and market clearing are encoded. `open-hypothesis`

These distinctions are not pedantry. They protect the programme from false victories. It is easy to map a simplified coordination game into a potential and then speak as if all equilibrium economics had been grounded in Hopfield theory. It has not. What has been grounded is one important family of equilibrium-selection problems. A mature economics volume should celebrate that exact family and let other equilibrium concepts demand their own operators. `interpretive`
## Mathematical Minimum for a Future Anchor Paper

A future economics anchor paper should begin with definitions rather than analogies. Define a finite player set, finite action sets, payoff functions, and the unilateral-deviation graph on pure profiles. Define exact potential, ordinal potential, and weighted potential. Define Hopfield energy on the same graph as $H=-\Phi$ where $\Phi$ exists. Then prove: a pure profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if it is a local minimum of $H$ on the unilateral-deviation graph. This theorem is short, but it is the hinge on which the exact claim turns. `derived-under-assumptions`

The second theorem should cover continuous games. Let strategy sets be compact and convex, payoffs differentiable, and suppose a differentiable potential satisfies $\nabla_i u_i=\alpha_i\nabla_i\Phi$ with positive weights where appropriate. State the KKT conditions under which a constrained local maximum of $\Phi$ is a local Nash equilibrium. Then state the limitations: non-concavity, boundary degeneracy, and second-order conditions. This prevents a first-order stationary point from being mistaken for equilibrium. `derived-under-assumptions`

The third result should be a decomposition or counterexample. Show explicitly that rock--paper--scissors has no scalar potential compatible with its payoff differences. Show that a zero-sum two-player game is naturally saddle-structured. Show that symmetrising an asymmetric game changes unilateral incentives. These negative results are not hostile; they are the formal fence around the valid import. `derived-under-assumptions`

The fourth result should connect dynamics. For asynchronous best-response or logit updates in a finite potential game, state when the potential is a Lyapunov function or when the stationary distribution is Gibbs-like. If the proof requires reversibility, finite state space, or a particular noise rule, say so. If the dynamics are not reversible, do not call the stationary measure Boltzmann merely because it has exponentials. `derived-under-assumptions`

The fifth result should prepare empirical use. Define barrier height between two equilibria on the profile graph, perhaps as a minimum maximal energy along paths. Define basin volume relative to an initial-state distribution. Define transition probability under a specified stochastic process. These definitions make the economic claims measurable. Without them, "barrier", "basin", and "tunnelling" remain literary terms. `open-hypothesis`

Such a paper would be modest, but it would be valuable. It would tell economists exactly when the Hopfield identification is a theorem, when it is an approximation, and when it is wrong. It would also create reusable objects for laboratory and market studies. That is the kind of mathematics a domain book should encourage: not a universal proclamation, but a precise bridge strong enough to carry sceptical readers. `interpretive`

# Markets Near Criticality: Econophysics, Financial Cascades, and the Limits of Analogy

Markets are coupled systems with feedback. Prices aggregate information, but they also change constraints, collateral values, expectations, risk limits, and narratives. A fall in one price can force sales elsewhere; a volatility spike can raise margins; rising correlations can invalidate diversification; a liquidity provider can become a liquidity demander. These features make financial markets natural candidates for criticality language. They do not make criticality true by assertion. `interpretive`

Econophysics emerged from the observation that financial time series often show features familiar to statistical physicists: heavy tails, volatility clustering, scaling regimes, long-range dependence in absolute returns, and collective behaviour. Random-matrix methods separate market modes from noise; spin models represent herding; percolation and network models represent contagion; minority games model adaptive competition for scarce payoff opportunities. The field has produced useful tools and controversial claims. Its best work is empirically disciplined; its weakest work mistakes visual similarity for explanation. `interpretive`

The [T]-Theory economics volume should be read as joining the disciplined side. Its phase-transition language must specify an order parameter, control parameter, susceptibility, and empirical test. A market crash cannot be called a phase transition merely because it is sudden. It becomes a phase-transition model only when one identifies a collective variable whose qualitative stability changes as a control parameter crosses a threshold. Candidate control parameters include leverage, liquidity depth, cross-asset correlation, funding fragility, margin requirements, concentration of positions, and common risk-model exposure. `open-hypothesis`

A simple schematic may help. Let $x(t)$ be a vector of market-state variables: returns, order imbalances, leverage, volatility, funding spreads, correlation factors, and liquidity measures. Let $H(x;\theta)$ be an inferred potential under parameters $\theta$. Normal trading corresponds to a basin in which liquidity replenishes after perturbations. Stress corresponds to flattening along a collective selling mode. A crash corresponds to a loss of local stability or a noise-driven escape into a deleveraging basin. This is a model class, not a fact about every market. `open-hypothesis`

Criticality has several signatures. Correlation length grows: distant assets or institutions move together. Susceptibility rises: small news has large price impact. Recovery slows: after a perturbation, the system takes longer to return. Variance and autocorrelation may increase. Eigenvalues of correlation or exposure matrices concentrate. Liquidity becomes asymmetric: buying and selling no longer have symmetric depth. These signatures are studied in ecological early-warning signals and financial systemic-risk metrics. [@scheffer2009early] Their application to markets requires care because market participants react to the signals themselves. `open-hypothesis`

The phrase "correlation length" needs translation. In a physical lattice it has spatial meaning. In finance, the relevant distance may be sectoral, balance-sheet, derivative-exposure, supply-chain, ownership, funding, or belief-network distance. A small regional bank and a global hedge fund may be close in funding-space while far in geography. A criticality model must define the graph before measuring length. Otherwise "diverging correlation length" is a metaphor. `interpretive`

Similarly, temperature is not volatility. Volatility is a measured dispersion of price changes. Effective temperature may be a reduced-form parameter summarising noise, disagreement, leverage-induced instability, or bounded rationality. In some models higher noise helps escape bad equilibria; in others it destabilises good ones. The original generated framing spoke of effective temperature falling as herding intensifies. Other econophysics models associate crisis with rising volatility or rising susceptibility. The correct direction is model-dependent. This volume therefore treats $T_{\text{eff}}$ as a parameter to be defined, not a slogan. `open-hypothesis`

Financial cascades can be landscape escapes without being thermodynamic transitions. A bank run, margin spiral, fire sale, or clearing-house stress event may result from threshold rules and network obligations. The system crosses contractual discontinuities. A topological picture may still be useful: basins merge, barriers vanish, and paths that were impossible become forced. But the micro-foundations are legal and institutional as much as physical. The model must include them. `interpretive`

The WKB metaphor is tempting. In physics, barrier crossing has exponentially small probabilities controlled by action or barrier height. In markets, rare transitions may likewise depend nonlinearly on leverage, liquidity, and coordination barriers. But WKB is not a universal name for any exponential. To use it seriously, one needs a stochastic differential equation, a noise scale, an action functional, boundary conditions, and empirical calibration. The programme's path-sensitive extension ISS-035 is relevant here: the cost of transition may be cumulative along constrained micro-paths, not just local barrier height. `open-hypothesis`

The regulatory-intervention idea should be understood in that limited sense. Suppose a market is trapped in an undesirable basin: a bank-run equilibrium, a frozen credit market, a collusive convention, or a panic liquidation mode. An intervention may change payoffs, inject liquidity, guarantee liabilities, alter information, or coordinate expectations. In a landscape model, the intervention is a source term or parameter shift. Below some scale it may fail; above it may change regime. That is plausible. It is not by itself a policy recommendation. `open-hypothesis`

Economists will immediately ask about Lucas critique and reflexivity. If agents know the intervention rule, they change behaviour before the threshold. If risk managers monitor the same spectral gap, their actions may either stabilise the system or amplify crowding. If a central bank credibly promises support, the landscape changes immediately through expectations. A field model must therefore be strategic and anticipatory, not merely mechanical. The propagator transmits beliefs as well as flows. `interpretive`

Market efficiency also needs careful treatment. The swarm-propagator file's theorem `propagator_beats_classical` proves the arithmetic inequality that concrete propagator cost $N^2$ is lower than classical message-passing cost $NK$ when $0<N<K$. `kernel-verified` It does not prove that financial markets are efficient, nor that a market can coordinate in one step. In an economic reading, the result suggests a target: if a truthful global kernel is available, some coordination problems can avoid sequential local gossip. But markets have private information, incentives to conceal, transaction costs, adverse selection, and strategic delay. `interpretive`

The Britain 1939 broadcast example in the benchmark material creates a warning. The formal theorem states a cost advantage when $0<N<K$; an example with $N=45,000,000$ and $K=5$ cannot be justified by the same raw operation inequality. It may illustrate low-latency broadcast topology rather than arithmetic operation count. The economics book should not repeat the example as a theorem-backed market claim. This is precisely why evidence labels matter. `interpretive`

What, then, can financial economists take from the propagator idea? First, dense common signals can coordinate many agents faster than local pairwise updating. Prices, central-bank announcements, clearing prices, benchmark rates, and public stress tests are propagators in this loose sense. Second, common signals can stabilise or destabilise depending on incentives. A public backstop can halt a run; a public mark-to-market shock can intensify one. Third, the cost of distributing a global signal differs from the cost of computing it truthfully. `interpretive`

Econophysics sometimes underplays institutions; institutional economics sometimes underplays nonlinear dynamics. The field view can connect them. An exchange rule, circuit breaker, margin model, disclosure regime, settlement cycle, or bankruptcy law changes the coupling matrix. It can damp cascades, delay them, reroute them, or concentrate them. A topology of markets would be a topology of legally structured obligations, not merely a topology of price correlations. `open-hypothesis`

There are plausible empirical projects. One could estimate time-varying correlation networks and test whether spectral concentration predicts future liquidity stress better than standard volatility metrics. One could model order-book states as basins and test whether depth imbalance predicts regime shifts. One could analyse clearing networks for barrier-like thresholds where defaults cascade. One could run agent-based markets with known payoff landscapes and compare observed crash probabilities to action-based predictions. None of these would prove [T]-Theory as a whole. Each would test a narrow economic claim. `open-hypothesis`

The behavioural component matters in crises. Panic is not irrational noise outside economics; it is a coordination state. If each agent expects others to sell, selling may be locally rational even when collectively destructive. A crash basin is therefore both psychological and strategic. Behavioural finance already studies overreaction, underreaction, disposition effects, limits to arbitrage, and noise-trader risk. [@shleifer2000inefficient] The field language adds a way to discuss how individual biases couple into collective modes. `interpretive`

Affect should be handled cautiously. Saying that markets have a somatic field does not mean an index feels fear. It means that embodied human agents, algorithmic systems trained on human behaviour, institutional constraints, and communication networks can produce aggregate states usefully modelled by field variables. The substrate is economic and social. The term "somatic" is inherited from the programme's core but must not anthropomorphise markets. `interpretive`

Financial criticality also has a normative dimension. Crashes redistribute wealth, employment, housing security, public debt, and political trust. A model that predicts fragility without addressing distribution is incomplete as economics. Conversely, a policy that prevents every local failure may create moral hazard and larger future fragility. Stability is valuable but not absolute. This returns us to the earlier warning: attractor depth is descriptive, not automatically normative. `interpretive`

No paragraph in this chapter is investment advice. A criticality signal, even if someday validated, would be uncertain, reflexive, and context-specific. It would not tell an individual reader what to buy, sell, or hold. The purpose is scientific and institutional: to formulate hypotheses about systemic risk, coordination, and policy thresholds that can be tested with appropriate data and governance. `interpretive`

The strongest version of the financial-criticality proposal is therefore modest. It says: some markets may approach regimes in which correlations, susceptibility, and recovery times change in ways analogous to critical phenomena; a Hopfield or stochastic-potential representation may capture parts of that process; regulatory or institutional interventions may be modelled as landscape deformations; empirical validation requires pre-specified observables, out-of-sample tests, and comparison with standard econometric baselines. That is enough. `open-hypothesis`


## A Measurement Protocol for Financial Criticality

A testable criticality model begins by refusing to model "the market" as a whole. It selects a domain: an equity limit-order book, a repo funding network, a sovereign-bond market, a clearing member network, a stablecoin redemption mechanism, a commodity supply chain, or an interbank exposure graph. Each domain has different state variables and boundaries. The criticality claim is meaningful only after the domain is fixed. `open-hypothesis`

The second step is to define the state vector. In a limit-order-book study the state might include bid and ask depth at several levels, order arrival rates, cancellation rates, signed order flow, short-horizon volatility, and inventory proxies. In a funding-network study it might include haircuts, maturities, collateral classes, counterparty exposures, rollover rates, and liquidity buffers. In a portfolio-overlap study it might include holdings, leverage, redemption pressure, and covariance of liquidation demand. These choices are substantive economics, not data plumbing. `derived-under-assumptions`

The third step is to define the coupling matrix. Correlation is not always coupling. Two assets may be correlated because they share a macro factor, because investors rebalance them together, because one is collateral for the other, or because both are priced by the same dealer balance sheet. A field model should distinguish statistical association from transmission mechanism where possible. Otherwise the inferred landscape may fit the past and fail under intervention. `open-hypothesis`

The fourth step is to define candidate order parameters. A market-criticality study might track the largest eigenvalue of a correlation matrix, the algebraic connectivity of a liquidity network, a common-mode order-flow factor, the slope of the price-impact curve, the probability of failed settlement, or the fraction of agents in a forced-sale state. Each order parameter implies a different theory of what becomes ordered during crisis. There is no generic crash order parameter. `open-hypothesis`

The fifth step is to define critical precursors. Possible precursors include increasing variance, rising autocorrelation, slower recovery after shocks, shrinking liquidity depth, growing common-mode exposure, declining network modularity, and concentration of leverage in nodes with high betweenness. These should be pre-registered and compared with null models. Financial data are noisy, non-stationary, and reflexive; many apparent precursors will be artefacts. `open-hypothesis`

The sixth step is out-of-sample evaluation. A model that explains 2008, March 2020, or a known flash crash after the fact is not enough. It must predict withheld episodes or cross-sectional stress differences better than baseline econometric and risk-management models. Baselines might include GARCH-family volatility forecasts, CoVaR, SRISK, liquidity-adjusted VaR, stress-test scenarios, or standard network contagion models. The field model earns status only by improving something measurable. `empirical-result`

The seventh step is intervention analysis. If the model claims a barrier height, ask how an actual intervention changes it. A trading halt changes time and information. A liquidity facility changes funding constraints. A guarantee changes beliefs about default. A capital requirement changes balance-sheet geometry slowly. A disclosure rule changes information and possibly panic. These are different source terms. Lumping them into one $J(t)$ may be useful for algebra, but policy interpretation requires decomposition. `interpretive`

The eighth step is reflexivity. Once agents know that a spectral-gap measure matters, they may game it, hedge it, arbitrage it, or crowd around it. Public risk metrics can become targets. A useful model of financial criticality should therefore include a second-order response: how publication of the diagnostic changes the field it measures. This is one reason no criticality measure should be sold as a simple alarm bell. `open-hypothesis`

The final step is ethical and institutional review. Crisis prediction can move markets, influence regulation, affect public trust, and redistribute losses. Even a correct systemic-risk model requires governance about disclosure, use, uncertainty, and accountability. A field model that ignores these issues would be economically naive. It would also be inconsistent with the programme's own insistence that social and legal constraints matter at higher scales. `interpretive`
## Financial Criticality and Behavioural Microfoundations

A market-criticality model also needs microfoundations for why correlations rise. One route is balance-sheet mechanics: leveraged agents sell the same assets when constraints bind. A second is information: agents infer from one another's trades and converge on a common belief. A third is risk management: similar models produce similar de-risking at similar thresholds. A fourth is institutional: regulation, accounting, or mandates force common action. A fifth is affective and narrative: fear spreads through communication and changes risk tolerance. These mechanisms can produce similar correlation patterns with different policy implications. `interpretive`

If common movement comes from balance-sheet constraints, liquidity provision and capital structure matter. If it comes from information cascades, disclosure design and signal credibility matter. If it comes from model crowding, diversity of risk models matters. If it comes from mandates, rule design matters. If it comes from narrative panic, communication and trust matter. A single spectral-gap measure may detect fragility but not identify cause. The field model must therefore be paired with causal identification. `open-hypothesis`

Behavioural microfoundations are likewise diverse. Loss aversion may reduce selling of losing assets in one setting and intensify panic in another if losses threaten solvency. Present bias may lead to under-insurance before a crisis and overreaction during one. Framing may stabilise expectations when it clarifies policy, or destabilise them when it makes losses salient. The landscape is not fixed; it is continuously reframed by information and institutions. `interpretive`

This is why market criticality should not be reduced to a universal crash formula. A useful model might be local: a particular funding market under a particular collateral regime, or a particular platform with a known liquidation rule. Local models can be wrong in ways that teach. Universal formulas often survive only because they are too vague to test. The economics book should prefer local falsifiability over universal drama. `open-hypothesis`
## Boundary Conditions as Economic Content

Field equations are incomplete without boundary conditions, and economics is largely about boundaries. A budget constraint is a boundary. A limited-liability rule is a boundary. A margin call is a moving boundary. A bankruptcy stay, trading halt, collateral eligibility rule, deposit-insurance cap, or platform access condition defines where agents can and cannot move. Many economic discontinuities arise not from smooth preference changes but from hitting such boundaries. `interpretive`

This matters for the energy picture. A basin may look shallow in an unconstrained model and deep once borrowing constraints are imposed. A cooperative equilibrium may look reachable until enforcement costs are included. A market may appear liquid until a regulatory capital boundary forces dealers to withdraw. A household may appear to prefer the status quo when the real barrier is fixed cost, paperwork, or fear of losing eligibility. Boundary conditions are not peripheral technical details; they are often the mechanism. `derived-under-assumptions`

For game theory, boundaries determine admissible deviations. A Nash equilibrium is defined relative to what each player is allowed to choose. Change the action set and the equilibrium changes. For mechanism design, the whole art is often to change the boundary: make some messages admissible, some transfers feasible, some coalitions illegal, some commitments credible. A Hopfield landscape without its admissible-neighbour graph is therefore not an economic object yet. `derived-under-assumptions`

For financial criticality, boundaries determine cascades. A value-at-risk rule, a margin threshold, a redemption gate, or a covenant can transform a continuous price movement into a discrete institutional event. Criticality may therefore appear as a topological change in admissible action, not only as a smooth loss of stability. The WKB or path-action language must be adapted to such hybrid systems, where continuous market variables meet discrete legal triggers. `open-hypothesis`

For behavioural economics, boundaries include attention and comprehension. An option that is formally available but unnoticed may be outside the agent's effective action set. A contract term that is legal but unread has weak behavioural presence. A default works partly by defining the path of least action. Thus the field model must distinguish formal feasibility from perceived feasibility, and both from normatively acceptable choice architecture. `interpretive`

The boundary-condition emphasis gives economists a concrete way to use the USF grammar without mysticism. Before asking where the field flows, ask what walls, gates, frictions, and discontinuities define the space. Before claiming a phase transition, ask which boundary changed. Before proposing intervention, ask whether it changes preferences, payoffs, information, constraints, or admissible moves. In economics, the boundary is often the theory. `interpretive`
A final boundary is epistemic. Data availability defines what the modeller can see; reporting conventions define what the institution can know; accounting standards define when losses appear; model assumptions define what counts as a state. Economic fields are therefore partly measurement fields. A variable omitted from the measurement system cannot guide intervention, even if it drives the real transition. This is why the book repeatedly asks for operational variables before accepting a criticality claim. `interpretive`
The same epistemic boundary applies to welfare. If unpaid care, informal insurance, climate exposure, mental load, or distributional vulnerability is not measured, a landscape model may optimise around it. Economic modelling has always faced this problem; field language does not solve it. It can, however, make omission visible by asking which variables are allowed into the state vector and which are treated as outside the field. That question should be part of every applied use. `interpretive`
This also disciplines cross-country comparison. A credit market with recourse mortgages, floating rates, and concentrated banks has different boundaries from one with non-recourse mortgages, fixed rates, and securitised funding. A labour market with strong unions, portable benefits, and active policy has different barriers from one without them. The field grammar can compare such systems only after their institutional walls are drawn. `interpretive`
The practical rule is conservative: do not compare landscapes until the axes, walls, shocks, and observables have been named. Once they have been named, disagreement becomes productive, because economists can argue about a specified object rather than about an attractive metaphor. `interpretive`
That conservatism is not retreat; it is how interdisciplinary models earn trust. `interpretive`

# Behaviour, Institutions, and Mechanism Design as Landscape Engineering

Behavioural economics began by showing that real choices systematically depart from the simplest expected-utility and exponential-discounting models. People evaluate outcomes relative to reference points, overweight some losses, display present bias, respond to framing, avoid regret, follow defaults, and use heuristics under attention constraints. A field-theoretic economics should not pretend to have discovered these facts. Its question is whether the facts can be organised dynamically as deformation, memory, and traversal of a valuation landscape. `interpretive`

Consider loss aversion. In prospect theory, the value function is steeper for losses than for gains around a reference point. In a landscape reading, the loss side has a sharper gradient or deeper basin. That translation is faithful only if it preserves the reference dependence and empirical asymmetry of prospect theory. It becomes an `open-hypothesis` when it predicts additional dynamics: for example, that loss aversion should correlate with measured persistence of loss-related physiological or behavioural states, or that interventions changing the reference point reshape the basin. `open-hypothesis`

Framing effects likewise translate naturally but not automatically. A frame changes the description of options, salience of outcomes, social meaning, and perceived reference point. In energy language, it changes the landscape before choice occurs. But framing is not merely an external field term; it can alter beliefs, preferences, attention, and moral categorisation. A precise model must say which state variables the frame modifies. Otherwise the language explains everything after the fact. `open-hypothesis`

Present bias can be read through temporal kernels. If a field's response is weighted toward near-term states, distant payoffs have weaker present influence. Hyperbolic discounting and quasi-hyperbolic $\beta$--$\delta$ models already describe such behaviour. The Soma-field contribution would be to link discounting to memory kernels, arousal, or attention dynamics. Again the burden is empirical: does the kernel predict observed reversals better than standard models? `open-hypothesis`

Status quo bias is perhaps the cleanest landscape case. Remaining where one is requires no barrier crossing; changing options requires effort, uncertainty, identity revision, transaction costs, and sometimes social permission. Defaults work because they place the agent in an attractor. Behavioural public policy already uses defaults in pensions, organ donation, privacy settings, and consumer choice. A field model can add vocabulary about basin depth and barrier height, but it must still confront welfare and consent. Stable defaults can help or exploit. `interpretive`

Mental accounting, endowment effects, ambiguity aversion, and sunk-cost effects can also be represented as landscape features. Accounts partition the state space; ownership deepens a basin around the endowed good; ambiguity thickens perceived barriers; sunk costs attach memory kernels to past investment. These translations are conceptually neat. Their value depends on whether they yield new predictions or better unification, not merely new metaphors. `open-hypothesis`

Institutional economics enters because individual landscapes are embedded in rules. A market institution defines admissible actions, information timing, enforcement, settlement, liability, and dispute resolution. These are boundary conditions. Changing them changes the energy landscape more reliably than exhorting agents to choose differently. Mechanism design is therefore the most natural economics home for [T]-Theory: it already asks how rules shape equilibria. `interpretive`

Take auctions. The mechanism determines incentives for truthful revelation, shading, collusion, entry, and winner's curse. A landscape reading would examine not only equilibrium bids but the slopes around truthful strategies, the barriers to collusion, and the basins created by information release. In a Vickrey auction, truth-telling is weakly dominant under ideal assumptions; in real auctions, budget constraints, common values, risk aversion, and repeated interaction modify the landscape. The field model would have to include those modifications. `derived-under-assumptions`

Take matching markets. Stability in the Gale--Shapley sense is not energy minimisation in the Hopfield sense by default. It is absence of blocking pairs under preferences. Yet the algorithm creates a path through a combinatorial landscape toward a stable matching. Some matching problems may admit potential-like representations; others involve priorities, strategy-proofness, and distributional constraints that resist scalar energy. The correct move is not to force a Hopfield model, but to ask whether a potential exists for the chosen institutional problem. `interpretive`

Take public-goods games. Voluntary contribution often has a social optimum above the Nash equilibrium. Institutions such as matching grants, punishment, communication, assurance contracts, and repeated interaction change incentives. In landscape terms they deepen the cooperative basin or lower the path into it. But each intervention has costs and distributive effects. A high barrier against defection may be authoritarian; a low barrier may be fragile. Mechanism design cannot be reduced to basin engineering without normative theory. `interpretive`

Take platform markets. Algorithms shape attention, prices, reputation, search order, and matching. They are active landscape operators. Recommender systems can create lock-in; surge pricing can shift supply basins; reputation scores can stabilise trust or entrench exclusion. A [T]-Theory economics of platforms would study operator design: how local algorithmic rules create global attractors in buyer--seller behaviour. This is a promising domain because platforms often have data, interventions, and feedback loops. `open-hypothesis`

The O($N^2$) coordination theme enters institutional design as a warning about communication architecture. Many institutions rely on local message passing: firms infer demand from prices; banks infer risk from counterparties; voters infer viability from polls; regulators infer stress from reports. A public signal can coordinate faster, but may also create common-mode failure. Dense connectivity can improve information and increase contagion. The goal is not maximum coupling; it is robust coupling with controlled failure modes. `interpretive`

This is where the spectral-gap metaphor becomes economically interesting. In networks, gaps can measure connectivity, mixing, or robustness depending on the matrix. In institutions, a very small gap may mean fragmentation: groups do not communicate, coordination is slow, arbitrage fails. A very large common mode may mean fragility: everyone moves together. The economically desirable region may be intermediate, with enough connectivity for learning but enough modularity to prevent cascades. That claim requires a specified network model. `open-hypothesis`

Behavioural welfare must be kept separate. If agents have constructed, context-dependent, or internally conflicted preferences, then "making the desired attractor stable" begs the question: desired by whom, under which frame, and with what authority? Behavioural economics has long wrestled with libertarian paternalism, preference purification, autonomy, and manipulation. [@thaler2008nudge] A field model can describe how a nudge changes a basin; it cannot alone justify the nudge. `interpretive`

The same applies to regulation. If a central bank, government, or platform has enough power to reshape landscapes, it may stabilise panic or entrench incumbents. Minimum intervention is not necessarily legitimate intervention. Distributional incidence matters. Democratic accountability matters. Legal authority matters. A purely energetic view can become technocratic if not constrained by political economy and law. The adjacent [T]-Theory law and PPE volumes carry that normative burden; this economics volume notes it rather than solving it. `interpretive`

There is an important positive possibility. Landscape modelling may help distinguish interventions that change incentives from interventions that only add noise. A policy announcement that is not credible may perturb expectations without changing payoff gradients. A subsidy too small to alter adoption thresholds may dissipate. A guarantee that changes the perceived payoff discontinuously may shift the system at low fiscal cost, if credible. These are familiar policy ideas; the field language organises them through barrier crossing and source terms. `open-hypothesis`

Experimental economics is the natural test bed. Laboratory games can vary payoff matrices, communication, repetition, default framing, and institutional rules while measuring transition paths. One can compare games with and without exact potentials. One can induce shocks and observe whether basin estimates predict equilibrium switching. One can test whether physiological arousal measures add predictive power for behavioural transitions, without making clinical claims. Such experiments would either strengthen or falsify the field account. `open-hypothesis`

Agent-based modelling is the second test bed. Artificial agents with known update rules can operate in known landscapes, allowing precise comparison of stochastic stability, transition rates, and intervention thresholds. But agent-based success is not empirical validation unless calibrated to human or market data. It is simulation evidence. The programme's QUANT-EXP-1 status is a model: strong within its statevector simulation, bounded in interpretation. Economics should use the same discipline. `simulated`

A third test bed is historical institutional change. Currency unions, market-design reforms, disclosure changes, trading halts, bankruptcy reforms, deposit insurance, and platform-rule changes are natural experiments in landscape deformation. They are messy and endogenous, but they provide real institutional variation. A [T]-Theory economist would pre-register which variables represent the landscape, which intervention is the source term, and which transition counts as success or failure. `open-hypothesis`

The behavioural chapter's conclusion is therefore neither reductionist nor dismissive. Behavioural economics remains the empirical source of many choice anomalies. Mechanism design remains the mathematical source of incentive-compatible institutions. The Soma-field language can connect them dynamically: behavioural regularities shape individual basins; institutions shape collective landscapes; mechanisms alter barriers; stochastic adjustment selects paths. Where a potential exists, the mathematics can be exact. Elsewhere, the language is a disciplined analogy awaiting tests. `interpretive`


## Mechanism Design: Five Landscape Questions

A mechanism-design translation should ask five questions before drawing any energy diagram. First, what behaviour is intended to be stable? Truthful reporting, efficient matching, cooperation, liquidity provision, entry, innovation, or restraint are different target basins. Second, what deviations are locally profitable? The neighbourhood relation might be unilateral misreporting, bilateral blocking, coalition deviation, withdrawal, collusion, or manipulation of an algorithm. Third, what makes the target basin robust? It may be dominant strategy incentives, repeated-game punishment, reputation, verification, collateral, or legal sanction. Fourth, what undesirable basins remain? Fifth, who bears the cost of making the desired basin deep? `interpretive`

Truthfulness illustrates the point. A direct-revelation mechanism may make truthful reporting dominant under formal assumptions. In field terms, every unilateral misreport slopes back toward truth. But real mechanisms can add privacy costs, complexity costs, mistrust, fear of future use, or strategic ambiguity. These add hidden dimensions to the landscape. A mechanism that is incentive compatible in a narrow model may have a shallow truthful basin in practice because agents do not understand or trust it. `open-hypothesis`

Collusion is the opposite problem. An auction or procurement system may have an efficient competitive equilibrium but also a collusive basin. Repeated interaction, communication channels, market concentration, and weak enforcement deepen that basin. Antitrust policy then changes the landscape by raising the expected cost of collusion, disrupting communication, or changing market structure. A field model could help compare whether enforcement changes the local gradient or merely adds noise around a still-deep collusive basin. `open-hypothesis`

Information design fits naturally. A sender chooses what signal structure receivers observe. A public signal can coordinate everyone on a good equilibrium or everyone on a panic. A private signal can preserve diversity but slow coordination. A stress-test publication can reassure if credible or frighten if interpreted as hidden bad news. The signal is a propagator: it maps a source into many belief updates. Its welfare effect depends on the strategic landscape into which it is released. `interpretive`

Market microstructure is a further case. Tick size, maker-taker fees, transparency rules, speed bumps, batch auctions, dark pools, and circuit breakers all reshape local incentives. Some reduce adverse selection; some alter queue position games; some stabilise liquidity; some create new gaming margins. A landscape account would represent liquidity supply as an attractor supported by inventory limits, information risk, and fee structure. It would test whether rule changes deepen the liquidity basin or merely relocate fragility. `open-hypothesis`

Platform governance gives a modern example. Search ranking, recommendation, reputation, content moderation, dispute resolution, and pricing algorithms jointly define the terrain on which users and sellers act. Bad equilibria include spam, fraud, harassment, low-quality content, lock-in, and race-to-the-bottom pricing. Good equilibria include trust, quality, discovery, and fair entry. Platform operators already manipulate landscapes; the question is whether the manipulation is accountable, welfare-enhancing, and robust. `interpretive`

Public economics adds fiscal and distributive constraints. A subsidy can move adoption across a threshold, but it may also transfer surplus to inframarginal adopters. A tax can raise a barrier against harmful behaviour, but it can be regressive. A guarantee can stop a run, but it can subsidise risk-taking. A minimum-intervention principle cannot be purely energetic; it must include incidence, legitimacy, and second-round incentives. `interpretive`

In each case, the model can fail usefully. If measured behaviour does not cluster near predicted basins, the state variables are wrong. If shocks do not propagate through the proposed kernel, the coupling matrix is wrong. If interventions above the supposed barrier fail, the barrier estimate or action model is wrong. If a non-potential game is forced into a potential, negative controls will expose it. A good [T]-Theory economics paper should welcome such failure modes because they make the claim scientific. `open-hypothesis`
## From Laboratory Games to Institutional Data

The cleanest behavioural test would combine experimental economics with physiological and temporal measurement while keeping the claim modest. Participants could play a known potential game, a near-potential game, and a cyclic non-potential game. The experimenter would vary noise, framing, communication, and repetition. The model would predict selection in the potential game from basin depth and barrier height, weaker prediction in the near-potential game, and failure in the cyclic game. Physiological measures would be exploratory covariates, not clinical evidence. `open-hypothesis`

A second test could examine defaults. If status quo bias is basin depth, then changing transaction cost, salience, ownership duration, social endorsement, and reversibility should change estimated barrier height in predictable ways. The study would compare a standard discrete-choice or prospect-theory model with a dynamic landscape model. The field account earns value only if it predicts switching paths or hysteresis better than static models. `open-hypothesis`

A third test could examine repeated public-goods games. Communication, punishment, matching contributions, and institutional enforcement should reshape the cooperative basin. The model could estimate whether cooperation collapses after shocks depending on barrier height and memory. The negative control is a game where incentives are deliberately non-potential or where communication creates cycles. Again, the prediction must be specified before looking at the data. `open-hypothesis`

Institutional data can then extend laboratory results. Pension default changes, platform rule changes, market-design reforms, and disclosure shifts produce before-and-after transitions. They are not randomised cleanly, but they show whether basin language helps explain persistence, overshoot, reversal, and heterogeneous response. The method should combine structural modelling, causal inference, and the evidence labels used throughout this programme. `interpretive`
## Welfare, Robustness, and the Bad Attractor Problem

The bad attractor problem is central to economics. A discriminatory convention, a low-trust equilibrium, a cartel, a poverty trap, a bank-run equilibrium, or a corrupt procurement network can be stable because each participant's local incentives reinforce it. Stability in such cases is the problem, not the solution. A field model is useful if it explains persistence and identifies leverage points; it is dangerous if it treats deep basins as healthy basins. `interpretive`

Welfare analysis asks who gains, who loses, and under which social welfare criterion. A landscape can include a welfare function, but the welfare function is not given by the dynamics. Potential functions sometimes align with welfare, but often they do not. A congestion-game potential, for instance, need not equal total surplus. A Nash equilibrium may be locally stable and socially inefficient. A regulatory intervention may move the system to a more stable basin while harming a vulnerable group. The model must keep descriptive and normative objects separate. `derived-under-assumptions`

Robustness is also multi-valued. A system can be robust to small shocks because it is rigid, or robust because it is adaptive. It can be robust for incumbents and fragile for entrants. It can preserve liquidity by socialising risk, or preserve discipline by allowing failures. It can prevent panic while creating moral hazard. A spectral gap or barrier height captures one dimension of robustness; political economy decides whether that robustness is desirable. `interpretive`

This suggests an economics-specific extension of the evidence ledger. Each intervention claim should state not only its label but its welfare criterion, distributional incidence, strategic response, institutional authority, and failure mode. For example: "a guarantee lowers run probability" is a stability claim; "the guarantee is welfare-improving" is a welfare claim; "the authority should issue it" is a legal-political claim. They require different evidence. `open-hypothesis`

The same separation protects behavioural applications. A default can increase savings, but whether that is good depends on liquidity needs, debt, income volatility, and autonomy. A frame can move choices, but whether it informs or manipulates depends on transparency and purpose. A platform can deepen a trust basin, but also deepen dependency. The field language can model movement; economics and ethics must judge direction. `interpretive`

For game theorists, the bad attractor problem connects to equilibrium refinement and implementation. We do not merely want any equilibrium. We want equilibria that survive credible deviations, information perturbations, coalition threats, and welfare scrutiny. A Hopfield minimum is a local notion; economic desirability often requires global and normative checks. Future [T]-Theory economics should therefore pair every attractor statement with an equilibrium-refinement and welfare statement. `open-hypothesis`

# Reading Nash Equilibria as Hopfield Energy Minima

The game-theory anchor paper is the volume's centre, and it needs the most careful reading. Its thesis is powerful: strategic equilibria can be understood as attractors in an energy landscape. # Conclusion: Economic Criticality Without Metaphysical Inflation

The repaired thesis of this volume is stronger than the generated slogan because it is narrower. Nash equilibrium is not always a Hopfield minimum. In exact potential games, pure Nash equilibria are local optima of a scalar potential, and with $H=-\Phi$ they become local energy minima. In symmetric Hopfield-style interaction games, asynchronous best-response dynamics can be read as energy descent. In general games, especially asymmetric, cyclic, zero-sum, and signalling games, the Hopfield mapping is at most partial unless a potential component is isolated. That is the central mathematical boundary. `derived-under-assumptions`

Within that boundary, [T]-Theory offers economists a useful grammar. Equilibrium selection becomes basin selection. Risk dominance becomes a question of barriers and basin volume. Institutional design becomes landscape deformation. Behavioural anomalies become possible features of dynamic valuation landscapes. Financial fragility becomes a hypothesis about susceptibility, coupling, and loss of resilience. Regulatory action becomes a source term whose effect depends on the current landscape. These are not replacements for economics; they are translations into a dynamical language. `interpretive`

The programme's greatest danger in economics is metaphysical inflation. Markets do not become conscious because a field model uses the word somatic. A price system is not a nervous system because both propagate signals. A crash is not a thermodynamic phase transition because it is abrupt. A policy is not justified because it lowers an energy barrier. The evidence labels exist to prevent precisely those slides. Use them. `interpretive`

The strongest immediate research path is theorem-first. Formalise the Nash--Hopfield claim for finite exact potential games. State the profile neighbourhood. Prove local equivalence. Extend to weighted and ordinal potential games with appropriate weakening. Give counterexamples: matching pennies, rock--paper--scissors, and games with antisymmetric payoff components. Then connect stochastic best-response dynamics to Gibbs-like stationary distributions where standard assumptions allow. This would turn the anchor slogan into a mathematical contribution. `open-hypothesis`

The second path is experiment-first. Use laboratory games with known potential landscapes. Estimate individual and group transition paths under controlled noise, framing, communication, repetition, and institutional modifications. Test whether barrier height, basin size, and spectral features predict equilibrium selection beyond standard learning models. Include non-potential games as negative controls. If the field account predicts where it should and fails where it should not apply, it gains credibility. `open-hypothesis`

The third path is market-data-first. Define a state vector for a specific market: not "the market" in general, but a particular limit-order book, funding network, clearing network, or asset class. Estimate couplings. Pre-register criticality indicators. Compare them against standard risk models out of sample. Ask whether susceptibility, recovery time, or spectral concentration adds predictive power for liquidity stress or regime shift. Avoid trading advice. The unit of success is scientific prediction or institutional risk measurement, not portfolio return. `open-hypothesis`

The fourth path is mechanism-first. Treat auctions, platforms, matching systems, public-goods mechanisms, and regulatory rules as landscape operators. Ask how rule changes alter basins of truthful reporting, cooperation, collusion, entry, exclusion, or panic. Connect the field vocabulary to welfare economics and political economy rather than bypassing them. A robust mechanism should be stable against shocks, but not every stable mechanism is just. `interpretive`

Behavioural economics remains indispensable. It supplies the empirical catalogue of reference dependence, loss aversion, framing, present bias, status quo bias, and bounded attention. The field account may unify some of these as dynamic landscape features, but it must earn that unification. A new vocabulary is not progress unless it predicts, compresses, or clarifies more than the old vocabulary. `interpretive`

Econophysics also remains both ally and warning. It shows that physical mathematics can illuminate markets; it also shows how easily analogy can outrun identification. The correct standard is not disciplinary purity. It is assumption discipline. If the operator matches, the theorem may travel. If only the imagery matches, the claim stays interpretive. If a model makes a prediction, the prediction must face data. `interpretive`

Nothing in this volume instructs a reader to buy, sell, hold, hedge, lever, delever, lobby, regulate, deregulate, or redesign an institution. The book is a conceptual and mathematical research text. It aims to make economic dynamics more explicit by showing where attractor theory, potential games, Hopfield energy, Green's functions, and critical phenomena can be used responsibly. `interpretive`

The final sentence of the generated conclusion was: "The equilibrium is a Hopfield minimum. The dynamics are physical. The policy implications are derivable." The hardened version is less dramatic and more useful: in exact potential games, pure equilibria can be represented as local energy minima; in specified stochastic landscapes, dynamics can be modelled physically; in empirically calibrated institutional systems, some intervention thresholds may be derived under assumptions. The landscape is not the whole theory. It is the part of the theory that tells us how equilibrium is reached, missed, escaped, or redesigned. `derived-under-assumptions`


## What Would Make the Programme Fail?

The economics programme should be easy to criticise, because unclear failure is the sign of a metaphor rather than a model. The Nash--Hopfield claim fails if its theorem cannot be stated without smuggling in assumptions that exclude the economic cases it wants to explain. It also fails if negative-control games with cyclic incentives are misclassified as energy systems. Exactness requires the courage to say no. `open-hypothesis`

The market-criticality claim fails if its indicators do not outperform simpler risk measures, if the proposed order parameter changes after every crisis, if apparent precursors are visible only retrospectively, or if reflexive agent response destroys the signal. It also fails if the model cannot distinguish a crash, a repricing, a liquidity event, a solvency event, and a policy-induced transition. Abruptness alone is not criticality. `open-hypothesis`

The behavioural-landscape claim fails if it merely renames prospect theory, default effects, or present bias without adding prediction. It also fails if physiological or memory-kernel variables do not improve models after accounting for known behavioural covariates. A successful translation should compress several phenomena under one dynamic mechanism or forecast when standard effects strengthen, weaken, or reverse. `open-hypothesis`

The mechanism-design claim fails if landscape language ignores incentive compatibility, information, participation constraints, coalition deviations, and distribution. A mechanism is not good because it creates a deep basin; it is good, if at all, because the basin implements a normatively defensible objective under legitimate constraints. Economics cannot outsource value to topology. `interpretive`

The programme as a whole fails in economics if it cannot produce at least one narrow, independently checkable result: a theorem about a class of games, a laboratory equilibrium-selection prediction, a market-fragility indicator, or a mechanism-design robustness metric. Broad claims about fields are not enough. The proper unit of progress is a claim that could have been false and then survives a serious test. `open-hypothesis`

This failure list is not defensive. It is constructive. It tells future authors where to work: formalise exact potential-game equivalence; build negative controls; estimate landscapes in small controlled settings before claiming markets; compare against standard baselines; keep welfare and legitimacy visible; and preserve labels in every public-facing statement. If those constraints make the theory less dramatic, they also make it more useful. `interpretive`
## A Validation Ladder for Economic [T]-Theory

A careful validation ladder would have five rungs. Rung one is formal: prove the exact potential-game correspondence and publish counterexamples. Rung two is computational: simulate known landscapes and show that the proposed estimators recover basin structure, barriers, and transition probabilities. Rung three is laboratory: test equilibrium selection and switching in controlled games. Rung four is institutional: apply the model to observed rule changes or market microstructure with pre-registered predictions. Rung five is comparative: show that the field model outperforms established economic baselines in a domain where the extra complexity is justified. `open-hypothesis`

No rung can be skipped by rhetoric. A simulation does not replace a theorem; a theorem does not replace measurement; a suggestive historical fit does not replace out-of-sample prediction. This mirrors the programme's wider discipline. Lean checks syntax and proof, not economic meaning. QUANT-EXP-1 checks reachability in a small model, not markets. A book chapter can propose a bridge, but a research programme has to cross it one plank at a time. `interpretive`

The ladder also clarifies publication claims. A paper on potential games may legitimately claim a derived mathematical result. A paper on laboratory games may claim an empirical result if the experiment is run and documented. A paper on financial criticality may claim only an open hypothesis until its indicators survive out-of-sample tests. A policy-facing note must state uncertainty and avoid advice. This volume's evidence ledger is therefore not an appendix; it is the proposed house style for future economic work. `interpretive`

If the programme succeeds, it will not be because it declared economics to be physics. It will be because it found exact islands, built bridges from them, marked the waters where analogy begins, and produced tests that economists who do not share the wider metaphysics can still run. That is the right standard for a domain book addressed to economists and game theorists. `interpretive`

# [T]-Theory Cheatsheet

[T]-Theory names a programme in which field dynamics, energy landscapes, propagators, and typed formal proofs are used to model emotional, social, and physical systems under explicit evidence labels. For economics, the safe cheat sheet is: exact potential games can be written as energy landscapes; general games cannot be assumed to do so; markets may show critical-like dynamics only when measured variables support that model; behavioural and institutional effects can be read as landscape deformations when this adds testable content; no market or policy recommendation follows from the formalism alone.
