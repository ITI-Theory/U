# The Green Propagator

**G-ID:** *The Cortical EMF Propagator — macroscopic field from synchronised neural firing, `open-hypothesis`.* In this volume the Green propagator is read at neural scale: a perturbation in one part of the organism affects distributed neural and bodily state through electromagnetic, ephaptic, autonomic, and mechanical pathways. The literal Green's function equation, $(\nabla^2+k^2)G(x,x')=\delta(x-x')$, is a mathematical template for response. Its use in neuroscience is not a claim that EEG, MEG, fascia, and subjective experience are already one measured object; it is a disciplined way to ask which response kernels can be estimated from data and which remain speculative `interpretive`.

The zoom note is therefore narrow. This book works mainly at $\sigma=6$ and $\sigma=7$: local neural circuits, oscillatory assemblies, limbic-cortical loops, and whole-brain electromagnetic fields. It occasionally descends to tissue-scale ephaptic and interoceptive coupling, and it occasionally rises to organism-scale regulation, but it does not ask neuroscientists to accept cosmology in order to test the neural claims `open-hypothesis`.

# Introduction: The Electromagnetic Nervous System

Neuroscience already has field theories. They are not always called that. Local field potentials, current-source density analysis, volume conduction, evoked fields, MEG inverse solutions, dynamical systems models of attractor basins, and neural-field equations are all ways of saying that the nervous system is not exhausted by single-neuron events. A spike is a discrete event; a membrane potential is a local state; a synapse is a contact; but the experimental record keeps returning to collective variables. Oscillatory phase, coherence, gain, arousal, autonomic tone, and interoceptive precision are not properties of one neuron alone. They belong to populations, loops, tissues, and bodies. Soma Field Theory begins from that ordinary neuroscientific fact and asks whether the missing explanatory object is a field variable coupling body and brain `interpretive`.

The proposal is deliberately less dramatic than the older book wrapper suggested. It is not that current instruments are useless, nor that experience floats outside neural tissue, nor that a hidden biofield has already been measured. It is that contemporary methods measure projections of a larger dynamical system. EEG and MEG provide macroscopic access to postsynaptic current flow and synchronised neural activity, with all the familiar difficulties of source reconstruction, volume conduction, reference dependence, field spread, and artefact. fMRI measures haemodynamic consequences with high spatial but slow temporal resolution. Intracranial recordings and local field potentials give sharper local access but limited coverage. These methods do not directly report first-person affect; they report physical variables from which affect is inferred. The inference may be strong, clinically useful, and experimentally reproducible, but it remains an inference `interpretive`.

That distinction matters because the programme's strongest neuroscience claims concern the coupling between measurable field variables and affective state. McFadden's Conscious Electromagnetic Information theory proposes that synchronised neural firing generates an endogenous electromagnetic field that integrates information and exerts causal influence on neural firing [@mcfadden2002a; @mcfadden2002b]. CEMI is controversial, but it is a real position in the consciousness literature, not a slogan. Its minimal neuroscientific burden is twofold: first, that the brain's endogenous EM field carries integrated information about neural state; second, that field effects can make a causal difference to future neural dynamics. The first burden is close to standard electrophysiology. The second is where ephaptic coupling and field effects become decisive. There is evidence that extracellular fields can bias neural excitability, spike timing, and synchronisation in some preparations; the open question is whether such effects are strong, structured, and behaviourally relevant enough in the intact human brain to support a theory of conscious affect [@anastassiou2011ephaptic; @jefferys1995nonsynaptic].

Soma Field Theory accepts CEMI as an important bridge, but it should not be presented as a completed reduction. The safe claim is that the cortical electromagnetic field is a plausible neural projection of the somatic field, and that CEMI supplies a testable constraint on any field-theoretic account of consciousness `open-hypothesis`. The unsafe claim would be that the USF has already been derived to Maxwell's equations in a way that compels neuroscientific assent. In this book the CEMI relation is therefore stated as a working identification under assumptions: if a somatic field has a neural electromagnetic component, then that component must be compatible with what EEG, MEG, intracranial recordings, and ephaptic experiments already show `derived-under-assumptions`.

The other major bridge is the Hopfield tradition. Hopfield's 1982 network showed how a recurrent system with symmetric weights can store patterns as attractor minima in an energy landscape [@hopfield1982]. Modern Hopfield networks generalise the energy and retrieval mechanism, linking attractor memory to attention-like operations and exponentially larger storage regimes [@ramsauer2020; @ramsauer2020hopfield]. Neuroscientists need not believe the brain is literally a Hopfield network to find the language useful. Attractor dynamics are already used in models of working memory, decision-making, olfaction, navigation, perceptual multistability, seizure transitions, and cortical state changes. The energy landscape is a model class: it describes how a high-dimensional system tends towards stable patterns and how noise, drive, or perturbation can move it between them `interpretive`.

The Field-Modulated Hopfield Network, or FM-HN, is the book's central open extension. The classical Hopfield picture has attractor basins but no living body. A fixed inverse temperature $\beta$ determines how sharply the system retrieves a pattern; a fixed weight matrix $W$ determines the landscape. Biological organisms, however, do not operate with fixed arousal, fixed gain, fixed autonomic tone, fixed interoceptive precision, or fixed limbic bias. Threat, exhaustion, inflammation, pain, attachment context, and pharmacology alter the effective dynamics. FM-HN encodes this by allowing a limbic or somatic field variable $\Phi(t)$ to modulate temperature and weights: $T(t)=T_0+\sigma\Phi(t)$, $\beta(t)=1/T(t)$, and $W(t)=W_0+\gamma\Phi(t)J$ in the simplified presentation. Under calm or zero-field conditions, the formal equations collapse back to a standard Hopfield limit; under stress, the field changes retrieval sharpness and basin geometry `derived-under-assumptions`.

That is a useful hypothesis, not a finished neuroscience. The theorem `LimbicHopfield.correspondence_principle` checks the zero-field correspondence limit for the encoded definitions `kernel-verified`. It does not prove that a limbic field exists, that $\Phi(t)$ has the proposed biological meaning, or that human clinical states are correctly classified by these parameters. Several analytical limits and empirical mappings remain open. In this book, FM-HN is therefore treated as a modelling proposal with a clear falsification programme. If limbic-autonomic state truly modulates effective attractor dynamics, then perturbation experiments should show state-dependent changes in entrainment width, transition probability, dwell time, and recovery after perturbation. If no such state-dependent modulation appears after adequate measurement and controls, the model fails `open-hypothesis`.

The most concrete proposed measurement is Arnold tongue width. In nonlinear dynamics, coupled oscillators lock over a region of frequency and coupling space. In neuroscience, entrainment paradigms already measure frequency-following responses, steady-state evoked potentials, auditory and visual tagging, phase-locking, and sensorimotor synchronisation. The programme proposes that the width of the locking region between a stimulus and neural-somatic oscillation can function as an operational analogue of attentional bandwidth. Too narrow a tongue may correspond to rigid, brittle, or hyperselective coupling; too broad a tongue may correspond to flooding, distractibility, or unstable capture. This is not a diagnostic claim about autism, ADHD, trauma, or anxiety. It is a testable dynamical marker that might, if validated, supplement existing physiology `open-hypothesis`.

EEG and MEG coherence are relevant but must be handled with care. Coherence can mean phase consistency, amplitude co-variation, or spectral dependence between channels or sources. Sensor-level coherence can be inflated by common reference, volume conduction, muscle artefact, filtering, and source leakage. Source-level MEG reduces some problems but introduces inverse-model assumptions. Functional connectivity is not automatically causal connectivity. For this reason, the field programme should prefer preregistered source-space measures, imaginary coherency or phase-lag metrics where appropriate, perturbational designs, and multimodal convergence rather than unqualified claims that high coherence equals communication [@nolte2004identifying; @fries2005mechanism].

The limbic-cortical interaction at stake is also familiar. The amygdala, hippocampal formation, hypothalamus, periaqueductal grey, insula, anterior cingulate cortex, ventromedial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices, basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem autonomic nuclei form recurrent loops. These loops regulate threat detection, salience, memory, bodily state, action readiness, and reportable feeling. They are not a single limbic switch; the limbic system itself is a historical and contested construct. The programme's phrase "missing limbic layer" should therefore be read as a modelling claim: existing Hopfield and modern attention models omit an explicit state variable for bodily affective modulation, not as a literal claim that anatomical neuroscience forgot the limbic system `interpretive`.

The developmental chapters add a second caution. The pre-verbal manifold paper uses a single author case to ask how early bodily events might structure later affective and interoceptive dynamics. For neuroscientists, the relevant issue is not whether one life history can establish a new nosology. It cannot. The useful proposal is methodological: early development may configure coupling parameters before autobiographical memory and language are available, and later diagnostic categories may be projections of deeper developmental dynamics `open-hypothesis`. This belongs beside work on sensitive periods, attachment, pain imprinting, interoception, autonomic regulation, and right-brain affective development, not above it. The N=1 material is testimony and hypothesis, not general evidence.

Clinical caution follows. The book does not diagnose, treat, or advise. It does not claim that a person with trauma, autism, ADHD, depression, anxiety, alexithymia, pain, dissociation, or any other condition has a particular field geometry. It proposes modelling variables that could be tested in cohorts: attractor depth, transition barriers, field temperature, entrainment width, interoceptive precision, autonomic recovery, and limbic-cortical coupling. If these variables prove useful, they may one day inform research stratification or mechanistic understanding. They are not presently validated clinical instruments `open-hypothesis`.

What, then, does this volume offer neuroscientists? First, it offers a way to connect CEMI, ephaptic coupling, field measurement, and coherence without collapsing them into one claim. Second, it offers a Hopfield-attractor language for affect that explicitly includes body-state modulation. Third, it offers a developmental hypothesis about pre-verbal structure that can be tested rather than merely narrated. Fourth, it offers an evidence discipline: Lean proofs for definitions, simulations for model reachability, empirical neuroscience for measurement, and open labels where the bridge is not yet built. The value of the programme is not certainty. It is the possibility of asking a sharper question: what physical variables would have to be measured for felt affect to become a field-theoretic object in neuroscience `interpretive`?

# Electromagnetic Integration and Neural Field Measurement

A neuroscientist coming to [T]-Theory should begin with what is already secure. Brains generate electromagnetic fields. Every action potential, synaptic current, dendritic integration event, and transmembrane ion flow contributes to extracellular potentials. EEG measures voltage differences at the scalp, mostly reflecting synchronised postsynaptic currents in aligned cortical pyramidal cells. MEG measures the magnetic fields generated by intracellular currents, with different sensitivity to source orientation and somewhat less distortion by skull and scalp. Local field potentials measure nearby population activity. None of this is speculative. The question is not whether neural tissue has field properties; it is whether those fields are epiphenomenal summaries, weak causal contributors, or central integrative variables `interpretive`.

The conservative view is that fields are read-outs. Neurons communicate primarily through synapses and gap junctions; extracellular fields are by-products whose causal role is usually small compared with chemical and electrical synaptic transmission. This view is often appropriate. Synaptic specificity, plasticity, neurotransmitter systems, dendritic computation, and circuit architecture explain a vast amount. A field theory of neural dynamics must not ignore these mechanisms. The programme's claim is more limited: where synchrony, phase, coherence, and weak global bias matter, the field may be part of the computation rather than merely an observation channel `open-hypothesis`.

Ephaptic coupling is the key boundary case. In ephaptic interaction, an extracellular electric field generated by one population influences nearby membranes without a conventional synapse. Such effects are well known in peripheral nerve bundles, cardiac tissue, tightly packed axons, and some cortical or hippocampal preparations. They are often small, local, geometry-dependent, and frequency-dependent. That makes them scientifically interesting precisely because they are not magical. They supply a physical route by which a field can alter thresholds, spike timing, phase alignment, and excitability. The open question is scale: can such effects, embedded in real cortical geometry and noise, alter cognition or affect in a measurable way `open-hypothesis`?

CEMI answers yes in a strong form. McFadden's theory argues that conscious information is instantiated in the brain's endogenous electromagnetic field, generated by synchronised firing and capable of feeding back into neuronal firing [@mcfadden2002a; @mcfadden2002b]. The theory is attractive to [T]-Theory because it gives the field a causal role and places synchrony at the centre of integration. It is also vulnerable. Critics can ask whether field strength is sufficient, whether the spatial grain is correct, whether causal effects survive realistic noise, whether non-conscious neural fields have the same properties, and whether CEMI explains reportability, unity, and content better than global workspace, recurrent processing, integrated information, predictive processing, or dynamical-systems accounts.

A careful [T]-Theory neuroscience should welcome those objections. They become experimental design requirements. If the cortical EMF propagator is a real bridge, then endogenous field structure should predict more than local firing rates or anatomical connectivity alone. It should predict state transitions, entrainment ranges, perturbation recovery, and cross-frequency coupling in ways that survive controls for volume conduction and common drive. If transcranial alternating current stimulation, sensory entrainment, or intracranial stimulation changes phase relationships, a field model should predict when such changes do and do not alter affective or attentional state. If it cannot, it is decorative `open-hypothesis`.

EEG and MEG coherence offer a first pass but not a final answer. Coherence is tempting because it seems to show distributed integration: two regions oscillate together, therefore they communicate. Neuroscientists know why that inference is too quick. Signals recorded at two sensors may share a source. A common driver may synchronise both without direct interaction. The reference electrode can introduce shared variance. Source leakage can create false connectivity. Filtering and epoch selection can bias phase estimates. For the field programme, these caveats are not inconveniences; they are the difference between science and pattern reading. A usable field theory must specify what kind of coherence matters, at which frequency, in which source space, under which behavioural task, and with which controls `derived-under-assumptions`.

The programme's preferred observable is therefore not raw coherence but a response function. In physics, a Green's function is about how a system responds to an impulse. In neuroscience, the analogous object can be estimated by perturbation and response: sensory tagging, rhythmic stimulation, TMS-EEG, intracranial stimulation, closed-loop neurostimulation, optogenetic stimulation in animals, or naturalistic perturbations aligned to physiology. The question becomes: given a controlled input $J(t)$, what is the distributed neural and bodily response $G*J$? Does the response depend on affective state? Does it predict subjective report, autonomic recovery, or behavioural flexibility? Does it change with sleep, stress, medication, inflammation, or learning `open-hypothesis`?

This response-function framing also prevents a common error. The field need not be a separate substance. It can be a macroscopic state variable constructed from ordinary neural and bodily processes. The cortex, thalamus, brainstem, heart, lungs, gut, endocrine system, musculature, fascia, and immune state all contribute to the organism's dynamics, but not all contribute in the same way or on the same timescale. The cortical EMF propagator is the neural electromagnetic projection of that system. It does not replace membrane biophysics; it summarises a coupling regime `interpretive`.

Nor should field language be used to flatten spatial anatomy. The amygdala is not the insula; the anterior cingulate is not the hypothalamus; the hippocampus is not the periaqueductal grey. Field theory is most useful when it respects anatomy as boundary condition, source distribution, and coupling geometry. A propagator in a cortex with folded sheets, anisotropic white-matter tracts, layered laminae, vascular constraints, and skull conductivity is not a propagator in a homogeneous medium. If [T]-Theory is to become neuroscience, it must become anatomically specific `open-hypothesis`.

That specificity begins with timescales. Synaptic events unfold in milliseconds; oscillatory cycles in milliseconds to seconds; autonomic adjustments in seconds to minutes; hormonal and inflammatory processes over minutes to days; developmental plasticity over months to years. A single word such as "field" can hide these differences. The somatic field proposal requires a hierarchy of kernels: fast electromagnetic and ephaptic kernels, slower autonomic and interoceptive kernels, still slower developmental and plasticity kernels. The temporal dynamics paper elsewhere in the programme makes this explicit with memory kernels; here the neuroscientific demand is operationalisation `derived-under-assumptions`.

The CEMI relation becomes more plausible when placed inside this hierarchy. Synchronous dendritic currents can generate measurable fields; fields can bias nearby excitability; phase relationships can organise timing; timing can alter plasticity; plasticity can reshape future attractors. No one step needs to be metaphysically exotic. The open claim is that these steps form an integrated loop sufficient to carry affective field state. That claim can be tested by combining EEG/MEG, autonomic signals, behavioural tasks, and perturbations in the same design `open-hypothesis`.

One candidate design is an affective entrainment protocol. Participants receive rhythmic auditory, visual, tactile, or interoceptive stimuli across a frequency range while EEG/MEG and autonomic data are recorded. Source-space phase locking, cross-frequency coupling, HRV, respiration, skin conductance, pupil size, and report are modelled together. The predicted Arnold tongue is not merely the range in which a cortical signal follows a stimulus; it is the range in which cortical, autonomic, and subjective responses cohere. The model predicts state-dependent narrowing or broadening under stress, fatigue, hypervigilance, dissociation, or focused attention. Failure to find such state dependence would constrain the theory `open-hypothesis`.

A second design uses perturbation recovery. If affective states are attractor basins, then a perturbation should displace the system and recovery should reveal basin geometry. In EEG/MEG, recovery can be measured as return of spectral profile, phase organisation, evoked-response shape, and source-space connectivity. In the body, recovery appears as HRV rebound, respiration normalisation, skin conductance recovery, and movement/posture changes. In report, it appears as return to baseline affect or a transition to a new state. A field model predicts that these recoveries are coupled; a purely local model may not `open-hypothesis`.

A third design is pharmacological or physiological modulation. Caffeine, anxiolytics, beta-blockers, sleep deprivation, breath manipulation, exercise, inflammation, and stress induction alter arousal, autonomic balance, and gain. FM-HN predicts that effective inverse temperature and basin geometry should change with such manipulations. The ethics are non-trivial, especially with vulnerable participants; clinical research must be conservative. But in healthy participants and carefully screened clinical cohorts, state modulation can test whether attractor transitions depend on field temperature as proposed `open-hypothesis`.

The measurement chapter therefore ends with a principle: do not infer the field from a pretty coherence map. Estimate response functions, perturb the system, control artefacts, model anatomy, include bodily physiology, and demand predictions that could fail. If [T]-Theory contributes to neuroscience, it will do so by making field language harder, not easier `interpretive`.

# Attractors, Limbic-Cortical Loops, and the FM-HN Hypothesis

Attractor language is already native to computational neuroscience. Neural systems can settle into stable patterns despite noise; they can switch abruptly between states; they can display hysteresis, metastability, multistability, and critical slowing near transitions. A memory, a percept, a motor plan, a seizure, a mood state, and a threat posture are not the same kind of object, but each can be described as a region of state space with characteristic entry, dwell, and exit dynamics. Hopfield's contribution was to show, in a simple formal system, how stored patterns can be energy minima and how dynamics can converge to them [@hopfield1982]. SFT imports that lesson cautiously `interpretive`.

The caution is essential. Biological nervous systems are not symmetric binary Hopfield networks. They are heterogeneous, spiking, plastic, neuromodulated, embodied, metabolically constrained, and developmentally shaped. Inhibition is structured; dendrites compute; synapses fail; glia matter; oscillations cross scales; learning is ongoing; bodies intervene. If the Hopfield formalism is useful, it is as a coarse-grained energy landscape for recurrent affective dynamics, not as a literal microcircuit diagram `derived-under-assumptions`.

Modern Hopfield networks make the bridge more interesting because they connect attractor retrieval to attention-like operations. Ramsauer and colleagues show that exponential energy formulations can retrieve stored patterns rapidly and with high capacity [@ramsauer2020]. In machine learning, that connection helps relate memory and attention. In neuroscience, it suggests a formal path from stored affective patterns to rapid state selection. The missing biological variable is organismic state. A model that retrieves the same way regardless of pain, threat, safety, sleep, hunger, inflammation, social context, and autonomic tone cannot be a full model of affect `interpretive`.

FM-HN introduces that variable as $\Phi(t)$, a limbic-somatic field amplitude or vector. In the simplest scalar presentation, $\Phi$ raises effective temperature and modulates the weight matrix. High temperature means diffuse, noisy, exploratory, or unstable retrieval. Low temperature means sharp, rigid, deterministic retrieval. Weight modulation changes which attractors are deep, which transitions are easy, and which states are mutually reinforcing. The checked correspondence theorem says that when $\Phi=0$, the encoded FM-HN temperature and weight terms reduce to their baseline Hopfield forms. That is a formal property of the encoded equations `kernel-verified`. The biological claim that $\Phi$ tracks limbic-somatic state remains open `open-hypothesis`.

This structure maps naturally onto limbic-cortical loops. Threat detection and salience processing alter cortical gain. Hippocampal memory contextualises threat and safety. Hypothalamic and brainstem circuits alter autonomic and endocrine state. Insular cortex integrates interoceptive signals. Anterior cingulate cortex participates in conflict, effort, autonomic regulation, and pain. Ventromedial prefrontal cortex contributes valuation and regulation; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex supports task control and reappraisal. The body feeds back through vagal, spinal, humoral, immune, and mechanical pathways. FM-HN compresses this loop into a modulating field variable; that compression is useful only if its lost detail can be recovered experimentally `derived-under-assumptions`.

Affect is especially suited to attractor modelling because affective states have inertia. Fear, shame, grief, calm, curiosity, disgust, anger, awe, and numbness are not momentary labels attached to isolated stimuli. They can persist, self-reinforce, recruit memory, bias perception, tune attention, alter posture, modify autonomic tone, and constrain action. They also have thresholds and transitions. A person can be near tears before crying; near panic before panic; near sleep before sleep; near shutdown before freeze. These ordinary transitions resemble changes in basin occupancy and barrier crossing `interpretive`.

SFT represents this with an affect vector $e(t)$ and an energy $H(e)=-\tfrac12 e^\top W e-b^\top e$. Positive entries of $W$ make modes co-activate; negative entries inhibit; biases tilt the landscape. The Langevin form $\gamma\dot e=-\nabla H(e)+\sqrt{2D}\xi(t)+J(t)$ adds damping, noise, and input. Neuroscientifically, this is a coarse-grained state-space model. The question is whether real data can estimate $e(t)$, $W$, $b$, $D$, $\gamma$, and $J(t)$ from combinations of report, behaviour, EEG/MEG, autonomic signals, endocrine markers, and movement `derived-under-assumptions`.

One virtue of the model is that it distinguishes several mechanisms often blurred in clinical language. A state can persist because its basin is deep; because barriers around it are high; because noise is too low to explore alternatives; because noise is too high to stabilise alternatives; because input repeatedly pushes the system back; because coupling to bodily state maintains it; because regulation fails to reshape the landscape; or because development never built an accessible alternative. These are different mechanisms with different predictions `interpretive`.

For example, a rigid low-temperature attractor should show long dwell times, narrow entrainment, strong return after perturbation, and perhaps reduced flexibility in affective switching. A high-temperature unstable regime should show broad or erratic entrainment, rapid switching, poor dwell stability, and high autonomic volatility. A deep threat basin should show biased perception, rapid threat entry, slow recovery, and physiological mobilisation. A dissociative or freeze-like basin may show low behavioural output, altered interoception, and paradoxical physiological patterns. These are hypotheses, not diagnoses `open-hypothesis`.

The FM-HN paper also applies the model to ADHD, autism, and complex PTSD. For this neuroscience volume, those operator descriptions must be softened. ADHD is not simply "high temperature"; autism is not simply "low temperature"; C-PTSD is not simply "a barrier". Each construct is heterogeneous, behaviourally defined, developmentally complex, and often co-occurring. What the model can responsibly say is that some phenotypes may be usefully described by different gain, precision, sensory-threshold, temperature, and barrier regimes. Whether those regimes align with diagnostic categories is an empirical question `open-hypothesis`.

The same caution applies to "trauma wells". Attractor language can be clinically illuminating because trauma often has recurrence, state-dependence, avoidance, and involuntary re-entry. But a mathematical basin is not a moral identity, and it is not a treatment plan. The programme's clinical papers use wells, barriers, and temperature as modelling devices. Neuroscientists should ask how to operationalise them: dwell-time distributions in affect sampling, physiological recovery curves, startle modulation, threat-generalisation gradients, extinction and reconsolidation metrics, sleep effects, and longitudinal changes. Without such measures, the landscape remains metaphor `open-hypothesis`.

A strong empirical programme would therefore build an attractor model from data. Participants provide repeated affect reports and behavioural measures while continuous physiology is recorded. EEG/MEG tasks estimate entrainment and perturbation response. State-space models infer latent affective coordinates. Energy-landscape methods estimate basin structure and transition rates. Clinical or developmental variables are then tested as predictors of landscape parameters. The programme succeeds only if these inferred parameters predict future transitions better than simpler baselines `open-hypothesis`.

The Arnold tongue proposal can be inserted into this landscape. An attractor system with strong coupling to a stimulus will lock over a wider region; one with weak or selective coupling will lock narrowly; one with unstable internal dynamics may lock inconsistently. The width and shape of the tongue depend on coupling strength, noise, detuning, and nonlinearity. In attention, this may correspond to how flexibly a person can entrain to relevant signals without being captured by irrelevant ones. In social neuroscience, it may correspond to interpersonal synchrony under conversation, music, movement, or joint attention. But again, the marker must be validated against tasks and outcomes `open-hypothesis`.

Limbic-cortical interaction gives the model its biological plausibility. The cortex does not simply represent the body after the fact. Interoceptive prediction, autonomic regulation, salience assignment, and action preparation are continuous loops. Damasio's somatic-marker tradition, interoceptive accounts of self, and affective neuroscience all support the idea that bodily state shapes cognition [@damasio1994; @seth2021]. SFT adds a formal field-and-attractor language, not a replacement for those traditions `interpretive`.

A useful comparison is global workspace theory. Global workspace models emphasise broadcast and reportability; integrated information theory emphasises intrinsic causal structure; predictive processing emphasises hierarchical inference; CEMI emphasises electromagnetic integration; Hopfield models emphasise attractor energy. SFT's neuroscience version tries to combine field integration, attractor state, and bodily modulation. That synthesis is ambitious. Its virtue is that it generates measurements. Its risk is that it names too many things with one vocabulary. The evidence labels are designed to prevent that risk `interpretive`.

Lean contributes at the formal boundary. A named theorem can show that the calm limit of a definition reduces to a standard model; it can show arithmetic facts about thresholds; it can check that a typed ontology has the expected constructors `kernel-verified`. It cannot show that a human nervous system implements the definition. For neuroscientists, this is not a weakness but a clarification. Formalisation tells us exactly what model is being tested `interpretive`.

The FM-HN hypothesis therefore stands or falls as a neuroscientific model. It predicts state-dependent changes in effective temperature, gain, entrainment width, basin depth, transition probability, and perturbation recovery. It predicts that body-state measures improve model fit beyond neural measures alone. It predicts that CEMI-compatible field variables carry information relevant to affective transitions. It predicts that interventions altering arousal or bodily state shift attractor dynamics in lawful ways. These predictions can be wrong. That is why the hypothesis is useful `open-hypothesis`.

# Development, Interoception, and Clinical Caution

The body enters neuroscience first as physiology, then as development. A newborn nervous system is not an adult cortex awaiting content. It is a living organism regulating temperature, breathing, feeding, pain, touch, vestibular input, sleep, proximity, and distress before it can narrate any of these. The pre-verbal manifold paper gives [T]-Theory's most controversial developmental proposal: early bodily events may shape the coupling geometry of later affective life before explicit memory and language are available. The safe version is a hypothesis about sensitive-period configuration of interoceptive, autonomic, affective, and relational systems `open-hypothesis`.

This hypothesis sits near established research without being identical to it. Developmental neuroscience already recognises sensitive periods, attachment effects, stress physiology, pain imprinting, autonomic regulation, some right-hemisphere accounts of affective development, and the long reach of early adversity [@schore2001]. Clinical literatures on trauma and somatic psychotherapy emphasise bodily memory and non-verbal affect [@levine2010; @ogden2006; @vdkolk2014]. Interoceptive neuroscience studies how signals from the body become feelings, self-related processing, and regulation [@craig2003; @seth2021]. SFT's contribution is to ask whether these can be modelled as an attractor manifold with measurable coupling parameters `interpretive`.

The term "pre-verbal manifold" should not be read as a claim that an infant has an adult-like hidden narrative. It names a low-dimensional structure of regulation and response: what sensations are salient, how pain and comfort are weighted, how quickly arousal rises and falls, how touch and proximity are interpreted, how vocalisation and movement recruit caregivers, how sleep and feeding organise state, and how the body predicts safety or danger. Later language can redescribe this structure, but it may not erase it `open-hypothesis`.

Neuroscientifically, this directs attention to longitudinal measures. Neonatal cry acoustics, movement variability, sleep organisation, heart-rate variability, respiratory patterning, pain reactivity, caregiver synchrony, sensory thresholds, startle, gaze, and later interoceptive accuracy could be studied as early projections of a developing manifold. The difficult question is not whether early life matters. It plainly does. The question is whether a field-attractor model predicts developmental trajectories better than existing risk-factor models `open-hypothesis`.

Interoception is central. The insula, anterior cingulate, somatosensory cortex, brainstem nuclei, thalamic relays, vagal afferents, spinal pathways, endocrine signals, immune signals, and peripheral mechanoreceptors all contribute to bodily feeling [@craig2003]. SFT's field language can be useful if it prevents an overly cortical account of emotion. It becomes unhelpful if it dissolves specific pathways into a vague whole-body aura. The book therefore treats $E_{\text{body}}\otimes E_{\text{neural}}$ as a modelling product: body and neural terms are coupled, but each must be measured through concrete channels `derived-under-assumptions`.

The physical substrate paper broadens this to biotensegrity, fascia, interstitial signalling, mechanical stress, bioelectricity, and contested biofield physiology. Neuroscientists should separate three levels. First, the body is mechanically continuous; fascia and connective tissue transmit force and house sensory endings. Second, interoceptive pathways carry bodily state to the nervous system. Third, stronger claims about coherent biophotonic or biofield correlates of emotion are speculative. The first two are mainstream enough to discuss as substrate; the third requires careful evidence and should remain explicitly open `open-hypothesis`.

Fascia is not a magic emotional organ. It is connective tissue, innervated and mechanically active, embedded in movement, pain, posture, inflammation, and interoception. Chronic stress, injury, guarding, and altered movement can change tone, stiffness, and sensory feedback. Whether these changes map quantitatively onto Hopfield barrier heights is not established. The programme's proposed bridge between fascial stiffness and attractor depth is therefore a research question: can elastography, pain maps, autonomic measures, and affective transitions be linked in a reproducible model `open-hypothesis`?

This distinction protects clinical readers as much as neuroscientists. People with trauma, chronic pain, autism, ADHD, alexithymia, dissociation, or other conditions have often been overinterpreted by models that sounded scientific before they were validated. A field model must not become another way to tell people what their bodies mean. Its appropriate use at this stage is to generate hypotheses, design measurements, and offer language for research. It is not a substitute for clinical assessment, consent, individual formulation, or established care `interpretive`.

The author-case material in the pre-verbal paper is best read as theory-generating testimony. N=1 can reveal a phenomenon, refine a construct, and motivate a cohort study. It cannot estimate prevalence, establish causality, or define diagnostic categories. The paper is explicit that cohort replication is needed. For this book, the relevant methodological lesson is that lived experience can suggest variables that laboratory models miss, while evidence labels prevent lived experience from being misclassified as population evidence `interpretive`.

One example is onset. Standard diagnostic systems often require evidence from developmental history, but histories before language are indirect. Parents, clinicians, schools, medical records, and later self-report each see different projections. A pre-verbal manifold hypothesis says that the deepest structure may predate reportable memory and may appear later as sensory, social, autonomic, attentional, or affective patterns. This is plausible enough to test and risky enough to handle carefully. It must not be used to claim acquired autism in general, nor to erase genetic and neurodevelopmental evidence `open-hypothesis`.

A second example is neurodivergence. The programme sometimes frames autism, ADHD, and C-PTSD as operator modifications. That language can be respectful if it means: these are different response regimes, not moral failures or simple deficits. It becomes unsafe if it implies a single mechanism, a universal hierarchy, or a cure. The neuroscience version should ask whether subgroups show different coupling parameters, sensory precision, entrainment profiles, temperature-like dynamics, or barrier structures. It should also allow that diagnostic categories may split into multiple field regimes and that field regimes may cut across categories `open-hypothesis`.

A third example is clinical intervention. The broader programme uses terms such as barrier lowering, somatic injection, temperature regulation, and basin transition. In this volume these are not instructions. They are candidate mechanisms for future research. A therapy study would need ethics approval, trained clinicians, standard-of-care safeguards, validated outcome measures, adverse-event monitoring, preregistration, and comparison conditions. Until such work exists, the model has no clinical efficacy claim `open-hypothesis`.

The developmental programme also requires humility about measurement. Infant and early-childhood data are noisy, ethically constrained, and heavily context-dependent. Caregiver behaviour, socioeconomic environment, medical history, genetics, sleep, nutrition, pain, infection, and culture all influence trajectories. A field model that ignores these will overfit stories. A good field model would treat them as boundary conditions and inputs, estimating uncertainty rather than forcing a single narrative `derived-under-assumptions`.

The same humility applies to retrospective data. Adult reports of early experience are shaped by memory, family stories, records, therapy, current state, and meaning-making. That does not make them worthless; it makes them layered. The pre-verbal manifold idea is precisely about layers. But scientific use requires triangulation: medical records, developmental histories, contemporaneous documents, physiological measures, and prospective cohorts wherever possible `interpretive`.

Where might the model fail? It may fail if EEG/MEG field measures add little beyond standard connectivity and arousal variables. It may fail if attractor parameters inferred from affect sampling do not predict transitions. It may fail if Arnold tongue measures are unreliable or unrelated to attention and regulation. It may fail if body-state variables do not improve model fit. It may fail if developmental manifold predictions collapse into existing attachment or stress models without added explanatory power. These failures would be informative `open-hypothesis`.

Where might it succeed? It may succeed by offering a common mathematical language for interoception, autonomic regulation, attractor dynamics, and electromagnetic field effects. It may help design experiments that combine neural and bodily data rather than treating physiology as nuisance. It may clarify why some interventions work through state regulation rather than cognitive insight alone, without claiming efficacy. It may identify quantitative phenotypes that cut across DSM categories. It may also fail in its larger metaphysics while leaving useful methods behind `interpretive`.

The clinical caution, then, is not a timid appendix. It is part of the science. The nervous system being modelled belongs to people. Field language can dignify embodied experience, but it can also overreach. The discipline of this book is to hold both facts together: affect may be field-like, measurable, and mathematically tractable; the present programme has not yet earned clinical authority `open-hypothesis`.

# Experimental Programme: From Coherence to Causal Tests

The programme becomes useful to neuroscience only when its concepts are translated into designs that could fail. A field variable inferred after the fact from any interesting pattern is not a scientific variable. It must enter a model before the data are inspected; it must make a prediction that a simpler account might not make; and it must be estimated with uncertainty. For this volume, the target is not a single spectacular experiment, but a ladder of increasingly demanding tests `interpretive`.

The first rung is measurement reliability. Before Arnold tongue width, effective field temperature, or attractor depth can be used theoretically, they must be reliable within individuals and sensitive to known manipulations. A steady-state auditory or visual entrainment task can estimate phase locking across frequencies. A respiration-paced interoceptive task can estimate coupling between bodily rhythm and cortical response. Resting-state EEG or MEG can estimate source-space spectral structure, but resting state alone is weak because it lacks a controlled input. The field programme should therefore prefer active response paradigms over passive maps `open-hypothesis`.

The second rung is convergent physiology. SFT predicts that affective field state is not purely cortical. If an entrainment task changes cortical phase but not autonomic state, report, posture, pupil, respiration, or behaviour, then the result may be sensory tracking rather than affective field coupling. Conversely, if autonomic state changes without cortical response, the relevant kernel may be bodily rather than cortical. A useful experiment therefore records EEG or MEG, ECG-derived HRV, respiration, skin conductance, pupil, movement, and self-report together. The goal is not to maximise variables, but to test whether a latent field model explains their covariance better than separate-channel models `open-hypothesis`.

The third rung is state dependence. FM-HN says that the same stimulus should not produce the same transition under all organismic states. Fatigue, stress, safety cues, breath pacing, social context, sleep loss, medication status, and pain should alter effective temperature, gain, or basin geometry. A simple test would estimate entrainment width before and after a controlled state manipulation. A stronger test would predict the direction of change in advance: for example, whether a mild stressor narrows attention-related locking while increasing threat-biased coupling, or whether paced breathing broadens regulation-related locking. The exact predictions must be preregistered; otherwise the field can explain anything `open-hypothesis`.

The fourth rung is perturbation recovery. A field account should predict not only steady entrainment but recovery from displacement. In TMS-EEG, sensory oddball paradigms, startle, or emotionally salient perturbations, one can ask how quickly the system returns to baseline or enters a new state. Attractor depth appears as return strength; barrier structure appears as asymmetric transition probability; effective temperature appears as variability around the path. These quantities can be inferred without assuming that the person has a single emotion label. The label becomes one projection of a multi-channel recovery process `derived-under-assumptions`.

The fifth rung is longitudinal prediction. A model of field geometry should forecast future transitions. Daily or hourly experience sampling, wearable physiology, and periodic EEG/MEG tasks could estimate a person's landscape over weeks. The model would then predict when transitions are likely, which states are sticky, and which perturbations destabilise or stabilise them. This is where clinical caution is most important. Such models should be used for research and with consent; they should not be sold as personal risk or treatment tools. The ethical risk of overclaiming a field model is highest where the data are intimate `open-hypothesis`.

The sixth rung is comparative modelling. Every [T]-Theory model must be compared against alternatives. For entrainment, compare against standard signal-to-noise, arousal, and attention models. For attractor landscapes, compare against hidden Markov models, dynamical causal models, predictive-processing models, and ordinary autoregressive physiology. For developmental claims, compare against established attachment, adversity, pain, genetic, and socioeconomic predictors. A field model that cannot beat these baselines should be abandoned or narrowed. A field model that beats them in one domain should not be generalised to all domains `interpretive`.

The seventh rung is causal intervention, but this rung should come late. Non-invasive stimulation, neurofeedback, breathwork, movement, sensory entrainment, or clinical interventions might alter coupling parameters, but testing that requires mature measurement and ethics. The present book does not claim such interventions are effective. It only says what a future mechanistic trial would need: baseline landscape estimation, predicted parameter change, blinded or controlled conditions where possible, adverse-event monitoring, and follow-up. Without these, mechanism talk is premature `open-hypothesis`.

A neuroscientific field programme should also define negative controls. Randomised phase stimuli should not produce the same locking as structured stimuli. Sensor-space coherence that disappears under source correction should not count as field evidence. A bodily manipulation with no physiological effect should not shift inferred temperature. A task unrelated to affect should not strongly predict affective transitions unless the model explains why. Null, weak, and contradictory results must be publishable, because they are how the field boundary is learned `interpretive`.

The most important negative control is semantic. The word "field" must not be allowed to migrate between meanings during an argument. It can mean an electromagnetic field, a neural population field, a latent dynamical state, a body-wide interoceptive coupling, or a mathematical field over a manifold. These are related only under stated mappings. A paper that begins with EEG fields and ends with clinical fields without a bridge has changed subject. This volume's labels exist to block that slide `derived-under-assumptions`.

The experimental programme can be summarised as a chain. Controlled input estimates response. Response across channels estimates a latent state. Latent state predicts transitions. Transitions reveal basin geometry. Basin geometry changes with organismic state. Organismic state changes with development and context. Each arrow is a hypothesis, and any arrow may break. Neuroscience does not need to accept the whole chain to test one link `open-hypothesis`.

This is why the programme should be attractive even to sceptics. A sceptical laboratory could take one claim — Arnold tongue width as attentional bandwidth, for example — and operationalise it without endorsing USF metaphysics. If the measure is unreliable, the claim weakens. If it is reliable but unrelated to attention, the interpretation weakens. If it predicts attention under some states and not others, the theory narrows. If it predicts cross-modal regulation better than existing measures, the theory gains a foothold. That is the right pace for a neuroscience of fields `interpretive`.

# Worked Examples: Attention, Threat, Social Entrainment, and Sleep

A field theory is easiest to misunderstand in abstraction, so this chapter gives four worked examples. They are not applications already established by data. They are examples of how a neuroscientist might turn the programme into hypotheses while preserving ordinary disciplinary caution `interpretive`.

## Attention as entrainment bandwidth

Consider sustained attention to a rhythmic auditory stream. Standard accounts can measure behavioural detection, reaction time, evoked potentials, phase locking, and spectral power. A field account adds the shape of the locking region. The participant is tested across frequencies and coupling strengths while cortical and bodily variables are recorded. The Arnold tongue is estimated as the region in which the response reliably phase-locks and improves task performance. The hypothesis is that flexible attention corresponds not to maximum locking, but to adaptive locking: wide enough to follow relevant rhythms, narrow enough to resist irrelevant capture `open-hypothesis`.

This predicts several dissociations. A person may show strong sensory locking but poor task selectivity; this would be entrainment without attentional usefulness. Another may show weak sensor-level locking but good source-space and behavioural selectivity; this would warn against scalp coherence as a proxy. Stress might narrow the tongue around threat-relevant frequencies while reducing flexibility elsewhere. Fatigue might broaden noisy capture without improving performance. These predictions are measurable and could be wrong `open-hypothesis`.

The FM-HN interpretation is that attention depends on the interaction between carrier dynamics and basin structure. A stimulus does not merely enter a passive cortex; it perturbs a landscape already shaped by goals, bodily state, and prior expectation. Effective temperature controls whether the system explores alternatives or snaps to a dominant pattern. Weight geometry controls which inputs reinforce which states. This gives a formal language for attentional set, distractibility, and rigidity without reducing them to a single parameter `derived-under-assumptions`.

## Threat as basin capture

Threat processing is another natural case. A threat cue can rapidly bias perception, autonomic state, posture, memory retrieval, and action readiness. In an attractor model, the threat basin is entered quickly and exited slowly if barriers are high or regulation is weak. In a field model, the cue perturbs cortical and bodily variables together. The empirical question is whether the coupled recovery process has a shape that can be estimated `open-hypothesis`.

A laboratory design might use mild ethically approved threat or aversive stimuli, with startle, pupil, skin conductance, HRV, respiration, EEG/MEG, and report. The model would predict transition probability into a threat-like latent state and recovery time out of it. It would also predict asymmetry: the path into threat may be faster than the path out. If the field temperature is high, transitions may be volatile; if low, recovery may be rigid. If bodily signals improve prediction beyond neural signals alone, the somatic field hypothesis gains support `open-hypothesis`.

Clinical extrapolation must stop there. A laboratory threat basin is not trauma, and a trauma well is not measured by one startle task. Trauma involves memory, meaning, development, social context, and safety. The worked example shows how basin language can be made empirical; it does not authorise clinical conclusions `interpretive`.

## Social entrainment as coupled response

Human interaction often involves synchronisation: speech rhythm, gaze, posture, gesture, heart rate, respiration, and neural oscillatory alignment can become coupled. Social neuroscience already studies interpersonal synchrony. [T]-Theory reads rapport as a coupled-field phenomenon, but the cautious version is simple: two organisms exchange stimuli and adjust their response kernels over time `interpretive`.

A dyadic experiment could record two participants during conversation, joint music, coordinated movement, or cooperative tasks. EEG hyperscanning is tempting but methodologically difficult; movement, speech, respiration, and common sensory input can create spurious synchrony. A rigorous design would include surrogate pairings, delayed signals, task controls, and source-space analysis. The field prediction is not merely that synchrony exists, but that specific cross-person coupling parameters predict coordination, repair after mismatch, and subjective rapport better than individual arousal alone `open-hypothesis`.

FM-HN gives this a landscape reading. Each participant has attractor dynamics; interaction supplies inputs; successful rapport may widen the range over which both systems can lock without either losing autonomy. Too little coupling yields disconnection; too much yields capture, compliance, or flooding. This is an analogy until quantified, but it is a useful one because it suggests measurable failure modes `interpretive`.

## Sleep and state transitions

Sleep shows why state matters. The same stimulus has different effects in wakefulness, drowsiness, non-REM sleep, REM sleep, and awakening. Oscillatory regimes, neuromodulators, autonomic tone, memory consolidation, and sensory thresholds all change. If the field programme cannot handle sleep, it cannot handle state-dependent neuroscience `open-hypothesis`.

A sleep study could examine how affective or interoceptive stimuli perturb EEG, heart rate, respiration, and later report across sleep stages. The model would not claim that sleep is a somatic field by itself. It would ask how global state changes response kernels and attractor accessibility. A stimulus ignored in deep sleep, incorporated into a dream in REM, and consciously reported in wakefulness may be the same input passing through different kernels. This is a natural testbed for threshold and field-temperature ideas `derived-under-assumptions`.

Sleep also guards against a simplistic consciousness claim. The formal threshold in the programme is a predicate over an amplitude. Real consciousness has graded reportability, memory, attention, arousal, and content. Sleep stages show that threshold crossing cannot be read naively. A field neuroscience must distinguish subthreshold processing, reportable awareness, memory encoding, and later narration. The Lean dichotomy is formal; the biology is layered `kernel-verified`.

These four examples share a method. Choose a phenomenon already studied by neuroscience. Define the field variable as a response property, not as a metaphysical conclusion. Measure neural and bodily channels. Estimate a model with failure criteria. Compare to simpler alternatives. Keep clinical and philosophical claims out of the result until the measurement earns them. That is how [T]-Theory can enter neuroscience without demanding premature belief `interpretive`.
# What Would Count as Evidence

Because [T]-Theory deliberately crosses vocabularies, it needs a stricter evidential grammar than a single-domain model. A neuroscientist should ask four questions of every claim. What is the measured variable? What is the model variable? What mapping connects them? What result would force revision? If any of the four is missing, the claim is not yet neuroscience `interpretive`.

For the CEMI bridge, the measured variables include source-resolved electromagnetic activity, phase relations, perturbation responses, and possibly local field effects. The model variable is the cortical electromagnetic component of a broader somatic field. The mapping says that specific field configurations should predict integration, transition, or report better than non-field summaries. The falsifier is clear: if well-controlled EM field variables add no predictive or causal value beyond standard synaptic, anatomical, and arousal models, then the CEMI extension should be narrowed or dropped `open-hypothesis`.

For the ephaptic bridge, the measured variables include extracellular field strength, geometry, membrane polarisation, spike timing, and local synchronisation. The model variable is a weak field-to-threshold coupling. The mapping says that extracellular fields can bias excitability in a way that matters for population dynamics. The falsifier is again clear: if realistic endogenous fields are too weak, too local, or too noisy to affect relevant dynamics in vivo, then ephaptic coupling can remain a local biophysical effect without supporting the programme's larger claim `open-hypothesis`.

For the EEG/MEG bridge, the measured variables are spectral power, phase, source-space current estimates, evoked responses, cross-frequency coupling, and perturbation recovery. The model variable is a response kernel or attractor state. The mapping says that field structure is not merely a recording artefact but a predictor of state transitions. Falsification requires methods discipline: source leakage, common drive, reference effects, and movement artefact must be controlled before a null result or positive result is meaningful `derived-under-assumptions`.

For the Hopfield bridge, the measured variables are latent states, dwell times, transition probabilities, recovery curves, and parameter estimates from repeated observations. The model variables are $W$, $b$, temperature, noise, and basin geometry. The mapping says that affective dynamics behave like movement across an energy landscape. The falsifier is that simpler state-space models predict equally well without energy, barriers, or field modulation. In that case Hopfield language may remain pedagogical but not explanatory `open-hypothesis`.

For the limbic-cortical bridge, the measured variables include amygdala, hippocampal, insular, cingulate, prefrontal, hypothalamic, brainstem, autonomic, and endocrine indicators, depending on species and modality. The model variable is not "the limbic system" as a lump, but a coupling operator between bodily state and cortical computation. The mapping says that organismic state alters effective gain and transition geometry. A falsifier would be robust evidence that bodily and limbic variables do not improve prediction once cortical variables are known, at least for the affective tasks tested `open-hypothesis`.

For the developmental bridge, the measured variables are harder: early physiology, pain history, caregiver synchrony, movement, sensory profile, sleep, medical records, later report, and cohort outcomes. The model variable is a pre-verbal coupling manifold. The mapping says early non-verbal bodily regulation leaves structured projections in later affective dynamics. The falsifier is failure of prospective measures to predict later field parameters beyond established developmental predictors. The ethical requirement is that such research not become retrospective blame `open-hypothesis`.

This grammar also clarifies what would not count as evidence. A striking personal story is not population evidence. A coherence map is not proof of communication. A Lean theorem about a definition is not validation of a biological mechanism. A simulation is not a clinical trial. A metaphor that helps one reader is not a measurement. These statements are not hostile to [T]-Theory; they protect it from the fate of many grand theories of mind, where every observation becomes confirmatory because the theory never risked contact with disconfirmation `interpretive`.

A mature evidence ladder for this volume would have at least six levels. Level one is formal coherence: the equations are well defined and their limits behave as claimed. Level two is simulation: the model produces the predicted transitions in controlled artificial systems. Level three is measurement reliability: proposed neural and bodily parameters can be estimated consistently. Level four is cross-sectional prediction: parameters distinguish tasks or states better than baselines. Level five is longitudinal prediction: parameters forecast future transitions. Level six is intervention: changing a parameter changes the predicted dynamics under ethical and controlled conditions. The present programme is strongest at levels one and two, partial at level three, and mostly open beyond that `derived-under-assumptions`.

The evidence ladder should be reported in every future neuroscience paper. A claim such as "field temperature predicts recovery" should state whether this is a formal definition, a simulation result, a pilot correlation, a replicated empirical result, or a proposed clinical mechanism. The difference is not stylistic. It tells the reader what kind of trust is appropriate `interpretive`.

The same discipline applies to consciousness language. This volume can discuss consciousness because CEMI does and because affect becomes reportable. But it should avoid claiming that a field measure has solved phenomenality. At most, the neuroscience programme can identify physical and dynamical conditions under which field activity becomes reportable, integrated, and behaviourally available. Whether that exhausts experience belongs partly to the philosophy volume. Here the question is operational: what changes when subthreshold affect becomes reportable feeling `open-hypothesis`?

The most productive near-term output may be a shared dataset rather than a grand conclusion. A dataset combining entrainment tasks, perturbation recovery, EEG/MEG, autonomic physiology, report, and longitudinal sampling would allow multiple laboratories to compare models. Field models, predictive-processing models, dynamical causal models, hidden Markov models, and standard connectivity analyses could compete on the same observations. If [T]-Theory wins only in prose, it loses. If it wins predictive comparisons, it begins to become neuroscience `open-hypothesis`.

A second near-term output would be a negative-results registry. Because the programme is broad, selective publication would be especially dangerous. Failed Arnold tongue predictions, null field-temperature correlations, unreliable attractor estimates, and unsuccessful body-state mappings should be recorded. They would show where the field metaphor stops. A theory of response must include non-response `interpretive`.

A third output would be terminology discipline. The programme should reserve `kernel-verified` for named Lean theorems; `derived-under-assumptions` for consequences of explicit model choices; `simulated` for computational outputs; `empirical-result` for documented measurements; `interpretive` for conceptual readings; and `open-hypothesis` for claims awaiting discrimination. These labels make it possible for a sceptical neuroscientist to read the book without wondering where the evidential floor is `interpretive`.

The question "what would count as evidence?" therefore has a direct answer: reliable response functions, state-dependent predictions, body-brain convergence, perturbation recovery, longitudinal forecasting, comparative model performance, and eventually controlled parameter change. Anything less may still be interesting, but it should be labelled accordingly.

# Conclusion: Toward a Measurable Field Neuroscience

A neuroscience of the somatic field would not replace cellular neuroscience, systems neuroscience, affective neuroscience, computational psychiatry, or clinical science. It would add a level of description: the response-function level at which body, brain, field, and report are modelled together. The promise of [T]-Theory is that it gives this level a shared mathematical language. The danger is that the same language can make unearned identities sound settled. This conclusion therefore states the programme in falsifiable terms `interpretive`.

First, the electromagnetic claim must be narrowed to what can be measured. Brains generate fields; EEG and MEG measure aspects of them; ephaptic effects can occur; CEMI proposes that endogenous EM fields carry and causally integrate conscious information. SFT extends this by asking whether affective field state is visible in response functions, entrainment, perturbation recovery, and state-dependent attractor dynamics. The claim remains open until field variables predict something that local activity, anatomy, and standard connectivity do not `open-hypothesis`.

Second, the Hopfield claim must be treated as model class rather than literal anatomy. Affective states can be represented as attractor basins; $W$ can encode co-activation and inhibition; temperature can represent flexibility or noise; input can tilt the landscape. FM-HN adds bodily modulation. The model earns its keep only if inferred landscape parameters predict transitions, dwell times, recovery curves, or treatment-independent longitudinal changes better than competing models `open-hypothesis`.

Third, limbic-cortical interaction must remain biologically specific. The field variable cannot be a decorative name for everything subcortical. It must be decomposed into measurable components: oscillatory phase, source-space field structure, autonomic state, interoceptive signals, endocrine and immune context, movement and posture, developmental history, and report. Different components operate on different timescales. A good model will have multiple kernels, not one undifferentiated field `derived-under-assumptions`.

Fourth, EEG/MEG coherence must be disciplined. The programme should not use coherence as an icon of unity. It should use coherence, phase lag, cross-frequency coupling, source reconstruction, perturbation response, and behavioural correlation under strict controls. Raw synchrony is not proof of communication; high coherence is not proof of consciousness; source-space modelling is not direct access to experience. The field must be inferred by convergent prediction `interpretive`.

Fifth, the developmental claim must become prospective. The pre-verbal manifold is most valuable if it leads to cohort designs measuring early regulation, pain, autonomic variability, caregiver synchrony, movement, sleep, sensory response, and later affective dynamics. Retrospective testimony can generate hypotheses, but only longitudinal data can sort developmental pathways. This is especially important where neurodivergence and trauma overlap `open-hypothesis`.

Sixth, clinical language must remain cautious. Trauma wells, operator modifications, field temperature, and barrier lowering are research constructs. They are not diagnostic tools, not treatment advice, and not evidence of clinical efficacy. A future clinical neuroscience might use field parameters to stratify mechanisms, but that future would require replication, ethical trials, and comparison to existing care. Until then, the book's claims remain mechanistic hypotheses `open-hypothesis`.

The immediate experimental programme is clear. Measure Arnold tongue width under controlled sensory and interoceptive entrainment. Estimate affective attractor landscapes from repeated state sampling and physiology. Use perturbation designs to infer recovery kernels. Combine EEG/MEG with autonomic and interoceptive measures. Test whether body-state variables improve prediction of neural transitions. Compare CEMI-compatible field models against non-field baselines. Publish null results. Update the model `open-hypothesis`.

The formal programme is also clear. Keep Lean claims scoped to their definitions. A correspondence theorem establishes a property of equations, not a fact about the organism. A threshold dichotomy establishes a predicate split, not consciousness. A simulation establishes model-class reachability, not therapy. A co-identification imports structure only under matched assumptions. This discipline is not a concession; it is the programme's best defence `kernel-verified`.

If the programme fails, it may fail productively. It may show that CEMI effects are too weak for affective integration, that Arnold tongues are not useful markers, that field variables add no predictive power, or that attractor models can be replaced by simpler dynamical accounts. Those outcomes would still clarify the relation between field measurement and affective neuroscience. The goal is not to protect [T]-Theory from failure; it is to expose it to the right failures `interpretive`.

If the programme succeeds, neuroscience gains a bridge language. It could connect electrophysiology to interoception, attractor dynamics to limbic-cortical regulation, development to adult field geometry, and subjective report to perturbation response. It would not solve the hard problem by declaration. It would make felt affect a measurable dynamical target. That is enough work for a research programme `open-hypothesis`.

There is also a useful conservative reading of the entire volume. Suppose CEMI in its strong consciousness form fails. Suppose ephaptic effects remain too weak for macroscopic cognition. Suppose biofield language is discarded. Much of the book would still leave a tractable programme: affective neuroscience needs better models of body-brain state, response kernels, attractor geometry, and developmentally shaped coupling. Hopfield language, entrainment measures, perturbation recovery, and interoceptive physiology would remain useful tools even without the full USF ontology `interpretive`.

This fallback matters for scientific adoption. A programme that survives only if every speculative layer is accepted is brittle. A programme that decomposes into testable modules can fail locally and improve. The CEMI module can be tested against non-field integration models. The FM-HN module can be tested against ordinary state-space models. The developmental manifold module can be tested against established developmental predictors. The clinical metaphor can be dropped if it fails to improve measurement. This modularity is not a retreat; it is how a broad theory becomes a research discipline `derived-under-assumptions`.

The strongest immediate claim may therefore be methodological rather than ontological. Neuroscience often measures neural, bodily, behavioural, and subjective variables in separate literatures. [T]-Theory insists that affective state is a coupled object and that the coupling itself should be modelled. Even if the ultimate field ontology is wrong, a research culture that estimates body-brain response functions, publishes negative results, and distinguishes formal proof from empirical validation would be stronger than one that treats physiology as peripheral noise `interpretive`.

The remaining task is translational. A physicist's propagator, a computational neuroscientist's state-space model, a clinician's regulation window, and an electrophysiologist's evoked response are not the same object. Yet they can be made commensurable if each is treated as a response relation under perturbation. This is the practical meaning of the Green propagator in the present volume. It is a shared bookkeeping device for cause, medium, boundary condition, and effect. Used carefully, it helps laboratories compare findings without pretending that all scales are identical `interpretive`.

The programme should therefore proceed by small bridges. One bridge from CEMI to source-space EEG/MEG. One bridge from ephaptic coupling to gain modulation. One bridge from Hopfield energy to affect sampling. One bridge from interoception to bodily report. One bridge from early physiology to adult transition dynamics. Each bridge needs its own assumptions and failure criteria. The universal language earns credibility only by surviving these local crossings `open-hypothesis`.

This is also why the book keeps returning to clinical caution. A model of response can make suffering more intelligible, but intelligibility is not authorisation. Neuroscience can study attractor depth, regulation, and developmental coupling without telling a person what they are, what caused them, or what will heal them. The ethical form of field neuroscience is therefore collaborative, consent-based, and modest about present knowledge `interpretive`.

The strongest version of the proposal is thus patient with its own uncertainty. It invites laboratories to treat affect as a coupled dynamical system and to ask whether electromagnetic, interoceptive, and attractor variables improve prediction. It does not require allegiance to every metaphysical layer before ordinary experiments can begin `open-hypothesis`.

That modesty is not decorative. It is the condition under which a speculative synthesis can become a normal empirical programme rather than a closed belief system `interpretive`.

The book therefore argues for measurable bridges, not premature closure `interpretive`.

It can be tested, narrowed, and rejected without losing its useful questions `interpretive`.

The electromagnetic nervous system is therefore a proposal about scale. A neuron fires; a population synchronises; a field propagates; a body regulates; a person feels; a report is made. [T]-Theory asks whether these are separate descriptions of one response system. Neuroscience will decide by measurement.
