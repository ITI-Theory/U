# The Green Propagator

**G-ID:** *Seismic Memory Propagator — elastic Green's function for crustal wave propagation* `derived-under-assumptions`. In this book the propagator is not introduced from the biological Soma-Field and then forced onto the Earth. It is already native to seismology. A force couple, a moment tensor, a pressure pulse, a teleseismic phase, or a laboratory acoustic emission is read through the same basic question: what displacement, strain, velocity, or stress is observed at $x$ and time $t$ when the medium is perturbed at $x'$ and time $t'$? That answer is a Green's function. The [T]-Theory claim begins only after this standard fact is acknowledged: seismic Green's functions may be treated as one restricted instance of the programme's master response grammar, provided the substrate, boundary conditions, material tensors, attenuation, and measurement scale are all kept explicit.

The zoom note for this volume is geological. It mainly works around $\sigma \approx 10$--$12$ in the programme's pedagogical scale dial: basin acoustics, crustal seismic waves, fault-zone nucleation, aftershock diffusion, plate-boundary memory, and stratigraphic time. Nothing in the argument requires a rock to have a nervous system. The word "soma" is a programme name and an analogy when applied to the Earth; the co-identification, where defensible, is at the level of propagators and energy landscapes, not at the level of biological feeling `interpretive`.

# Introduction: The Geological Soma

Seismology is one of the most successful observational sciences of the hidden Earth. A global earthquake can now be recorded by dense broadband networks; source mechanisms are inverted from waveforms; tomographic images turn phase delays into structure; strong-motion records inform engineering spectra; ambient noise correlations recover empirical Green's functions between stations; laboratory friction experiments constrain constitutive laws; geodesy tracks interseismic loading at millimetre scale; InSAR resolves coseismic displacement fields across whole ruptures. The discipline is not short of mathematical machinery. It has wave theory, elasticity, fracture mechanics, statistical seismology, geodesy, thermodynamics, and numerical simulation. A serious cross-domain proposal must therefore begin by respecting the field's existing competence.

The difficulty is not that geophysics lacks equations. It is that its most consequential problem, earthquake occurrence in space and time, lies at the intersection of equations whose scales do not join cleanly. Elastic wave propagation is well posed once the source and structure are given. Kinematic rupture inversion can reconstruct what happened after it happened. Rate-and-state friction can reproduce laboratory instabilities and explain important features of nucleation. Coulomb stress transfer can estimate how one event changes the stress field for another. Epidemic-type aftershock models capture clustering statistics. Yet no accepted theory converts the full evolving crustal state into reliable prediction of the next large rupture. This is not a failure of intelligence; it is the signature of a multiscale, heterogeneous, dissipative, history-dependent system.

The [T]-Theory programme enters at exactly that multiscale seam. Its recurring object is the propagator: the response of a medium to an impulse. In physics this is standard. In seismology it is standard. In the programme as a whole it is made into a scale-indexed organising principle `derived-under-assumptions`. The useful geological question is therefore not whether seismic waves are "like" Green's functions. They are described by Green's functions in the normal language of the field. The useful question is whether the programme's additional grammar — energy landscapes, critical transitions, memory kernels, WKB barrier crossing, and mathematical co-identification — can sharpen any geological problem without blurring what seismology already knows.

The book's answer is deliberately tiered. At the first tier, the contact is ordinary mathematics. The elastodynamic Green's tensor $G_{ij}(x,t;x',t')$ is the displacement in direction $i$ at $x,t$ caused by an impulsive unit force in direction $j$ at $x',t'$, subject to the medium's density, elastic moduli, attenuation, anisotropy, interfaces, and boundary conditions. This is a genuine equation-level contact with the programme's master propagator `derived-under-assumptions`. A seismologist does not need a new ontology to recognise it.

At the second tier, the programme proposes a co-identification between tectonic stress landscapes and associative-memory energy landscapes. Locked faults occupy metastable configurations; past ruptures and inherited fabrics bias future paths; a fault zone has a state variable as well as a stress variable; the geometry of weakness matters. These facts are familiar in geology and rate-and-state friction. The programme's Hopfield language may provide a compact way of saying that a medium stores patterns in its couplings `interpretive`. But this is not yet a theorem about real faults. It is a modelling proposal whose validity depends on whether the state variables, kernels, and observables can be specified.

At the third tier, the programme makes the boldest geological proposal: nucleation can be read as a WKB-like barrier-crossing problem in an effective fault-state coordinate `open-hypothesis`. This is not a claim of literal quantum tunnelling through crustal rock. In this volume $\hbar_{\text{geo}}$ names an effective action or noise scale, not Planck's constant. The appropriate comparison is not a magical quantum earthquake. It is the family of asymptotic, large-deviation, Kramers, and WKB methods that estimate rare transitions across barriers in noisy dynamical systems. The proposal becomes scientific only when $q$, $V(q)$, $E$, and $\hbar_{\text{geo}}$ are operationally defined from laboratory, geodetic, and seismic data.

That distinction matters because earthquake science already has a long history of attractive but overextended metaphors. Self-organised criticality was introduced by Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld for slowly driven threshold systems and was soon proposed as a way to think about earthquake scaling.<!-- REF-NEEDED: Per Bak, Chao Tang, and Kurt Wiesenfeld, "Self-organized criticality: An explanation of 1/f noise", Physical Review Letters, 1987; Per Bak and Chao Tang, "Earthquakes as a self-organized critical phenomenon", Journal of Geophysical Research, 1989 --> It also provoked a durable debate: are faults genuinely critical systems, merely broadly distributed threshold systems, or heterogeneous frictional networks whose apparent scale invariance emerges from mixing, finite-size effects, and observation thresholds? The same caution applies to Omori's law and its Utsu modification. Aftershock rates often decay approximately as $n(t)=K/(c+t)^p$, with $p$ commonly near one but not fixed.<!-- REF-NEEDED: Fusakichi Omori, "On the after-shocks of earthquakes", Journal of the College of Science, Imperial University of Tokyo, 1894; Tokuji Utsu, "A statistical study on the occurrence of aftershocks", Geophysical Magazine, 1961 --> That empirical regularity is now embedded in richer models such as ETAS, in which earthquakes can trigger further earthquakes in a branching point process.<!-- REF-NEEDED: Yosihiko Ogata, "Statistical models for earthquake occurrences and residual analysis for point processes", Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1988 --> If [T]-Theory says "Omori follows from a propagator", that sentence is not enough. Otherwise it has only renamed a law. A good geological version of the programme must be stricter than its own rhetoric.

Rate-and-state friction is the main discipline to which any nucleation proposal must answer. The Dieterich-Ruina framework writes friction as depending on slip rate and an evolving state variable, often in the form $\mu=\mu_0+a\ln(V/V_0)+b\ln(\theta V_0/D_c)$, with ageing or slip laws for $\theta$.<!-- REF-NEEDED: James H. If the programme's "geological memory" is to mean anything precise, it should meet this framework rather than bypass it. The natural bridge is that the state variable $\theta$, damage, gouge fabric, pore pressure, mineral alteration, and healed contact population are all memory-bearing quantities. The programme's Hopfield analogy is plausible only if it can be translated into these variables.

The audience for this book will also expect honesty about prediction. A WKB nucleation curve, even if successful, would not yield deterministic prediction of large earthquakes in the popular sense. It might yield a conditional hazard term, a rate multiplier, a testable departure from a baseline stochastic model, or a better account of why one cluster cascades and another dies. It might be more useful in laboratory stick-slip, mine seismicity, induced seismicity, volcanic tremor, or creeping fault sections than in plate-boundary earthquakes with centuries-long recurrence intervals. The appropriate success criterion is not "predict the earthquake". It is "improve a specified probabilistic forecast or discriminate between mechanisms under pre-registered conditions" `open-hypothesis`.

The phrase "geological soma" is therefore not a claim that the Earth feels. It is a shorthand for a medium with long memory, distributed coupling, thresholds, and propagating responses `interpretive`. Rock stores deformation. Fault zones remember previous slip through geometry, damage, gouge, mineralisation, permeability, and contact healing. Sedimentary basins amplify or attenuate waves according to their structure. Subduction zones carry inherited roughness, fluids, thermal gradients, and slab geometry into future rupture behaviour. These are geological facts in ordinary language. The programme's contribution, if it has one, is to place them in a common response-and-memory notation.

The volume follows that discipline. The first chapter develops the seismic Green's function contact in the field's own language: elastodynamics, source representation, reciprocity, attenuation, heterogeneous media, and inverse problems. The second chapter turns to faults, friction, Gutenberg-Richter statistics, Omori decay, rate-and-state nucleation, and criticality debates. The third chapter asks what "memory" could mean in geology and lays out concrete tests of the programme's WKB and Hopfield claims. The paper-reading sections then locate each embedded paper for a geophysical reader, so that even if the printed volume later replaces the papers with cross-references, the argument remains intelligible.

The most important rule is this: identity at the level of an equation does not imply identity at the level of substrate, mechanism, or ontology. A seismic Green's tensor and a neural field propagator may share a formal grammar; they do not share material parameters. A Gutenberg-Richter power law and a critical phase transition may be mathematically connected in a model; the exponent still has to be derived or estimated. A fault-zone memory and a Hopfield attractor may both be energy-landscape phenomena; the mapping must preserve dimensions, variables, kernels, and observables. Mathematical co-identification is not permission to flatten science. It is a stricter demand that the type match before any theorem travels `derived-under-assumptions`.

## What a geophysicist should permit, and what they should refuse

A geophysicist should permit the programme to use familiar mathematical language. There is nothing exotic about an impulse response, a resolvent, a kernel, an eigenmode, a spectral gap, a basin, or an instability. These words already appear in wave propagation, inverse theory, normal-mode seismology, stability analysis, and nonlinear dynamics. If [T]-Theory had to invent a new physics in order to speak to the Earth, that would be a warning sign. Its most credible geological claims are precisely those that do not require new vocabulary at the first step.

A geophysicist should also permit analogy when it is declared as analogy. Science uses analogy constantly: hydraulic head and electrical potential, heat flow and diffusion, epidemics and aftershock branching, spring-slider systems and faults, sandpiles and avalanches. The problem is not analogy. The problem is an analogy that forgets its own limits. In this volume, "geological soma" is an analogy for a history-bearing medium; "fault memory" is a modelling phrase; "trauma well" is a borrowed image for metastability; "limbic constraint" is not literal geology. These phrases may help organise thought, but they do not count as evidence.

A geophysicist should refuse claims that treat a name as a derivation. Saying "criticality" does not derive the Gutenberg-Richter exponent. Saying "Green's function" does not solve earthquake nucleation. Saying "memory" does not identify a state variable. Saying "phase transition" does not define an order parameter. Saying "scale invariant" does not prove that a laboratory friction law, a regional catalogue, and a plate-boundary recurrence sequence belong to one universality class. This refusal is not hostility. It is the discipline that makes the programme testable.

The programme itself benefits from that refusal. Its evidence labels distinguish `kernel-verified`, `derived-under-assumptions`, `simulated`, `empirical-result`, `interpretive`, and `open-hypothesis`. Those labels are not cosmetic. They are the difference between a theorem in a proof assistant, a consequence of a model, a numerical result, a measurement, a conceptual reading, and a proposed research question. A geological book without those labels would be too easy to read as overclaim. A geological book with them can be bold because it is bounded.

This is why the book treats the word "exact" carefully. A seismic Green's function is exactly a Green's function for its specified operator. A scalar Helmholtz Green's function is exactly the solution of its specified scalar equation. A finite-element synthetic waveform is exactly the numerical output of a discretised model within numerical error. None of these exactitudes implies that the scalar equation exactly describes a subduction megathrust, that a social propagator exactly describes a seismic wave, or that a clinical memory kernel exactly describes a fault gouge. Exactness belongs to a statement, not to a mood.

The same care applies to "derivation". In seismology, a derivation of the Gutenberg-Richter law would specify a physical ensemble, event measure, magnitude-moment relation, scaling assumptions, finite-size cut-offs, stress or energy distribution, and observation process. It would show why the exponent has the observed value and why it varies. In the current programme, that full derivation is not present. What is present is a conjectural placement of Gutenberg-Richter statistics within a critical or near-critical field landscape. That is worth investigating, but it should be called an `open-hypothesis`.

Similarly, a derivation of Omori decay would specify the time-dependent triggering or relaxation mechanism. It might come from rate-and-state response to a stress step, afterslip-driven stressing, viscoelastic relaxation, fluid diffusion, cascade branching, or some mixture. A Green's-function phrase alone cannot decide among these. If [T]-Theory can express those mechanisms in a common kernel notation and show which conditions yield $p\approx1$, it will have added something. Until then, Omori remains a benchmark against which the programme must measure itself.

The book therefore adopts a double standard, in the good sense. It gives the programme the strongest charitable reading compatible with geophysics, then applies the harshest evidence label compatible with the current state of the work. When a claim is a standard seismological fact, it is treated as part of the field. When it is a programme mapping under assumptions, it is labelled as such. When it is an analogy, it says so. When it is a proposed test, it remains open. This is not an attempt to make the theory smaller. It is an attempt to make it usable.

## The scale of the problem

Earthquake processes span more scales than a single model usually handles. Grain contacts evolve at micrometre to millimetre scales. Gouge layers and shear bands organise at millimetres to metres. Nucleation patches may occupy metres to kilometres. Dynamic ruptures run over kilometres to hundreds of kilometres. Stress redistribution reaches fault networks and plate boundaries. Recurrence intervals range from laboratory seconds to geological millennia. Seismic waves then propagate through structures from near-fault damage zones to the whole Earth. A theory that speaks easily across scales is attractive because the problem itself demands scale coupling.

But scale coupling is not scale erasure. The constitutive law at a grain contact is not the same as the elastodynamic equation for a teleseismic P wave. A damage-zone permeability change is not an aftershock productivity law. A megathrust's thermal structure is not a basin site response. The programme's zoom operator is useful only if it keeps track of what changes when the scale changes. The geological reader should therefore interpret the "twenty scales" not as a numerological ladder but as a reminder that every level has its own state variables and operators `interpretive`.

This point also protects the programme from a common criticism. If one hears "the same equation governs everything" as "nothing changes except labels", the claim is false. If one hears it as "many systems can be described by a source-response operator with scale-specific substrate and boundary conditions", the claim is far more defensible. Seismology already knows this. The elastic wave equation, diffusion equation, Darcy flow, heat equation, and rate-and-state ordinary differential equations are not the same equation. But all can be studied through operators, kernels, stability, and response.

The volume's title, *The Geological Soma*, should therefore be read as a provocation disciplined by method. The Earth has no clinical soma. It has crust, mantle, fluids, faults, sediments, basins, slabs, and waves. Yet it also has a body in the older physical sense: an extended material system whose present response depends on its structure and history. That is the permissible meaning. It is enough.

# Seismic Green's Functions and the Master Equation

The natural home for the programme's master equation in geophysics is not earthquake statistics but seismic wave propagation. In the simplest isotropic elastic case the displacement field $u_i(x,t)$ obeys an equation of the form

$$
\rho\,\ddot u_i = \partial_j \sigma_{ij} + f_i,\qquad
\sigma_{ij} = \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\,\partial_k u_k+\mu(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i),
$$

with density $\rho$, Lamé parameters $\lambda,\mu$, stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$, and body force $f_i$. In a heterogeneous anisotropic Earth the constitutive tensor, density, attenuation, and interfaces become spatially dependent, and the scalar Helmholtz equation becomes a reduced or idealised member of a wider elastodynamic family. The relevant object is the Green's tensor, not merely a scalar $G$. It carries source and receiver components, phase conversions, boundary interactions, and polarisation.

A seismic Green's function can be written conceptually as $G_{ij}(x,t;x',t')$, the $i$-component of displacement at the receiver caused by an impulsive force in the $j$-direction at the source. In the frequency domain and under appropriate assumptions this becomes the resolvent of the elastic operator. It is the field's impulse response in exactly the sense used by mathematical physics. This is why the programme's Green-propagator language is not foreign to seismology `derived-under-assumptions`. It is a relabelling of something the field already uses, with the warning that the real elastic operator is richer than the scalar textbook form.

For earthquake sources, the representation theorem expresses the observed displacement as a convolution of the Green's function with an equivalent force system or moment tensor. The centroid moment tensor is not just a catalogue convenience; it encodes double-couple faulting, isotropic components, and compensated linear vector dipoles in a way that naturally couples to Green's tensors. A finite rupture is a distribution of slip in space and time convolved with these responses. To a seismologist, "source times Green's function" is not a metaphor. It is the practical architecture of waveform modelling.

This matters for [T]-Theory because it locates the strongest possible co-identification. The programme's master equation says: a substrate plus boundary conditions plus a source determines a response kernel. Seismology says: an elastic Earth model plus a source determines waveforms by Green's functions. At this tier, the mapping is a mathematical co-identification under the stated operator and boundary assumptions `derived-under-assumptions`. It does not depend on claims about consciousness, trauma, cosmology, or M-theory. Those may be part of the larger programme, but the geological contact can be assessed independently.

The scalar Helmholtz equation, $(\nabla^2+k^2)G=\delta$, is best read as a pedagogical doorway rather than as a full crustal model. It is exact for simple scalar waves in simple media; it is an approximation or component reduction for elastic waves; it is inadequate for strong anisotropy, viscoelastic attenuation, poroelasticity, scattering in cracked media, and nonlinear near-fault damage. A hardened geophysical version of the programme should therefore speak of the elastic operator $\mathcal{L}$ and its resolvent $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$, with the scalar equation as a local or illustrative case. The programme is safer when it says "same response grammar" rather than "same scalar equation everywhere" `interpretive`.

In practice, seismic Green's functions are rarely known exactly. They are computed numerically, approximated by ray theory, inferred from ambient noise correlations, estimated by empirical Green's function methods, or embedded in inversion. Normal-mode summation works for global long-period signals; reflectivity and wavenumber integration handle layered media; finite-difference, finite-element, and spectral-element methods handle complex three-dimensional structure; full-waveform inversion updates Earth models by comparing predicted and observed waveforms. Each method is a way of approximating the response kernel of a complicated medium. The programme's language becomes useful if it can integrate these methods without pretending that a closed-form propagator exists.

The WKB or eikonal approximation occupies a particularly important place. In a slowly varying medium, high-frequency waves can be approximated by rays; phase accumulates along paths according to slowness; amplitudes evolve by geometrical spreading and focusing; caustics and shadow zones appear when the ray geometry folds. This is standard seismology, not a new theory. The programme's WKB nucleation proposal borrows the same asymptotic sensibility but applies it to transitions in a fault-state energy landscape rather than merely to wave travel through smooth velocity structure `open-hypothesis`. A geophysicist should keep those two WKB uses distinct.

Attenuation also matters. The Earth is not a lossless string. Intrinsic anelasticity, scattering, fluid effects, thermoelastic processes, grain-boundary relaxation, and partial melt can all reduce amplitudes and shift phases. The quality factor $Q$ is frequency-dependent and structure-dependent. In a Green's function this appears through complex velocities, damping kernels, or memory terms. A programme that treats the propagator as a pure Helmholtz object risks missing a central feature of seismology: the response of the Earth is dispersive and dissipative. That is not a defect for [T]-Theory; it is an opportunity to connect to its own memory-kernel language, provided the kernels are physically parameterised `derived-under-assumptions`.

Boundary conditions are equally important. A free surface doubles and converts phases; sedimentary basins trap surface waves; topography scatters; the Moho, core-mantle boundary, inner-core boundary, slab interfaces, and anisotropic fabrics all structure the response. The same source in two Earth models can yield different observed waveforms because the Green's function is different. In the programme's vocabulary, the medium is not a passive stage on which impulses travel. It is the operator that decides what an impulse can become. This sentence is interpretive, but it is grounded in ordinary wave physics `interpretive`.

The most useful geological reading of the programme's "scale invariance" is therefore modest. It should not claim that every scale has the same material equation. It should claim that the source-response architecture recurs: source, operator, boundary condition, propagator, observable. At one scale the operator is elastic; at another it may be acoustic, electromagnetic, hydrological, or social. A cross-scale comparison is legitimate only after the variables and operator class are named. The seismic case is the cleanest because the word "propagator" is already literal.

This has consequences for how the embedded Geographic Somatic Field paper should be read. The Thames Valley examples of dialect spread and parakeet flocking are not geophysics in the narrow sense. They are demonstrations of boundary-conditioned propagation across a landscape. For a seismologist, their value is not that they prove geology. Their value is that they make the programme's scale-translation rule explicit: only the response grammar is shared; the state variables and mechanisms change. That rule is precisely what prevents the geological book from becoming metaphor.

The Klöntalersee and Glarus examples sit closer to Earth science. A glacial valley can act as an acoustic resonator; a nappe structure records deformation and transport over geological time. The paper's phrase "ten-million-year wave" is best treated as interpretive compression, not as a literal oscillation in the acoustic sense `interpretive`. Geological structures do preserve the integrated result of long processes, but a preserved overthrust is not a seismogram. It is a deformed body whose present geometry constrains the history of stress, temperature, strain rate, lithology, and erosion. The propagator language should illuminate that constraint, not erase geological specificity.

A hardened [T]-Theory geophysics would therefore begin with a formal translation table. A seismic source is a moment tensor or distributed slip history. The substrate is a density-elastic-attenuation model. The operator is elastodynamic, possibly viscoelastic or poroelastic. The Green's function maps source to displacement, velocity, strain, or stress. The boundary conditions are surfaces, interfaces, anisotropy, and finite geometry. The evidence is waveform fit, travel-time residual, amplitude, phase, correlation, or inversion resolution. Any claim beyond that table should be marked as modelling proposal or interpretation.

There is a second translation table for inverse problems. In direct wave propagation, $G$ maps source to observation. In tomography or full-waveform inversion, the unknown may be the medium itself. Sensitivity kernels then describe how a perturbation in velocity, density, attenuation, or anisotropy changes a misfit. These kernels are Green's-function constructions too. If the programme speaks of the Earth as a field system with memory, inverse theory provides the technical discipline: what present observations constrain which past or present structures, and with what resolution? A "memory" that cannot be observed through an inverse kernel remains poetic.

Ambient noise interferometry is an especially suggestive example. Under suitable assumptions about diffuse wavefields and source distribution, cross-correlations of noise recorded at two stations approximate the Green's function between them.<!-- REF-NEEDED: Michel Campillo and Anne Paul, "Long-range correlations in the diffuse seismic coda", Science, 2003; Nikolai M. The programme's impulse-response language maps naturally here `derived-under-assumptions`. Yet the assumptions are strong: source distribution, scattering, equipartition, preprocessing, seasonal variability, and anisotropic noise all matter. Again, the good version is precise.

## Empirical Green's functions and the discipline of approximation

Empirical Green's functions provide a useful model for how [T]-Theory should behave. In earthquake seismology, a small event near a larger event can sometimes be used as an empirical Green's function for the path and site effects shared by both. Deconvolving or scaling the small event helps isolate the source process of the larger one. The method is powerful because it does not pretend to know the whole Earth. It uses a nearby, similar path as a pragmatic kernel. Its limitations are equally instructive: source similarity, location mismatch, focal mechanism differences, finite-frequency effects, and noise all matter.

That attitude is exactly what the programme needs. A propagator is rarely a perfect Platonic object in applied geophysics. It is an estimated, filtered, band-limited, windowed, regularised, uncertainty-bearing object. If a scale-invariant theory cannot tolerate that messiness, it will fail. A mature geological [T]-Theory should speak about approximate kernels, confidence intervals, model covariance, resolution limits, and bias. The fact that the formal master equation is clean does not make the measured Earth clean.

The same lesson appears in receiver functions, site response, and coda methods. Each extracts some aspect of the medium's response under assumptions. Receiver functions separate converted phases to infer discontinuities; site-response studies estimate how shallow structure modifies shaking; coda waves sample scattering and attenuation. None is "the" Green's function in full. Each is a disciplined partial view. The programme's language of field response should therefore be plural: propagators for different observables, bands, windows, and approximations.

Full-waveform inversion makes this explicit. The forward model maps structure and source to data; the adjoint field maps residuals back into model updates; sensitivity kernels show where data constrain the Earth. The procedure is not a direct photograph of the planet but an iterative negotiation between data, physics, parameterisation, regularisation, and starting model. If the programme uses inversion as an analogy for reading geological memory, it should also inherit this humility. Memory is not recovered whole. It is inferred under constraints.

There is a philosophical payoff here. [T]-Theory often speaks as though the propagator is the field's answer to a question. Seismology teaches that the answer depends on the question's bandwidth, aperture, noise, geometry, and prior assumptions. A sparse network asks a different question from a dense array. A teleseismic body wave asks a different question from ambient noise. A strong-motion accelerometer in a basin asks a different question from a global normal-mode record. The medium has no single response independent of interrogation. This does not refute the propagator idea; it makes it operational `interpretive`.

## Source, path, site: three places for co-identification to fail

Seismologists often divide a record into source, path, and site contributions. The source describes rupture geometry, moment rate, directivity, stress drop, and focal mechanism. The path describes propagation through the Earth: velocity, attenuation, scattering, anisotropy, interfaces. The site describes local amplification, basin effects, topography, and near-surface nonlinearity. This decomposition is imperfect but practical. It also offers three distinct places where a cross-domain mapping can fail.

At the source, an earthquake is not merely a point impulse. Finite faults rupture with evolving slip, rupture velocity, healing, branching, and heterogeneity. Treating every event as a delta source may be adequate for some far-field purposes and wrong for near-fault hazard. If the programme's source term $J(t)$ is too simple, it will misrepresent rupture physics. The source is an operator in its own right, not only an input.

Along the path, scattering and attenuation alter phase and amplitude. A Green's function in a strongly heterogeneous crust may be multiply scattered, anisotropic, frequency-dependent, and sensitive to small-scale structure. The programme's scale-invariant form can accommodate this only if it allows the operator to become complex and nonlocal where needed. A scalar $1/r$ decay is not enough for coda waves, basin reverberations, or fractured media.

At the site, local geology can dominate damage. Soft sediments, basin edges, weathered layers, and topography may amplify shaking far beyond what regional source magnitude suggests. This is a reminder that boundary conditions are not decorative. They determine lived hazard. A geological [T]-Theory interested in propagators should therefore be naturally allied with seismic hazard analysis: it asks how a medium shapes response. But it must respect the empirical engineering literature rather than replacing it with a universal phrase.

The source-path-site decomposition also clarifies the programme's broader co-identification. If a social, neural, or geological system has a propagator, one still has to ask what counts as source, path, and site in that system. For faults, these are literal enough. For other domains, they may be only analogical. Geophysics can thus supply an audit template for the whole programme `interpretive`.

The chapter's conclusion is therefore simple. The seismic propagator is not a speculative import from the Soma-Field. It is standard seismology. The programme adds value only if it uses this native object as a constraint on the larger theory. If a cross-scale claim contradicts what is known about elastic Green's functions, attenuation, moment tensors, or inversion, the geological case should fail first. That is not a threat to the programme; it is the only way for the geological volume to be scientifically useful `open-hypothesis`.

# Faults, Friction, and Tectonic Criticality

The most tempting geological claim in the programme is that earthquakes are phase transitions. The temptation is understandable. A locked fault accumulates elastic strain; a small nucleation patch grows; dynamic rupture propagates; stress drops; radiated energy and frictional heat carry the transition away; aftershocks reorganise the surrounding stress field. The difference between interseismic quiet and coseismic rupture is abrupt, collective, and threshold-like. Critical language seems natural. But seismology has spent decades learning that natural language about thresholds can mislead.

The Gutenberg-Richter law is the usual entry point. In its cumulative form, $\log_{10}N(M)=a-bM$, the number of earthquakes at or above magnitude $M$ decreases approximately exponentially with magnitude, which corresponds to a power law in seismic moment. The $a$-value reflects productivity and catalogue scale; the $b$-value is commonly near one but varies. Low $b$-values are often associated with higher differential stress or larger relative proportion of big events; high $b$-values with swarms, volcanic settings, induced seismicity, or heterogeneous stress and material conditions. These associations are useful but not universal.

Some programme drafts have claimed that $b\approx1$ follows from the universality class of the somatic tensor field. In this book that sentence must be softened. No derivation is available here that takes an elastic-frictional fault ensemble, specifies its state variables and symmetries, computes a critical exponent, converts that exponent into a seismological $b$-value, and predicts regional variation. Until such a derivation exists, the Gutenberg-Richter connection is an `open-hypothesis`. It may be a valuable target. It is not yet a result.

Self-organised criticality is the closest existing debate. The sandpile image suggested that slowly driven threshold systems naturally evolve to a critical state in which events of all sizes occur without a characteristic scale. Earthquakes look similar in their frequency-magnitude statistics, clustering, and cascade behaviour. But faults are not simple sandpiles. They have long-range elastic interactions, dynamic weakening, fluids, thermal pressurisation, rate-dependent friction, geometrical segmentation, inherited roughness, off-fault damage, finite seismogenic thickness, aseismic creep, and catalogues with detection thresholds. Whether the crust is "self-organised critical" depends on which of these features are essential and which can be renormalised away.

The programme can improve its criticality language by refusing to use SOC as a slogan. A better claim is: some seismic observables resemble critical phenomena; the programme proposes an energy-landscape and propagator notation in which spectral-gap closure, correlation length, and barrier crossing can be stated; this notation should be compared against SOC, ETAS, rate-and-state, and characteristic-earthquake alternatives `open-hypothesis`. That comparison is a research programme, not a conclusion.

Omori decay is the second statistical pillar. After a mainshock, aftershock rates commonly follow $n(t)=K/(c+t)^p$. The modified law captures short-time regularisation by $c$ and variable decay by $p$. The law is compatible with several mechanisms: stress corrosion, afterslip, rate-and-state response to stress steps, viscoelastic relaxation, pore-fluid diffusion, and branching cascades. ETAS treats earthquakes as events that trigger further events according to empirical productivity, temporal, spatial, and magnitude kernels. It is phenomenological but powerful. A Green's-function account of aftershocks must therefore say whether it is replacing ETAS, deriving ETAS kernels, or adding a physical stress/state layer beneath them.

Rate-and-state friction offers a more mechanistic route. In the Dieterich seismicity-rate formulation, a sudden Coulomb stress change modifies seismicity rate through a state variable whose evolution depends on stressing rate and the parameter $A\sigma$.<!-- REF-NEEDED: James H. Dieterich, "A constitutive law for rate of earthquake production and its application to earthquake clustering", Journal of Geophysical Research, 1994 --> This produces aftershock-like decays without needing each aftershock to be a direct independent clock. It also connects to laboratory friction. If [T]-Theory's memory kernel is to be geological, it should probably pass through this door. The "field memory" of a fault is not just a poetic trace of past ruptures. It is the evolving distribution of contact age, real area of contact, gouge fabric, damage, pore pressure, chemical healing, and stress heterogeneity.

## Rate-and-state friction as a correspondence principle

The rate-and-state framework is a useful correspondence principle for this volume. In physics, a new theory is expected to reduce to a successful older theory in the regime where the older theory works. The same should be demanded here. Where laboratory friction and rate-and-state models explain observed behaviour, [T]-Theory should reduce to them or explicitly state why it differs. This is not optional. It is the price of entering fault mechanics.

The state variable $\theta$ is especially important because it already carries memory. In the ageing law, stationary contact allows state to grow; in the slip law, state evolves with slip. The critical slip distance $D_c$ sets a memory length. Velocity weakening, $a-b<0$, allows instability under appropriate stiffness; velocity strengthening promotes stable sliding. These are not metaphors for memory and stability; they are experimentally grounded parameters. If the programme wants a Hopfield-like memory matrix, it should show how such parameters populate or constrain it.

One can imagine a hierarchy. At the smallest level, contact populations age, break, and reform. At an intermediate level, gouge fabric and shear localisation evolve. At a fault-patch level, rate-and-state variables control stability. At a network level, elastic stress transfer couples patches. At a catalogue level, event statistics emerge. A Hopfield landscape might be a reduced description of this hierarchy. But the reduction must preserve what matters: frictional stability, stress conservation, pore pressure, normal stress, and geometry. A reduction that erases friction is not a geological theory.

The Dieterich aftershock formulation is also a warning against unnecessary novelty. It already converts a stress step into a time-dependent seismicity rate. If the programme claims a memory kernel explains aftershocks, it should compare directly with Dieterich's rate-state kernel. Does it reproduce the same form? Does it add spatial structure? Does it handle variable stressing rate or aseismic slip better? Does it predict where ETAS parameters should change? The answer may be yes, but only a head-to-head comparison will tell.

Laboratory data should be the first court of appeal. Stick-slip experiments can vary normal stress, stiffness, humidity, temperature, gouge, loading rate, and fluid pressure. Acoustic emissions give catalogues with known boundary conditions. High-speed imaging and strain measurements can reveal nucleation. If [T]-Theory cannot identify its $q$, $V(q)$, and effective noise scale in such systems, it is unlikely to do so first on the San Andreas or Nankai megathrust. Conversely, if it succeeds in the lab, it earns the right to scale up cautiously.

## Slow slip, tremor, and mixed modes

Any field account of faults must handle more than ordinary earthquakes. Slow slip events release strain over days to months with little high-frequency radiation. Tectonic tremor consists of low-amplitude, emergent signals often associated with deep slow slip. Very-low-frequency earthquakes, low-frequency earthquakes, repeating earthquakes, and aseismic creep occupy a spectrum between locked and dynamically rupturing behaviour. Subduction zones in particular show that the boundary between seismic and aseismic slip is not binary.

This spectrum is good news for an energy-landscape theory. It suggests that faults can traverse the landscape by different paths: abrupt inertial rupture, stable creep, episodic slow slip, tremor-mediated release, or mixed cascades. A single threshold model is too simple. The programme's language of basins and barriers could help describe why one region releases energy seismically and another aseismically under similar loading `open-hypothesis`. But it must incorporate frictional stability, effective normal stress, temperature, fluids, and lithology.

Slow slip also offers a better testing ground for memory than large earthquakes. Geodetic time series observe repeated slow slip cycles in some regions; tremor catalogues provide high-resolution timing; models can estimate slip distributions. If the programme predicts that previous slow slip changes the barrier for future episodes, or that a spectral gap narrows before slow slip onset, those predictions might be testable within years rather than centuries. The same is true for repeating earthquakes on creeping faults, where nearly identical sources reveal small-scale repeating failure of asperities.

Mixed modes also complicate Gutenberg-Richter interpretation. If part of the strain budget is released aseismically, event-size statistics alone do not describe the full energy landscape. A region with high tremor and slow slip may have different seismic b-values not because the universal exponent changed but because the system partitions deformation differently. A geological [T]-Theory should therefore include aseismic degrees of freedom in its state vector. Otherwise it risks fitting the shadow of the process rather than the process.

## Fluids, temperature, and effective stress

Faults are not dry elastic cracks in an otherwise simple solid. Fluids alter effective normal stress, promote or inhibit mineral reactions, change permeability, and mediate pressure diffusion. Elevated pore pressure can bring faults closer to failure; dehydration reactions in subduction zones may contribute fluids; hydrothermal systems produce swarms; injection can induce seismicity. Thermal processes such as flash heating and thermal pressurisation can dynamically weaken faults. These effects are central, not secondary.

For the programme, fluids and temperature are where the phrase "effective field" must earn its keep. An energy landscape for a fault cannot be only elastic strain energy. It must include effective stress, pore-pressure evolution, thermal state, chemical reactions, and permeability feedbacks where relevant. The "barrier" to rupture may be lowered by pressure diffusion or thermal weakening rather than by an abstract topological operation. If those processes can be represented in a reduced potential, the reduction should show how.

Induced seismicity again offers a test. Injection changes pore pressure and effective stress in partly measurable ways. Seismicity often migrates; b-values and rates can change; shut-in may or may not stop events immediately. A propagator model could describe pressure diffusion, poroelastic stress changes, and earthquake triggering in one operator framework. But this is already an active field with physical models. The programme must add predictive structure or conceptual clarity, not merely rename pressure diffusion as a somatic wave.

The same caution applies to volcanic and geothermal settings. Swarms, tremor, and deformation may be driven by fluids and magma rather than tectonic stress alone. They are attractive cases for field models because sources are distributed and media are responsive. They are also dangerous for over-generalisation because multiple mechanisms produce similar statistics. A geological soma that ignores fluids would be too dry to describe the Earth.

In rate-and-state language, nucleation occurs when a velocity-weakening patch exceeds a critical size or when stiffness falls below a frictional stability threshold. The details depend on $a-b$, normal stress, critical slip distance $D_c$, shear modulus, effective stiffness, geometry, and loading. This is already a barrier problem in a broad sense: the system can remain stable under small perturbations but become unstable when state and stress pass a condition. The programme's WKB expression becomes interesting only if it yields a calculable correction or alternative to the rate-and-state nucleation rate `open-hypothesis`.

One possible translation is to treat $q$ as a low-dimensional reaction coordinate extracted from a high-dimensional fault-state vector. That vector might include average slip deficit, maximum shear stress, state variable distribution, pore pressure, damage, and a measure of locked-patch coherence. The potential $V(q)$ would not be a literal gravitational potential but an effective quasipotential or large-deviation landscape estimated from simulations, laboratory experiments, or data-assimilating models. The "energy" $E$ would be the current driving level, and $\hbar_{\text{geo}}$ would be an effective noise/action parameter. In stochastic dynamical systems this is not absurd. But every word in that sentence requires operational work.

The language of WKB should also be compared with Kramers escape and Freidlin-Wentzell large deviations. Rare transitions in noisy systems often have probabilities proportional to $\exp(-S/D)$, where $S$ is an action or barrier and $D$ a noise amplitude. The programme's proposed

$$
P_{\text{nucl}}\approx \exp\left[-\frac{2}{\hbar_{\text{geo}}}
\int_{q_1}^{q_2}\sqrt{V(q)-E}\,dq\right]
$$

has the right exponential sensitivity to barrier shape, but its exact square-root action is imported from semiclassical tunnelling. For geological systems the more defensible claim is that an exponential action law may describe nucleation probability under a suitable reduction `open-hypothesis`. Literal quantum terminology should be avoided unless a genuine quantum mechanism is being asserted, which this book does not assert.

The physical processes at a fault tip are not merely thermal noise crossing a smooth barrier. They include dilatancy, compaction, flash heating, thermal pressurisation, dynamic weakening, acoustic fluidisation hypotheses, off-fault plasticity, rupture directivity, supershear transition, roughness interactions, and fluid pressure changes. Some earthquakes begin with slow nucleation; others appear abrupt at available resolution. Slow slip and tremor show that faults can release strain aseismically or in mixed modes. Induced seismicity shows how fluid pressure and anthropogenic operations alter failure conditions. A [T]-Theory nucleation model must be humble before this diversity.

The programme's "spectral gap" language may be more promising than its "tunnelling" rhetoric. In a linearised system near stability, eigenvalues of the governing operator determine whether perturbations decay or grow. A locked fault approaching instability might be described by decreasing stability margins, increasing susceptibility, longer correlation lengths, or growing coherent modes. In laboratory stick-slip and numerical simulations, one can sometimes observe precursory localisation, accelerating acoustic emissions, changing b-values, or critical slowing down. A spectral-gap proposal could ask whether a measurable operator inferred from microseismicity, geodesy, or lab sensors loses its gap before failure `open-hypothesis`.

This connects naturally to aftershock spatial kernels. The generated anchor paper suggested an $e^{-kr}/r$ Yukawa-like decay. In a homogeneous scalar screened medium that form is familiar. Real aftershock zones, however, follow fault geometry, stress lobes, damage zones, depth limits, and network topology. A single isotropic radial kernel is unlikely to survive contact with data except as a first approximation. A stronger version would say: the aftershock spatial density should follow the anisotropic elastic or rate-state response kernel appropriate to the medium, and the scalar Yukawa form is a pedagogical special case. That version is testable and geophysically literate `derived-under-assumptions`.

Pre-seismic electromagnetic anomalies require even greater care. Reports of electric, magnetic, ionospheric, or luminous precursors have a complicated history, with problems of noise, retrospective selection, non-tectonic sources, instrumentation, and reproducibility. A programme claim that EM anomalies are "CEMI-like fluctuations" is at best an open hypothesis. To become science, it must specify frequency bands, sensor geometry, source mechanisms, controls for cultural and industrial noise, solar and ionospheric conditions, hydrological effects, and blind prospective tests. Otherwise it belongs to the long catalogue of earthquake precursor claims that looked suggestive after the fact `open-hypothesis`.

The criticality debate also intersects with the characteristic earthquake model. Some faults appear to have repeated ruptures of similar size on similar segments; palaeoseismic trenches show event histories that can look quasi-periodic; segmentation and barriers matter. Other catalogues look scale-free over broad ranges. The field has never been cleanly divided between "critical" and "characteristic"; both patterns can appear depending on scale, fault maturity, loading, and observation window. The programme's Hopfield landscape can accommodate both: a deep attractor may generate recurrent similar events, while a rough landscape near instability may generate scale-free cascades. But accommodating both is not predicting either.

For this reason, the geological use of "phase transition" should be operational. A phase transition in physics involves an order parameter, control parameter, susceptibility, correlation length, symmetry or topology change, and finite-size behaviour. What is the earthquake order parameter? Possibilities include slip rate, coherent rupture area, stress-drop connectivity, fraction of a fault patch in dynamic weakening, or a largest-cluster measure in a damage network. What is the control parameter? Shear stress, state variable, pore pressure, loading rate, effective temperature, or a composite? What diverges or peaks? Without answers, "phase transition" remains evocative `interpretive`.

The programme has one advantage here: it is willing to state failure conditions. A geological [T]-Theory should fail if WKB/action laws do not improve nucleation-rate models under pre-registered tests; if inferred spectral gaps do not behave differently before failure than in matched non-failure intervals; if b-value variations are fully explained by known catalogue and stress effects with no residual predicted by the proposed coupling variables; if aftershock kernels do not match the claimed propagator forms; or if the mapping to rate-and-state variables cannot be made dimensionally coherent. A theory that cannot lose cannot help seismology.

## Statistical baselines and the danger of hindsight

Earthquake catalogues invite hindsight. A cluster occurs, and afterwards one can often find a b-value change, a quiescence patch, a foreshock sequence, a geodetic anomaly, a gas signal, an electromagnetic fluctuation, or a pattern in small events. Some of these may be physically meaningful. Many will be products of multiple testing, catalogue incompleteness, changing detection thresholds, or ordinary clustering. Any new programme must protect itself against retrospective success.

The statistical baseline should therefore be explicit. For short-term clustering, ETAS or related branching models are natural nulls. For stress-mediated triggering, Coulomb and rate-state models are natural comparisons. For operational forecasting, one should compare against established prospective forecast experiments and scoring rules. For b-value studies, the null must include magnitude completeness, finite sample effects, spatial smoothing choices, and uncertainty in magnitude estimates. For laboratory failure, null models include standard frictional stability and acoustic-emission criticality analyses. A [T]-Theory prediction is useful only if it beats an appropriate null.

Prospective testing is particularly important for proposed precursors. A pre-seismic anomaly that is identified after the earthquake is not the same as an anomaly declared before the earthquake in a locked protocol. The protocol should define station selection, preprocessing, alarm thresholds, spatial and temporal windows, target magnitude range, and evaluation metrics. It should also count misses and false alarms. This is the difference between a research hypothesis and a story about a past event.

The WKB proposal lends itself to prospective form if made quantitative. Given a data stream and a fault-state estimate, it should output a rate multiplier or probability curve. That curve can be scored against observed events and non-events. If the model only explains why an event was likely after the event occurred, it has failed the operational test. If it raises probabilities too often, it may be scientifically interesting but operationally poor. The book does not demand immediate hazard utility, but it does demand honest scoring.

This statistical discipline should also be applied to claims of universality. Power laws are common in complex systems, but not every power law shares a universality class. Fitting a straight line on a log plot is not enough. One needs range, uncertainty, alternative distributions, finite-size cut-off, catalogue bias, and generative mechanism. The programme's scale-free language will be credible only when it distinguishes genuine scaling from broad but non-universal variability.

The chapter's positive proposal is therefore disciplined synthesis. Use Gutenberg-Richter and Omori not as trophies but as constraints. Use SOC not as a settled foundation but as a debated analogue. Use rate-and-state friction as the first required translation layer. Use WKB only as an effective rare-transition formalism. Use Hopfield energy landscapes only when the stored geological variables can be named. Use spectral gaps only when the operator and data window are specified. Then the geological soma becomes a research programme rather than a metaphor.

# Geological Memory, Inversion, and Tests

Geologists have always worked with memory. A bedding plane, an unconformity, a metamorphic fabric, a fault gouge, a pseudotachylyte vein, a coral terrace, a displaced channel, a turbidite, a varve, a speleothem, a thermochronological age, a palaeomagnetic direction, a cosmogenic exposure date: each is a present trace from which a past state is inferred. Seismologists do the same with waveforms. A seismogram is not the earthquake; it is the medium's recorded response to the earthquake. Geophysics is therefore already an art of reading memory through a propagator.

The [T]-Theory phrase "rock strata as geological memory" is acceptable if read in this broad sense `interpretive`. It becomes misleading if it suggests that strata store information the way a brain stores autobiographical memory. Rocks record by preservation, deformation, mineral reaction, damage, burial, erosion, and exposure. Their "forgetting" occurs by metamorphism, recrystallisation, diffusion, dissolution, overprinting, erosion, and tectonic reworking. The analogy to a memory kernel is useful only when one specifies the retention and erasure mechanisms.

Fault zones are especially memory-rich. Repeated slip localises in zones of weakness; gouge evolves; asperities break and heal; permeability changes; fluids migrate; mineral reactions alter friction; damage zones widen or narrow; roughness is worn down or preserved; previous ruptures leave stress heterogeneity and structural inheritance. Palaeoseismology reads past surface ruptures from colluvial wedges, offset layers, liquefaction features, tsunami deposits, and turbidites. These are not metaphors. They are physical records. The programme's Hopfield language becomes a compact way to say that the coupling matrix of the future system is changed by the past system `derived-under-assumptions`.

In a Hopfield network, patterns are stored in weights. A present input falls into an attractor basin shaped by those weights. For a fault, the analogue of weights might be spatial variations in frictional properties, normal stress, damage, roughness, lithology, pore pressure pathways, and elastic coupling. A previous rupture can lower resistance along part of a surface, increase stress on adjacent patches, seal or open fluid pathways, or create geometrical complexity that arrests later ruptures. In this sense, past events alter the landscape through which future events move. The analogy is legitimate as a modelling prompt. It is not yet a calibrated associative-memory theory of faults.

The clearest way to harden it is through state variables. Rate-and-state friction already gives one variable, but a real fault may need many. A "geological memory vector" could include contact age, gouge maturity, damage density, permeability, mineral alteration, healing state, cumulative slip, roughness spectrum, and residual stress. Laboratory experiments can observe some of these under controlled conditions; field studies can infer others from core, outcrop, geodesy, and seismic imaging. The programme's matrix $W$ would then be a parameterised coupling among these variables, not a mystical field of the Earth.

The same translation applies to stratigraphy. A sedimentary basin is a memory system because accommodation, sediment supply, subsidence, compaction, eustasy, climate, and tectonics are integrated into preserved layers. But the record is incomplete and biased. Hiatuses, erosion, non-deposition, diagenesis, and structural repetition complicate the archive. The anchor paper's comparison between unconformities and dissociation is evocative but should be labelled `interpretive`. A safer phrase is: an unconformity is a discontinuity in the preserved record; as an analogy it helps readers think about missing intervals, but it is not a psychological mechanism.

Geophysical inversion makes memory quantitative. A seismic image is an inference from present data to subsurface structure through a forward operator. Resolution matrices, point-spread functions, posterior covariances, and sensitivity kernels tell us what can be known. If the programme claims a fault stores a pattern, inversion asks whether that pattern is observable. If the pattern is below resolution or non-unique, the claim cannot carry empirical weight. This is an important correction to programme rhetoric: not every real memory is accessible; not every accessible pattern is uniquely interpretable.

## The archive is selective

Geological memory is not a faithful recording device. It is selective, lossy, and actively rewritten. Erosion removes records. Metamorphism overprints mineral assemblages. Fault reactivation reuses old weaknesses for new motions. Fluids dissolve and precipitate. Compaction changes porosity. Bioturbation disturbs sediment. Human land use destroys traces. Even when a record is preserved, dating uncertainty, correlation ambiguity, and structural complexity limit inference. A theory of geological memory must therefore include forgetting and distortion, not only storage.

This selectivity is geologically ordinary but theoretically important. A Hopfield network retrieves stored patterns because its weights encode them in a particular way. A geological medium preserves patterns only when physical processes permit. Some events leave strong traces; others leave none. A large earthquake under the sea may be recorded by turbidites, tsunami deposits, coral uplift, geodetic offsets, and changed stress. Another event of similar magnitude may leave an ambiguous or erased record. The "memory kernel" of the Earth is therefore medium-specific and observation-specific.

The programme's temporal-dynamics language can help if it treats memory as a kernel with decay, bias, and convolution. A stress perturbation may relax by afterslip, viscoelastic flow, fluid diffusion, or damage healing. A sedimentary signal may be filtered by accommodation and erosion. A seismic coda may retain scattering information over seconds to minutes. These are all memory effects, but their kernels differ. The word "memory" should always be followed by a mechanism and a timescale.

This selectivity also matters ethically and practically. Earthquake hazard often relies on incomplete records. A palaeoseismic trench may expose a few events over a few thousand years; a historical catalogue may be biased by settlement and literacy; instrumental catalogues are short relative to recurrence. The programme should not imply that a hidden field memory can overcome sparse data by conceptual elegance. It should instead help organise uncertainty: what does the medium remember, what have we measured, what have we missed, and how does that uncertainty enter hazard?

## From poetic memory to measurable state

The path from "geological memory" to science is a sequence of reductions. First, choose a system: a laboratory fault, a creeping segment, a subduction interface, a geothermal reservoir, a basin, or an exhumed fault zone. Second, define the state variables that carry memory. Third, define how those variables evolve under loading, slip, healing, fluids, temperature, and chemical change. Fourth, define observables: acoustic emissions, slip, stress drop, recurrence, waveform change, b-value, tremor, permeability, microstructure. Fifth, specify the kernel or coupling matrix that maps past state to future response. Sixth, test whether including the memory improves prediction or explanation over memoryless or simpler models.

This sequence is demanding, but it is not alien to Earth science. Hydrologists estimate aquifer memory from storage and transmissivity. Geodesists estimate postseismic relaxation from viscoelastic and afterslip kernels. Seismologists measure temporal changes in velocity using coda-wave interferometry. Rock physicists study healing and frictional ageing. Stratigraphers infer accommodation history. The programme's contribution is to put these examples in one conceptual frame: the present response of a geological medium depends on stored state `interpretive`.

One concrete example is coda-wave monitoring. Small changes in seismic velocity can be detected by comparing scattered waveforms over time. Stress changes, damage, fluids, and temperature may alter the medium. In this case the "memory" is not ancient stratigraphy but a short-term change in the scattering medium. Another example is repeating earthquakes, where nearly identical waveforms imply repeated rupture of the same asperity. Changes in recurrence interval and slip can reveal loading and aseismic creep. These are naturally memory-and-response phenomena.

Another example is postseismic deformation. After a large earthquake, GPS and InSAR may record months to years of motion due to afterslip, poroelastic rebound, and viscoelastic relaxation. Each mechanism has a temporal kernel. Disentangling them is difficult but standard. The programme's memory-kernel notation could be useful here if it encourages clear separation of kernels and observables. It would be unhelpful if it merely labels all relaxation as the Earth's soma recovering.

At longer timescales, thermochronology and structural geology record exhumation, burial, and deformation. These are not seismic memories, but they are geological state histories. A scale-indexed theory should not force them into one fault-nucleation model. It should allow different kernels at different timescales. The Earth has many memories, not one.

The first proposed test concerns WKB-like nucleation in controlled systems. Natural earthquakes are difficult because the state is poorly observed and recurrence is slow. Laboratory stick-slip experiments, biaxial fault apparatus, rotary shear, and acoustic-emission monitored rock failure offer better initial targets. There one can control normal stress, loading rate, pore pressure, roughness, temperature, and material; record acoustic emissions; image deformation; and observe repeated failures. A reaction coordinate $q$ could be learned from the experiment, and an action-like variable could be tested against failure probability. Success in the lab would not prove natural prediction, but failure in the lab would be informative.

The second test concerns induced seismicity. Fluid injection, reservoir impoundment, geothermal stimulation, mining, and hydrocarbon operations create catalogues where stressing changes are partly known. They also create ethical and practical urgency. A [T]-Theory model could test whether an action/barrier term improves forecasts over ETAS, Coulomb/rate-state, and operational traffic-light baselines. The prediction should be pre-registered: input data, training period, forecast window, magnitude threshold, spatial grid, evaluation score, and null models. Without that discipline, apparent improvement can be manufactured.

The third test concerns b-value variation. The programme suggests that $b$-value deviations might correlate with coupling parameters. A geophysical version would specify candidate parameters: differential stress proxies, heat flow, fault maturity, roughness, lithology, pore pressure, locking fraction, strain rate, depth, and catalogue completeness. It would then compare whether programme-derived variables explain residual b-value structure beyond known controls. The test must handle magnitude-of-completeness variation, short catalogue bias, spatial smoothing, and aftershock contamination. A single map with suggestive colours is not enough.

The fourth test concerns Omori and aftershock kernels. A propagator-based model should compete with ETAS and rate-and-state seismicity-rate formulations. Does it predict temporal decay exponent $p$, productivity, spatial anisotropy, stress-shadow effects, secondary triggering, and finite fault geometry? Can it distinguish afterslip-driven sequences from cascade-driven sequences? Does it predict when aftershock zones expand, contract, or migrate? If the programme only reproduces a generic inverse-time decay after fitting free parameters, it has not added much. If it predicts a kernel from independently measured structure, it has.

The fifth test concerns spectral-gap precursors. Dense seismic arrays and laboratory experiments can be used to construct evolving correlation, covariance, or interaction operators. One can ask whether leading eigenvalues, coherence lengths, susceptibility, or network measures change systematically before failure. Similar ideas already exist in critical-point and acoustic-emission studies; the programme's contribution would be to define a particular operator and failure criterion. The hard part is false positives: many systems become more coherent or noisier without failing. A useful precursor must beat matched controls.

The sixth test concerns electromagnetic anomalies, but this should be last rather than first. A rigorous protocol would require blind prospective monitoring, independent non-tectonic controls, solar-geomagnetic filtering, industrial noise characterisation, and pre-specified frequency-domain predictions. The programme's "imaginary pole" phrase should be turned into a measurable spectral peak or dispersion relation. Until then, pre-seismic EM claims remain `open-hypothesis` and should not be used as public-facing evidence.

The seventh test concerns geological memory decay. Drill core from active faults, exhumed fault zones, experimental gouge, and microstructural studies can constrain healing, pressure solution, grain growth, cementation, and damage annealing. If the programme predicts that recurrence memory decays with a diffusion-like or reaction-rate time constant, it should specify which mechanism dominates under which temperature, pressure, fluid, and mineralogical conditions. A mature version might connect laboratory healing laws to palaeoseismic recurrence variability. This would be a genuine contribution because it would join microphysics to long-term hazard.

The eighth test concerns basin and topographic Green's functions. Here the programme is on safe ground because wave amplification and resonance are already observable. Sedimentary basins amplify shaking; valleys trap waves; topography scatters; site response matters for hazard. The programme can use these as didactic cases of boundary-conditioned propagation without making new claims. If it wants novelty, it should propose a way to transfer intuition from these well-measured systems to less obvious memory systems, while keeping the evidence label modest.

The ninth test concerns cross-scale transfer itself. The programme says that a response grammar recurs across scales. A geophysicist can ask whether this grammar actually constrains models or merely redescribes them. A useful cross-scale theory should produce parameter correspondences, invariants, dimensionless groups, or failure modes that would not have been noticed otherwise. For example, it might identify when a social contagion kernel and an aftershock kernel are formally similar but differ in conservation, causality, and branching assumptions. That negative discrimination is as valuable as positive analogy.

## A pre-registration template for the geological programme

To make these tests real, the geological programme should adopt a pre-registration template. The first field is the target system: laboratory granite stick-slip, induced seismicity in a named reservoir, tremor in a named subduction zone, repeating earthquakes on a creeping fault, or regional catalogue b-values. The second is the baseline model: rate-and-state, ETAS, Coulomb stress, pressure diffusion, standard frictional stability, or a null statistical forecast. The third is the [T]-Theory addition: action term, memory matrix, spectral gap, propagator kernel, or scale-invariant parameter.

The fourth field is the observable. Examples include event rate, magnitude distribution, failure time distribution, acoustic-emission energy, slip velocity, geodetic displacement, waveform correlation, inferred stress change, or microstructural healing. The fifth is the prediction in a form that can fail: a sign, scaling exponent, curve shape, likelihood improvement, timing window, spatial anisotropy, or parameter correlation. The sixth is the exclusion criterion: what result would count as no support or active disconfirmation?

The seventh field is uncertainty. Earth data are incomplete. A pre-registration should state magnitude completeness, location uncertainty, focal mechanism uncertainty, geodetic noise, catalogue duration, laboratory sensor limits, model priors, and multiple-testing correction. The eighth is replication: what independent dataset, material, region, or laboratory would be tested next? The ninth is interpretation: if the prediction succeeds, which evidence label changes, and which does not? A successful lab action law might upgrade one nucleation model, not the entire Universal Somatic Field.

This template may seem bureaucratic, but it is the only way to protect a cross-scale theory from its own flexibility. A theory with many analogies can explain almost anything after the fact. A pre-registered test makes it choose. The result may be negative. That is acceptable. A failed geological prediction would be valuable because it would locate where the co-identification breaks: wrong state variable, wrong kernel, wrong scale, wrong analogy, or wrong theory.

## What would count as success?

Success has levels. The lowest useful success would be conceptual: the programme helps geophysicists formulate a clearer memory or response model, even if no new prediction follows. This is `interpretive` value. A stronger success would be model-comparative: adding a programme-derived memory or action term improves likelihood, forecast score, or explanatory parsimony over a baseline in a specified dataset. That would be `derived-under-assumptions` until replicated. A stronger success still would be prospective prediction in a laboratory or induced setting. That could become an `empirical-result`.

The strongest geological success would not be deterministic earthquake prediction. It would be a robust, independently replicated relation linking measurable fault-state variables to transition probabilities through an action or spectral-gap law, outperforming existing baselines in appropriate regimes. Even then, the result would remain a geophysical model, not evidence that all other [T]-Theory domains are correct. Cross-scale programmes earn trust locally before they earn trust globally.

Failure also has levels. If the WKB action cannot be operationalised, that weakens the nucleation proposal. If it works in the lab but fails in natural settings, it may mark a scale boundary. If b-value variation is fully explained by known stress and catalogue effects, the universality claim should be withdrawn or reformulated. If electromagnetic anomalies fail prospective tests, they should be removed from the evidence base. A serious programme should be willing to lose these parts.

The final test is philosophical but important: does the programme make better scientists? A field-theoretic vocabulary can help researchers see hidden assumptions about sources, media, boundaries, and observables. It can also seduce them into thinking that naming a Green's function solves a problem. This book argues for the first use and against the second. The geological soma is useful only if it increases the precision of the question: which medium, which operator, which memory, which observable, which null model, which failure condition?

If this discipline is followed, the programme's strongest geological claim becomes quite modest and potentially fruitful. Seismology is already a science of Green's functions and memory-bearing media. [T]-Theory offers a cross-scale notation that may help formulate rare-transition and geological-memory hypotheses. Those hypotheses must be tested first in systems where the state is observable, then against natural catalogues, and always against existing seismological models `open-hypothesis`. The Earth may be a field system with long memory, but the memory has to be read with the tools of Earth science.

# Reading Seismic Propagation and Tectonic Criticality

The anchor paper is the book's most direct geological statement. # Conclusion: The Earth as Field System

The geological version of [T]-Theory stands or falls on whether it can remain useful after its metaphors have been stripped away. The remaining core is clear. Seismic propagation is a Green's-function science. Faults are history-dependent frictional systems. Earthquake catalogues display scale-rich statistics. Aftershocks are clustered in time and space. Geological media preserve, erase, and overprint memory. These statements are ordinary geophysics. The programme's task is to organise them through a common response-and-memory grammar without claiming more than the evidence allows.

The strongest conclusion is therefore not that [T]-Theory has derived earthquake prediction. It has not. The strongest conclusion is that geophysics offers one of the programme's best tests because the propagator is already real in the field's mathematics `derived-under-assumptions`. A seismic Green's tensor is an impulse response. A site-response function is boundary-conditioned propagation. A tomographic sensitivity kernel is a response of data to structure. An ambient-noise correlation can approximate an interstation Green's function. These are not borrowed metaphors. They are points where the programme must match an existing technical discipline.

The second conclusion is that the programme's energy-landscape vocabulary is promising but unproven. Locked faults, asperity systems, and frictional patches can be described as metastable configurations. Past slip and inherited structure influence future behaviour. A Hopfield-like description may help formalise this memory, but only after the geological variables are specified. The coupling matrix of a fault is not a feeling; it is a shorthand for frictional, elastic, hydrological, mineralogical, and geometrical couplings. That translation is the next piece of work `open-hypothesis`.

The third conclusion is that the WKB nucleation proposal should be reframed as an effective rare-transition model. Its exponential action form is plausible enough to test in controlled settings and dangerous enough to overstate in public. It should not be advertised as quantum earthquake prediction. It should be compared against Kramers escape, large-deviation theory, rate-and-state nucleation, ETAS, and Coulomb/rate-state seismicity models. If it cannot improve or discriminate against those baselines, it should be discarded. If it can, it becomes a contribution to probabilistic hazard, not prophecy.

The fourth conclusion concerns Gutenberg-Richter and Omori. Both laws are central constraints, not solved examples. A future geological [T]-Theory must either derive the b-value and its variation from a specified universality class or stop saying it has done so. It must either derive aftershock temporal and spatial kernels from a stress/state propagator or treat Omori as an empirical law outside its derivation. The honest label for both in the present volume is `open-hypothesis`.

The fifth conclusion concerns memory. Here the programme is closest to geological intuition. Earth materials record stress, temperature, fluids, deformation, erosion, and time. Fault zones inherit weakness and complexity. Stratigraphy is a partial archive. Geophysical inversion is the art of reading present responses for hidden structure. Calling this "geological soma" is interpretive, but it points to something real: the Earth is not a memoryless medium. Its present operator has been written by its past `interpretive`.

The practical research path is staged. First, translate the programme's variables into rate-and-state, fracture, and elastic language. Second, test rare-transition action laws in laboratory stick-slip and acoustic-emission systems. Third, test induced-seismicity catalogues where forcing is partly known. Fourth, examine b-value, Omori, and aftershock-kernel predictions against strict null models. Fifth, attempt natural tectonic applications only when the smaller systems have constrained the parameters. Sixth, publish failures as carefully as successes. A programme that claims universality should welcome hard local tests.

For geophysicists and seismologists, the book's invitation is not to adopt a new faith. It is to use their field as an audit of a cross-scale theory. Seismology knows how waves propagate through complicated media. It knows how hard inversion is. It knows that power laws are seductive and that prediction is unforgiving. It knows that the Earth has memory but not intention. These are exactly the disciplines [T]-Theory needs. If the programme survives them, it will have earned a geological chapter. If it does not, geophysics will have done the programme a service by locating the fault.

This also means that the book's rhetoric should be read in the direction of geophysics, not away from it. The phrase "seismic memory propagator" does not ask the reader to abandon elastodynamics for a new ontology. It asks whether the elastodynamic response, the frictional state, and the geological archive can be placed in a single bookkeeping system. The phrase "tectonic criticality" does not settle the SOC debate. It asks whether critical-like observables can be tied to a specified operator and state variable. The phrase "geological soma" does not personify the Earth. It asks whether a material body with history can be described through response, memory, and transition.

The most useful future version of this volume would include fewer slogans and more translation tables. One table would list standard seismological constructs: Green's tensor, moment tensor, site response, attenuation kernel, rate-state variable, Coulomb stress change, ETAS triggering kernel, b-value, Omori $p$, nucleation length, slow-slip recurrence, coda velocity change. A second table would list the programme constructs: propagator, source term, boundary condition, memory kernel, coupling matrix, attractor basin, spectral gap, action barrier, effective temperature. A third would say which pairings are exact, which are derived under assumptions, which are analogies, and which are merely suggestive. That table would be more valuable than another declaration of universality.

Such a table would also show where [T]-Theory has nothing new to say. If a site-response problem is already solved by standard wave propagation, the programme should simply recognise it as a native case of response theory. If ETAS fits a catalogue as well as any proposed propagator model, the programme should not claim improvement. If rate-and-state friction explains an aftershock decay under a stress step, the programme should either reproduce that result or stay silent. A universal framework earns trust as much by declining claims as by making them `interpretive`.

There is a final reason to involve geophysics. Earth science is used to deep time and incomplete records. It knows that a theory may be constrained by traces rather than direct observation. It also knows that traces are ambiguous. That combination is precisely the philosophical situation of the wider [T]-Theory programme, which moves between formal structure, simulation, testimony, and interpretation. Geophysics can teach the programme how to reason from partial records without confusing reconstruction with proof.

That lesson is practical as well as philosophical. A seismic hazard model may be useful while incomplete, provided its uncertainties are explicit and its forecasts are scored. A palaeoseismic history may guide planning while remaining underdetermined, provided alternative correlations are acknowledged. A laboratory friction law may illuminate a fault while failing to capture every natural complication. Geological reasoning is comfortable with constrained humility. That is the tone this volume recommends for [T]-Theory: propose clearly, test locally, label evidence, and let the Earth decide which parts of the mapping survive.

If the programme is right, geophysics will not be absorbed into a theory of mind; it will be one of the places where the theory learns discipline. If it is wrong, geophysics will show where response grammar stops being explanatory. Both outcomes are scientifically respectable.

That is why this book has spoken more about operators, catalogues, friction, and tests than about grand unification. A universal claim that cannot survive local expertise is not universal. A local science that can discipline a universal claim is doing exactly what science is for.

The next move belongs in laboratories, catalogues, and field campaigns, where elegant mappings become numbers, errors, comparisons, decisions, and accountable geological knowledge under independent scrutiny worldwide.

The Earth is a field system in the modest, technical sense: perturbations propagate through media governed by operators, boundaries, and material histories. It is a memory system in the geological sense: structures preserve traces of former states while erasing others. Whether those two facts can be unified into a predictive theory of tectonic criticality remains open. This volume makes the proposal precise enough to be judged `open-hypothesis`.

## A minimal first study

The first serious geological study should be deliberately small. A laboratory stick-slip system with continuous acoustic-emission monitoring is preferable to a famous plate boundary. The apparatus should record loading, normal stress, slip, acoustic events, and any available imaging of damage or strain. A baseline rate-and-state or critical-point model should be fitted first. Then a [T]-Theory action variable should be defined from the evolving state: perhaps a low-dimensional coordinate learned from acoustic-emission covariance, slip deficit, and stress. The prediction would be whether an exponential action term improves the probability model for failure time or event-size distribution in held-out experiments.

The study should pre-register the reaction coordinate, the method for estimating $V(q)$ or its analogue, the effective noise/action parameter, the forecast window, the scoring rule, and the null models. It should include negative controls: shuffled event times, non-failing intervals, altered loading paths, and materials not expected to show the same memory. It should report failure as clearly as success. If the action term works only after post-hoc tuning, it has not worked. If it generalises across runs or materials under stated conditions, one geological claim moves from attractive analogy towards model evidence `open-hypothesis`.

The second study could move to induced seismicity, where forcing histories are partly known and catalogues are dense. The programme-derived model would compete against ETAS and pressure-diffusion or Coulomb/rate-state baselines. Its output should be probabilistic, not prophetic. A useful result might be improved short-term rate forecasts, better spatial anisotropy, or a parameter that tracks the transition from swarm-like activity to larger induced events. A failed result would still constrain the theory.

Only after such work should the programme be applied to natural tectonic prediction. The temptation to start with Parkfield, Nankai, Cascadia, or the San Andreas is understandable, but the state is too hidden and the stakes too high. The programme should earn scale by scale what it claims scale by scale. That would be a geophysical version of its own zoom doctrine.

## What survives if the bold claims fail?

It is possible that the Gutenberg-Richter derivation never materialises, that the WKB nucleation formula cannot be operationalised, and that electromagnetic precursor claims fail prospective tests. If so, much of the generated geological framing would need to be retired. But not everything would be lost. The response-kernel reading of seismology remains standard. The insistence on distinguishing equation-level identity from substrate analogy remains valuable. The memory-state vocabulary may still help organise frictional and geological histories. The evidence-label discipline would still improve cross-domain theorising.

This matters because the most durable part of [T]-Theory may be methodological. It asks researchers to state what is proved, what is simulated, what is measured, what is interpreted, and what is still open. Geophysics needs that discipline when it encounters large claims about prediction, precursors, criticality, or universality. A failed overclaim can still leave behind a better audit practice.

If some bold claims succeed, the same discipline will be needed even more. A successful laboratory action law would not authorise public earthquake prediction. A successful induced-seismicity forecast would not prove consciousness as phase transition. A successful b-value correlation would not prove a universal somatic field. Each success would update a bounded claim. The programme becomes credible not by leaping from one domain to all domains, but by carrying its labels with it.

The geological soma, then, is not a final doctrine. It is an invitation to make the Earth's long memory mathematically explicit and empirically accountable. The Earth already answers perturbations with waves, deformation, relaxation, and records. The task is to ask better questions of those answers.
