# Conclusion: The Integrated Account

The Oxford PPE tradition was built on the belief that philosophy, politics, and economics illuminate each other — that a student who has engaged seriously with all three is better equipped to understand the world than a student who has specialised in one. This book has made the stronger claim: that the three disciplines are not merely illuminated by each other but governed by the same mathematical structure, and that structure is the Universal Somatic Field.

The conclusion to this volume is not a summary but an integration: a sketch of what a field-theoretic PPE would look like as a mature discipline.

## The Common Structure

Each of the three disciplines, in the field-theoretic account, reduces to a version of the same question: what is the attractor landscape, and what dynamics govern the approach to equilibrium?

Philosophy asks: what is the good for a person, and what dynamics lead a person toward or away from it? The field-theoretic answer: the good is the configuration of the somatic field in which the attractor landscape is healthy — rich in adaptive basins, free from trauma wells, ergodic and temperature-appropriate. The dynamics of the good life are the dynamics of the somatic field.

Politics asks: what is the just organisation of collective life, and what dynamics lead a society toward or away from it? The field-theoretic answer: just organisation is the institutional landscape in which the constraint field is ergodic (rule of law), rights are topologically stable (topological invariants), and the regulatory interventions are above the WKB threshold (effective). The dynamics of just governance are the dynamics of the social somatic field.

Economics asks: what is the efficient organisation of production and exchange, and what dynamics lead a market toward or away from it? The field-theoretic answer: efficiency is attractor optimality in the Hopfield network — the condition that the energy landscape has its minimum in the welfare-optimal configuration. The dynamics of market efficiency are the Hopfield dynamics.

The three questions have the same mathematical form. The answers are the same mathematical object: an attractor landscape, and the field dynamics that determine which attractors are reached and how quickly.

## The Patient as Political-Economic Subject

The patient perspective paper in this volume served as the humanising chapter — the reminder that the abstract machinery of field theory has consequences for individual human lives. The patient is simultaneously a philosophical subject (a person with a somatic field, a phenomenal life, a good or bad existence), a political subject (a citizen whose rights are or are not protected, whose legal landscape is or is not ergodic), and an economic subject (a participant in markets whose structure may or may not be conducive to human flourishing).

The field-theoretic framework describes all three simultaneously. The same equation that describes the patient's trauma well describes the legal fragmentation that makes their rights insecure, and the market failure that makes their economic position precarious. This is not a coincidence. It is the recognition that the patient is embedded in field dynamics at multiple scales — personal, social, political, economic — and that all of these scales are governed by the same master equation.

## What a Field-Theoretic PPE Would Teach

A PPE curriculum built around the field-theoretic framework would teach students to:

**Identify the energy landscape.** Before analysing any political, economic, or philosophical problem, identify the attractor landscape: where are the stable states, where are the barriers, what is the temperature of the field? This is the first move in every field-theoretic analysis.

**Read the dynamics.** Understand what the field dynamics predict: where will the system end up if left alone, what interventions are above threshold, what topological transitions are approaching? The dynamics answer the question that equilibrium theory cannot: what happens next?

**Design for robustness.** Evaluate any proposed institution, policy, or practice not just for efficiency in equilibrium but for topological robustness: will it persist under perturbation? Will it maintain the rule of law (ergodicity) under stress? Will it protect rights (topological invariants) against powerful countervailing forces?

**Ground abstraction in lived experience.** The patient perspective is not a soft addition to the hard analysis. It is the check on the analysis: does the abstract account describe a human life as it is actually lived? The somatic field dynamics are ultimately the dynamics of felt experience, and any account that loses sight of that has lost the point.

## Open Questions for PPE

The field-theoretic PPE opens a set of questions that cross the traditional disciplinary boundaries.

**The good life and field health.** What would a positive account of field health look like — one that goes beyond the absence of trauma wells to specify what a flourishing somatic field configuration is? This is a philosophical question with empirical content: it requires both a normative theory and a measurement programme.

**Institutional design for robustness.** Given the field-theoretic account of institutional stability, what design principles produce institutions that are robustly ergodic and rights-preserving? This is a political theory question with engineering content.

**Market design for landscape optimality.** Given the Hopfield account of market dynamics, what market structures produce energy landscapes whose global minimum corresponds to welfare-optimal allocations? This is an economics question with physics content.

Mind, market, and mandate. The field is the common ground.
