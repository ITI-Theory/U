# The Green Propagator

**G-ID:** *Aesthetic Field Perturbation — BRECVEMA impulse response to musical stimulus*, `open-hypothesis`. In this book the Green propagator is not a mysterious universal solvent for art. It is a disciplined way of asking a familiar music-psychological question: if a listener is perturbed by a pulse, sonority, remembered song, expectation, image, or judgement, what kind of response does the body-field produce, how long does it last, and what route does it take through affective state space?

The zoom of the volume is mainly $\sigma=8$: the human organism at the scale of auditory stimulus, bodily response, memory, social coupling, and reportable affect. Where the text speaks about film, installation, projection, or [T]-Theory art practice, it remains at the same human scale unless explicitly marked as a cultural or collective extension. Claims about musical structure are usually `interpretive`; claims about the specific Soma Machine MVP are `derived-under-assumptions` as design commitments; the exploratory music-affect run is `empirical-result` only in the limited pilot sense reported by the source paper.

# Introduction: Music as a Field Art

Music psychology has an embarrassment of riches and a shortage of motion. It has taxonomies of emotion, scales of liking, arousal ratings, physiological measures, cue studies, cultural comparisons, and increasingly refined accounts of the mechanisms through which sound can evoke feeling. Juslin's BRECVEMA framework remains especially useful because it refuses to reduce musical emotion to one route: brainstem reflex, rhythmic entrainment, evaluative conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy, and aesthetic judgement are different mechanisms, not eight names for the same event [@juslin2013]. Yet much of the field still has difficulty representing a musical episode as an episode: a temporal passage in which multiple mechanisms overlap, compete, prepare, fail, resolve, recur, and leave a body changed. The starting point of this volume is therefore modest: a taxonomy of mechanisms needs a dynamics of response `interpretive`.

The valence-arousal circumplex, associated with James A. Russell's circumplex model of affect, is an indispensable map for many purposes. It tells us roughly where an affective report lies in a two-dimensional coordinate system, and it gives experimenters a common language for comparing episodes. <!-- REF-NEEDED: James A. Russell, A circumplex model of affect, 1980, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology --> But composers and listeners rarely experience music as a dot. They experience approach, suspension, release, derailment, looping, entrainment, fatigue, return, and after-image. A composer works with a path, not a point. A performer shapes thresholds and delays. An improviser feels when the field is ready to turn. The Soma Field Theory contribution is to ask whether these musical intuitions can be written as motion on an energy landscape rather than as positions on a static chart `open-hypothesis`.

The finite music-affect model embedded in this volume makes that question concrete. It writes a listener state as

$$
\mathbf{e}(t)=(e^s_1,\ldots,e^s_8,e^c_1,\ldots,e^c_8)\in[0,1]^{16},
$$

where each of eight affective modes has somatic and cognitive components. The modes used by the paper are calm, fight, flight, grief, freeze or dissociation, hypervigilance, flow or absorption, and joy. The proposed energy function is

$$
H(\mathbf{e})=\tfrac12\mathbf{e}^{\top}W\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{e},
$$

and the response dynamics are written as

$$
\gamma\dot{\mathbf{e}}=-\nabla H(\mathbf{e})+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t)+J(t),
$$

with $T_{\mathrm{eff}}=D/\gamma$. This is not a claim that a concert hall has secretly become a particle accelerator. It is a model class: affective state is represented as a damped, noisy, forced trajectory over basins and barriers `derived-under-assumptions`.

This volume therefore reads BRECVEMA as an operator lens. Brainstem reflex is the fast transient source: the crash, startle, scream, sudden registral eruption, or acoustic shock that enters the equation as $J(t)$. Rhythmic entrainment changes damping and phase relation; it is the route through which meter, pulse, and bodily periodicity can lock or fail to lock. Evaluative conditioning shifts the bias vector $\mathbf{b}$ because a sound that has been paired with a person, place, ritual, scene, or wound does not arrive neutrally. Emotional contagion is coupling $\kappa$ between performer and listener fields. Visual imagery becomes an endogenous source $J_{\mathrm{internal}}(t)$. Episodic memory is a memory kernel $K(\tau)$. Musical expectancy alters transient barriers $\Delta V$. Aesthetic judgement updates cognitive appraisal and bias. None of these translations is final; the value lies in making each mechanism's dynamical role inspectable and falsifiable `open-hypothesis`.

Juslin's own account of musical emotions distinguishes everyday emotion from aesthetic emotion and emphasises that musical induction can be mediated by multiple routes rather than one special musical faculty [@juslin2013; @juslin2019musical]. That is exactly why BRECVEMA suits a field model better than a single-cause theory. The field account does not say "rhythm causes pleasure" or "expectation causes chills" as if those were complete explanations. It asks which parameter is being modulated, at what time-scale, against which prior landscape, and with which competition from other channels. A listener may entrain rhythmically while resisting evaluatively; may enjoy expectancy violation while rejecting the style; may be pulled into memory by a timbre while the harmonic syntax remains opaque. A field model has room for such mixed trajectories `interpretive`.

The expectancy tradition also matters here. Leonard Meyer made musical meaning depend on inhibited expectation and stylistically learned implication, not merely on acoustic stimulus. David Huron's later ITPRA model divides expectancy into imagination, tension, prediction, reaction, and appraisal responses, emphasising that anticipatory and post-event processes are not the same thing. <!-- REF-NEEDED: Leonard B. Meyer, Emotion and Meaning in Music, 1956 --> <!-- REF-NEEDED: David Huron, Sweet Anticipation, 2006 --> The Soma Field reading does not replace Meyer or Huron; it gives their insight a landscape notation. Expectation is a preparation of the field toward one basin; surprise is the failure or displacement of that preparation; resolution is not only a chord label but a change in energy, barrier, and damping `interpretive`.

Entrainment has a comparable dual status. In music psychology it refers to temporal coordination between external periodicities and internal bodily or attentional rhythms; in dynamical-systems language it invites talk of phase locking, Arnold tongues, and oscillator coupling. The book uses that language carefully. It does not claim that every listener's groove response has already been measured as an Arnold tongue. It proposes that entrainment experiments can be designed that way: vary tempo, syncopation, phase stability, bodily movement, and prior musical training, then estimate the region in which stable locking occurs. A "groove" would then be not just a style word but a measurable region of stable coupled response `open-hypothesis`. <!-- REF-NEEDED: Edward W. For composers this shift is practical. If rhythmic entrainment modulates damping and phase, a rhythm section is not only providing time; it is regulating the listener's capacity to remain in a trajectory. If musical expectancy modulates barriers, harmonic syntax is not only "about" tension and resolution; it is a technology for temporarily making one transition harder and another easier. If timbre contributes directional forcing, orchestration becomes more than colour: it becomes a vectorial push in affective space. If episodic memory enters through $K(\tau)$, quotation, genre sign, and sonic grain alter the present by convolving it with remembered time. # BRECVEMA as a Dynamics, Not a Mood List

BRECVEMA is often misunderstood by those who meet it second-hand. It is not a typology of musical moods. It is a mechanism taxonomy: a list of routes by which music may induce emotion. The same passage can involve several mechanisms at once, and the same mechanism can produce different felt outcomes depending on context. A drum hit can be a brainstem reflex in one situation, an episodic-memory trigger in another, and a component of entrainment in a third. A field account must preserve that pluralism, or it becomes less accurate than the taxonomy it seeks to extend `interpretive`.

The first mechanism, brainstem reflex, is the easiest to translate into the model because it is nearest to an impulse. Sudden loudness, sharp attack, roughness, dissonant onset, or unexpected acoustic intensity can produce fast orienting and startle-like bodily response. In the Soma Machine mapping this is a transient source $J(t)$. For composers it is the domain of impact, cut, accent, burst, shock, and sensory salience. For researchers it predicts short-latency changes aligned to acoustic features rather than to learned harmonic syntax. The field claim is not that every accent has the same emotional meaning; it is that some acoustic events enter the dynamics as high-amplitude perturbations before reflective appraisal has much time to work `derived-under-assumptions`.

Rhythmic entrainment is slower and more relational. It concerns the fit between an external pulse hierarchy and internal oscillatory processes: movement, attention, breathing, cardiac rhythm, motor prediction, and social timing. In the field notation it primarily modulates damping $\gamma$ and phase locking. A stable pulse can reduce effective uncertainty by making future events temporally easier to anticipate. Syncopation can increase tension while preserving enough periodic structure for the body to remain locked. Too little regularity may fail to entrain; too much may become inert. The proposed Arnold tongue is the region in which a particular listener's field locks to a particular rhythmic drive `open-hypothesis`.

This matters for groove. Groove is not reducible to tempo, syncopation, or pleasure. It is a bodily invitation to continue. The field vocabulary suggests that groove occurs when rhythmic forcing is strong enough to capture bodily timing but not so rigid that it eliminates micro-adjustment. In that state the listener does not simply hear a beat; they are stabilised in a coupled dynamical relation with it. The empirical version of this claim would measure tapping, movement, physiological rhythm, perceived urge to move, and transition out of the entrained state when perturbations are introduced `open-hypothesis`.

Evaluative conditioning is the first mechanism that forces the model to take personal history seriously. A sound associated with a person, place, ritual, humiliation, pleasure, loss, school hall, club, parent, film, game, prayer, or period of life does not merely carry acoustic information. It shifts the bias vector $\mathbf{b}$ before the listener has had time to treat the sound as a neutral object. In standard music psychology this is unsurprising; in the field model it becomes a parameter update. The same chord sequence may point toward different basins because the listener's learned associations have already tilted the landscape `interpretive`.

Emotional contagion is interpersonal. It involves the listener's response to perceived emotion in a voice, instrument, body, performer, ensemble, or crowd. It is not just "recognising" that a singer sounds sad. It is coupling to a performed field. The Soma Field reading writes this as $\kappa$, an interpersonal coupling strength. A singer's breath, attack, pitch instability, vibrato, articulation, and timing all become possible coupling channels. In ensemble music, contagion may pass through the group before it reaches the audience: the listener hears not only notes but a coordinated social field `open-hypothesis`.

Visual imagery, in BRECVEMA, reminds us that music often induces or accompanies inner scenes. These may be narrative, spatial, bodily, synaesthetic, or fragmentary. The field account treats imagery as an endogenous source $J_{\mathrm{internal}}(t)$. The musical stimulus does not simply push the field from outside; it recruits inner generative material that then pushes from within. This is why the same music can feel expansive to one listener and claustrophobic to another. The imagery channel is not decorative. It can dominate the trajectory when internal scenes carry strong affective charge `interpretive`.

Episodic memory is close to imagery but more temporally anchored. A song can bring back a particular summer, a death, a first instrument, a breakup, a church, a political moment, a club, a hospital, or a childhood room. In the field notation this is the memory kernel $K(\tau)$: a way of saying that past events continue to weight present response with their own amplitudes and decay. For musicologists, this is one reason repertoire cannot be understood only as structure. Pieces accumulate social and autobiographical kernels. A national anthem, protest song, lullaby, hymn, rave track, film cue, or ringtone is never only its acoustic spectrum `interpretive`.

Musical expectancy is the most developed bridge to music theory. Meyer's account of emotion and meaning made expectation, inhibition, and fulfilment central to musical experience. Huron's account separated anticipatory and post-outcome components, including tension before an expected event and reaction after it. The Soma Field translation is barrier language: expectation prepares a path, and the expected arrival lowers or confirms a basin; violation may raise a barrier, divert the route, or reveal an alternative basin. The important point is that expectation is temporal preparation, not just probability assignment `interpretive`.

That field translation can sharpen familiar analytical cases. A deceptive cadence is not simply "unexpected"; it carries the field close to one attractor, then diverts it. A delayed dominant prolongation sustains gradient without release. A modulation may make a former basin inaccessible by changing the tonal landscape. A cadential arrival may release because the body has been held near a saddle. An extreme repetition may deepen an attractor until escape requires novelty or fatigue. These statements remain interpretations until linked to listener data, but they are more precise than saying merely that the music is tense or resolved `open-hypothesis`.

Aesthetic judgement is last in BRECVEMA and often the most intellectually mediated. It includes assessment of beauty, skill, appropriateness, originality, style, authenticity, difficulty, moral fit, and value. The field model treats it as appraisal and bias update rather than as a detached verdict after emotion has happened. A listener's judgement can amplify or inhibit other mechanisms: admiration may deepen attention; distrust may block contagion; perceived manipulation may change response to a climactic passage; recognition of craft may produce pleasure even when the immediate affect is dark. Aesthetic judgement is not outside the field; it is one of the routes by which cognition reshapes it `interpretive`.

The CODA-informed appraisal/context layer proposed by the Soma Machine MVP is crucial here. BRECVEMA answers which mechanisms may be active; appraisal asks why the same mechanism profile leads to different episodes. Novelty, familiarity, expectation, goal relevance, goal conduciveness, coping potential, and agency are educational weighting inputs in the MVP, not diagnostic measures. For music psychologists this is a welcome safeguard. It prevents the system from treating a listener as a passive transducer and allows context to modulate the forcing-to-response mapping `derived-under-assumptions`.

Consider a simple example: a loud, distorted guitar chord entering after silence. Brainstem reflex supplies a transient source. Entrainment may or may not follow depending on whether a pulse stabilises. Conditioning depends on genre history: metal, punk, noise, worship, film horror, or adolescent memory. Contagion depends on the performer's gesture and social context. Imagery may open a visual or bodily scene. Episodic memory may dominate if the sound recalls a specific event. Expectancy depends on the preceding syntax. Aesthetic judgement asks whether the sound is apt, earned, crude, liberating, or fake. A single "emotion label" cannot carry this analysis `interpretive`.

The field account's promise is not that it replaces careful mechanism-by-mechanism study. Its promise is integration. It offers one state equation into which separate mechanism profiles can be inserted as parameter changes. This makes mixed responses more tractable: joy with grief, calm with awe, fear with attraction, absorption with hypervigilance. It also creates measurable hypotheses: if the brainstem route dominates, early responses should track acoustic transients; if entrainment dominates, phase locking and movement should matter; if expectancy dominates, stylistic knowledge should change the trajectory; if memory dominates, autobiographical variables should predict response more strongly than surface features `open-hypothesis`.

The music-affect paper's pilot data are deliberately modest. A single logged session reports duration, transitions, threshold events, and attractor occupancies; one hypothesis receives an exploratory pass and the others are not testable in that run. This is not confirmation of a theory of music. It is an existence proof for a logging grammar: the instrument can record $H(\mathbf{e})$, $\|\nabla H\|$, $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$, threshold events, nearest attractor, and state trajectories at a useful temporal resolution. A serious research programme begins only when independent operators, blinded blocks, physiological co-validation, and pre-registered disconfirmation criteria enter the picture `empirical-result`.

For composers, the immediate value is conceptual rather than evidential. BRECVEMA becomes a palette of dynamical interventions. One can compose for impulse, for lock, for learned bias, for contagious gesture, for image, for memory, for expectation, and for judgement. These are not genres. They are ways of asking the listener's field to move. A composer may deliberately stack mechanisms: a metrically stable groove with an unstable harmony, a familiar timbre in an unfamiliar tuning, a memory cue placed against a contradictory visual image, a cadence that resolves acoustically but fails aesthetically. The model gives names to these composite operations `interpretive`.

For performers, the model highlights the body as part of the work. Timing, touch, breath, posture, micro-dynamic change, and audience coupling are not expressive ornaments added after the score. They are perturbation channels. A string player's bow pressure, a drummer's placement, an electronic musician's filter gesture, a singer's inhale, or a DJ's transition can alter coupling and expectation before any listener can verbalise why. A field aesthetics therefore respects the musician's practical knowledge: performers already operate on response dynamics, even when the written theory lags behind them `interpretive`.

For music psychologists, the model's strength will be judged by prediction. Does it outperform circumplex and autoregressive baselines on transition timing? Does it predict hysteresis: the fact that the route into a state differs from the route out? Does manipulated $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ alter dwell time and transition count? Do expectancy violations produce barrier-like signatures? Does entrainment show listener-specific locking regions? Do memory-heavy stimuli reveal slower kernels than pure acoustic startles? These are not rhetorical questions. They are the empirical future of the field account `open-hypothesis`.

## Mechanism-specific experimental sketches

A useful way to test the field translation is to refuse global confirmation. The question should not be "does music move the soma-field?" That is too broad. The better question is whether one mechanism, under controlled conditions, produces the parameter signature assigned to it. The field account becomes scientifically useful only if it can lose locally. A failure of the expectancy mapping should not automatically refute the entrainment mapping; a failure of body-grid rendering should not refute the state equation; a successful pilot should not promote the whole aesthetics to established fact `open-hypothesis`.

For brainstem reflex, the cleanest design is acoustic-transient control. The experiment varies onset abruptness, intensity, spectral roughness, and silence-to-sound contrast while holding higher-level musical context minimal. The predicted field signature is short-latency perturbation: a spike in the source-like channel, a rapid change in $\|\nabla H\|$, and immediate physiological response. If the response instead depends mainly on stylistic expectation, lyric meaning, or autobiographical familiarity, then the event is not primarily a brainstem-reflex case, however loud it is. This protects the mechanism from being used as a generic name for "strong sound" `open-hypothesis`.

For rhythmic entrainment, the design should estimate a listener-specific locking region. Present pulse trains, grooves, and musical excerpts with systematic variation in tempo, microtiming, syncopation, metrical clarity, and movement permission. Measure tapping, body motion, respiratory phase, perceived urge to move, and attention stability. The field prediction is not simply that faster tempi increase arousal. It is that there are regions where external pulse and internal response become mutually stabilising, and that leaving those regions changes dwell time and transition probability. A listener with a narrow locking region may still be highly musical; they are selective in coupling, not deficient `open-hypothesis`.

For evaluative conditioning, the study must begin before the music. The same sound should be paired with positive, negative, neutral, or personally meaningful contexts across learning sessions, then reintroduced later in a new musical frame. The predicted signature is a bias-vector shift: the sound changes the trajectory before the musical syntax alone would predict it. If conditioning is strong, a neutral interval, timbre, or motif can become a local landscape feature. If conditioning does not alter trajectory after learning, the field account must lower the weight assigned to $\mathbf{b}$ for that case `open-hypothesis`.

For emotional contagion, the design should separate expressive source from structural material. The same melody or rhythm can be rendered by performers with different bodily affect, by a deadpan synthesiser, by expressive human performance, or by deliberately mismatched visual gesture. If contagion is coupling, the listener response should track perceived performer state beyond the notated material. Group contexts matter here: choir, string quartet, club, ritual, and solo recording create different coupling geometries. The field variable $\kappa$ should therefore be treated as relational, not as an acoustic feature hidden in the waveform `open-hypothesis`.

For visual imagery, measurement is harder because the source is internal. One design uses music with high imagery affordance but low autobiographical specificity: sparse textures, suggestive spatialisation, or programmatic cues without personal memory. Participants report or continuously mark image vividness, spatiality, and affective force, while physiological and trajectory measures are logged. The prediction is an endogenous source: the response grows from internally generated scene dynamics rather than from acoustic transients alone. Failures would include cases where imagery reports are rich but field trajectory remains indistinguishable from non-imagery controls `open-hypothesis`.

For episodic memory, the correct design is participant-specific. Researchers should not pretend that one repertoire carries the same kernel for all listeners. Participants supply personally meaningful tracks, culturally familiar but non-personal tracks, and unfamiliar controls. The predicted difference is persistence: memory-heavy music should show slower decay, stronger after-response, and greater divergence between listeners. The model's $K(\tau)$ language becomes useful if it predicts the time-course of return after the sound stops, not merely the intensity during playback `open-hypothesis`.

For musical expectancy, the design should use stylistically competent listeners and carefully controlled continuations. Meyer and Huron matter because expectation is learned and temporally staged, not simply "surprise". A cadence, melodic continuation, or rhythmic arrival can be expected by one listener and not by another. The field prediction is barrier modulation: expected continuations lower one path; violations divert or raise transition cost; appraised violations can become pleasurable after the reaction phase. Studies that ignore stylistic knowledge will misclassify expectancy as acoustic novelty `interpretive`.

For aesthetic judgement, experiments must accept that the response may be slow and cognitive. Ask listeners not only what they felt, but when they judged the work apt, beautiful, manipulative, banal, skilful, excessive, morally troubling, or formally satisfying. The field prediction is appraisal-driven bias update: judgement changes subsequent response, including retrospective valuation of earlier passages. A listener may initially resist a harsh sonority, then revalue it once its structural role is understood. That is not an exception to music-induced affect; it is one of its mechanisms `interpretive`.

These mechanism-specific sketches imply a methodological ethic. A serious field account should never hide behind the complexity of whole pieces. It should welcome decomposed tests, then reassemble mechanisms in ecologically valid music. The composer supplies rich trajectories; the psychologist supplies controlled contrasts; the performer supplies coupling; the interface supplies logging; the listener supplies the final constraint. If all five are not present somewhere in the programme, the theory risks becoming an elegant vocabulary without empirical teeth `open-hypothesis`.

## Expectancy, entrainment, and the difference between prediction and preparation

Expectancy and entrainment are often confused because both concern the future. But they prepare different aspects of the listener. Entrainment prepares timing: when something is likely to happen, where the body can place itself, how attention pulses, whether movement can settle into relation. Expectancy prepares event-content: what is likely to happen, what implication has been set up, what style licenses, what continuation would make sense. A listener can be temporally entrained while harmonically surprised; can predict a cadence without bodily groove; can be metrically disoriented while still expecting a melodic continuation. The field account should keep these channels distinct `interpretive`.

This distinction matters for Huron's tension phase and Meyer's inhibition. The body can be temporally ready before the event arrives, and that readiness has affective weight. It can also be semantically or stylistically ready for one continuation rather than another. In field terms, entrainment can lower temporal uncertainty while expectancy shapes the topology of possible next basins. A delayed arrival may heighten both: the body remains phase-prepared and the event-content remains withheld. A sudden wrong-footing may preserve pulse while diverting harmonic route. These differences are audible in everyday listening `interpretive`.

Composers exploit the separation constantly. A dance track can maintain entrainment while delaying a bass drop; a late Beethoven movement can loosen metrical security while deepening motivic expectancy; a film score can pulse steadily under an ambiguous harmony; a jazz soloist can stay inside the groove while playing against harmonic expectation; a raga performance can stretch event-time while maintaining deep tala orientation. The field model becomes musically credible only if it can describe such crossed conditions without collapsing them into generic tension `interpretive`.

The Soma Machine can teach this distinction visually. Entrainment selection should highlight damping and phase-lock routes; expectancy selection should highlight barriers and transient wells. A user who chooses rhythmic entrainment should see the system ask about pulse, phase, locking, and decay. A user who chooses musical expectancy should see anticipated path, violation, barrier, and appraisal. If the interface renders both mechanisms with the same animation, it fails pedagogically even if the animation is beautiful `derived-under-assumptions`.

For empirical work, the separation suggests factorial designs: stable pulse with expected harmony, stable pulse with violated harmony, unstable pulse with expected harmony, unstable pulse with violated harmony. Add movement permission as another factor, because entrainment can be suppressed by task constraints. The predicted response patterns should differ in phase locking, reported tension, transition timing, and post-event appraisal. If all four conditions collapse into a single arousal score, the field model adds little; if they separate in trajectory space, it earns its keep `open-hypothesis`.

# Composition as Attractor Traversal

Composers have always written trajectories. Even when notation presents simultaneities, the craft is temporal: prepare, withhold, release, thicken, thin, repeat, rupture, return. A field-theoretic account does not teach composers that music moves. They already know. It gives a new vocabulary for what kind of movement is being written. Instead of saying only that a section is tense, one can ask which basin is being approached, which barrier is being raised, whether noise is being increased, whether damping is being reduced, whether memory is being activated, and whether the return path is the same as the departure path `interpretive`.

The simplest compositional operation is basin deepening. Repetition, tonal centring, rhythmic stability, timbral consistency, and registral anchoring can make a state feel more stable. In the field notation this means the trajectory is encouraged to remain near a local minimum. Minimalist process, drone, groove, chant, lullaby, and certain ambient practices all exploit basin deepening in different ways. The emotional result need not be calm. A deep basin may be ecstatic, obsessive, martial, melancholic, suspended, or dissociative depending on the coupling matrix and cultural frame `interpretive`.

The second operation is barrier raising. A composer can make exit from a state difficult: through unresolved harmony, locked repetition, metric insistence, narrowing register, increasing density, or refusal of expected cadence. In psychological terms, the listener may feel trapped, compelled, absorbed, suspended, or held. The fifth operation is coupling. Music is social even when heard alone, because it carries traces of bodies, rooms, genres, technologies, and imagined others. ## Worked compositional cases

Imagine a short piece designed around grief without melodrama. A conventional description might say slow tempo, minor harmony, low register, descending contours, and sparse texture. The field description asks a different set of questions. Which grief basin is being approached: loss, tenderness, collapse, dignity, ritual, memory? Is the piece deepening the basin or merely pointing at it? Is the listener given a path out, or held at a threshold? Does the memory kernel come from a quoted style, a recorded voice, a timbral patina, or the listener's own biography? Such questions do not replace musical analysis; they prevent the word "grief" from becoming a flat label `interpretive`.

Now imagine a work built around awe. Awe in the Tensor vocabulary lies near threshold, where fear and wonder share bodily substrate but differ in appraisal and integration. A composer might use expanding register, low-frequency power, slow harmonic rotation, high spectral richness, and a reduction of linguistic or metric certainty. But the field account asks whether the piece supplies enough safety for fear to tip into awe rather than into withdrawal. This is a compositional problem, not only a psychological one: awe may require a scaffolded loss of boundary `open-hypothesis`.

Consider joy. In simple mood taxonomies joy can become a bright point on a pleasant/high-arousal quadrant. In field practice joy may be a basin, an escape, a coupling phenomenon, or a return. A dance floor joy is entrained and collective; a reunion joy is memory-saturated; a comic joy may depend on violated expectation; a virtuoso joy may be aesthetic judgement; a child's song may be conditioning and contagion. The same label hides different mechanism stacks. A composer who wants joy must decide which stack is being built `interpretive`.

Consider anger or fight. Music can mobilise without violence, can hold anger as dignity, can parody it, can aestheticise it, can discharge it, or can trap a listener in it. Field language asks about direction and exit. Is the passage increasing energy while narrowing available basins? Is it coupling the listener to a performer whose anger is controlled? Is the rhythm organising mobilisation, or is noise raising temperature beyond coherent action? These distinctions are ethically important in political, ritual, and therapeutic-adjacent music `interpretive`.

Consider calm. Calm is not merely low arousal. It can be deep regulation, numbness, boredom, safety, dissociation, or absorption. The field model's distinction between calm, freeze, and flow is useful precisely because all may look quiet from outside. A piece that lowers $T_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and deepens a safety basin differs from a piece that narrows response and suppresses movement. Composers of ambient, liturgical, therapeutic, or installation music should care about that difference even when no clinical claim is made `open-hypothesis`.

These examples show why the field account is more a grammar than a style. It does not prescribe slow music for grief, fast music for joy, dissonance for fear, or consonance for calm. It asks what operations a work performs on a particular listener or listening culture. The same operation can be realised in different idioms. A noise artist and a choral composer can both work with threshold; a techno producer and a string quartet can both work with entrainment; a folk singer and a film composer can both work with memory kernel activation `interpretive`.

The grammar also helps explain why stylistic expertise changes affect. A listener trained in a style has different expectations, different learned basins, and different aesthetic judgement routes. What is chaotic to an outsider may be richly prepared for an insider; what is moving within one tradition may read as cliché in another. This does not make response arbitrary. It means the field includes learned structure. A music psychology that ignores style competence will misread many trajectories `interpretive`.

For artists working with [T]-Theory, a useful studio practice is the trajectory sheet. Before building a work, write the intended initial basin, target basin, barriers, memory kernels, entrainment channels, coupling channels, threshold events, and evidence status. After rehearsal or showing, write what actually happened. Did the audience move? Did they report the intended transition? Did the performer feel coupled? Did the visual response match the equation ledger? The difference between intended and actual traversal is the material for the next version `interpretive`.

Such sheets need not make art dry. Composers already use sketches, harmonic plans, dramaturgical arcs, cue sheets, patch diagrams, and rehearsal notes. A trajectory sheet is another craft document. Its advantage is that it includes the listener's body as part of the compositional situation without pretending to control it. It can also make collaboration easier: psychologist, performer, visual artist, programmer, and composer can see which mechanism each component is meant to address `interpretive`.

The live situation raises a further question: when should the work adapt? In a fixed score, the proposed trajectory remains stable while listeners vary. In a responsive system, sensor or audience data can alter rendering. The Tensor's resonance knob and the Soma Machine's future controller paths both live here. Adaptation can be generous, meeting the viewer where they are, or manipulative, constantly steering response without consent. A labelled art practice must therefore specify what is measured, how it is used, and what the participant is told `open-hypothesis`.

Improvisation provides a non-digital model of such adaptation. Skilled improvisers continuously read one another's timing, density, intensity, and direction. They may deepen an emerging basin, disrupt it, offer an exit, or hold a threshold. The ensemble is a coupled field without needing sensors. This is especially relevant to communal music: rave, chant, hymn, protest, procession, drumming, football song, lullaby, and ritual. These forms often work less by semantic content than by entrainment, contagion, repetition, memory, and shared appraisal. A field account can honour their intelligence without reducing them to "primitive rhythm" or "collective arousal". There is therefore no politically innocent field art. Music can widen a landscape, but it can also narrow one. ## Medium, renderer, and notation

The field score needs notation, but not every notation must look mathematical. This distinction can liberate artists. They need not wait for the physics to be settled before making work. They also need not exaggerate scientific status to justify the work. A film can explore an emotional score while the field ontology remains open. A concert can stage attractor traversal while the mechanism mappings remain hypotheses. A projection can make the body-field visible as an image while body-map validation remains incomplete. Art can move first, if it labels what kind of movement it is `interpretive`.

The same distinction protects science. Researchers can study response to [T]-Theory artworks without assuming the theory. They can compare predictions, collect data, and publish failures. If an artwork designed as an awe trajectory reliably produces boredom, irritation, or analytic detachment, that is valuable. If a design-map body overlay distracts or biases reports, that is valuable. If labels improve understanding but reduce immersion, that is valuable. The art becomes a generator of discriminating cases `open-hypothesis`.

## A field-aesthetic vocabulary for audience reports

One practical problem in music and art research is the poverty of available report language. Listeners can rate valence, arousal, liking, chills, familiarity, tension, and emotion labels, but these often miss trajectory. A field-aesthetic report form could ask about approach, resistance, capture, release, looping, threshold, return, after-image, bodily location, memory activation, social coupling, and appraisal shift. Such reports would not replace physiological measures; they would give the model better phenomenological data `open-hypothesis`.

For example, instead of asking only "how sad did the music make you?", the form might ask: Did the passage move toward sadness, hold you near it, pass through it, or leave it nearby? Did sadness feel personal, observed, ritual, beautiful, unwanted, or unresolved? Did it arrive through timbre, memory, harmony, voice, rhythm, image, or judgement? Did it remain after the sound? These questions align better with the field's concern for path, mechanism, and decay `interpretive`.

For entrainment, reports should include bodily urge, ease of synchronising, moments of losing the pulse, pleasure in lock, irritation at rigidity, and social movement. For expectancy, they should include what was anticipated, whether violation felt rewarding or disruptive, when appraisal changed, and whether repeated listening altered the response. For memory, they should distinguish personal recollection from generic nostalgia. For imagery, they should distinguish visual scene, bodily image, spatial feeling, and narrative association `interpretive`.

Such vocabulary can also help composers. Audience feedback often arrives as adjectives: beautiful, intense, boring, too long, moving, confusing. Field-aesthetic questions can turn those adjectives into revision cues. Was the piece too long because the basin was over-deepened? Confusing because expectation barriers were not legible? Moving because memory kernels activated? Boring because temperature fell without safety deepening? These interpretations may be wrong, but they are actionable hypotheses `open-hypothesis`.

The vocabulary must remain plural. Not every listener has the same interoceptive access, cultural training, language for emotion, or desire to disclose. Some may prefer metaphor; some may prefer numbers; some may refuse the frame. A field practice should not treat refusal as failed data. The listener's relation to the reporting apparatus is itself part of the episode. A person can be moved by music and reject the theory describing it `interpretive`.

For neurodivergent listeners, the caution is even more important. Differences in sensory threshold, timing, attention, temperature, and social coupling may alter musical response, but this volume does not diagnose or rank listeners. A field vocabulary can be respectful if it treats difference as operator variation rather than deficit; it becomes harmful if it turns profiles into rigid categories. Any claims about autism, ADHD, trauma, or other clinical/neurodevelopmental patterns require their own evidence and should be routed to the appropriate clinical volumes `open-hypothesis`.

The field-aesthetic report form also gives artists a way to document practice without pretending to run a clinical study. A concert programme can invite optional reflection: What moved? What held? What returned? What remained? A workshop can compare intended and reported trajectories. A research collaboration can turn these forms into structured data only with consent and methodological care. The point is to enrich language before quantifying it `interpretive`.

In the long run, this vocabulary might help bridge first-person and third-person accounts. Physiological measures can show timing, arousal, movement, and autonomic change. Reports can show meaning, appraisal, memory, and felt path. Logs can show model state. None is sovereign alone. The field account becomes credible when these streams converge, and instructive when they diverge in interpretable ways `open-hypothesis`.

## Failure modes, falsification, and artistic usefulness

A theory of music and affect that cannot name its failure modes is not yet useful. The field account can fail in several distinct ways. It can fail descriptively, by giving listeners and artists a vocabulary that does not illuminate their experience. It can fail predictively, by performing no better than circumplex, cue-based, predictive-processing, or autoregressive baselines. It can fail mechanistically, by assigning BRECVEMA mechanisms to the wrong parameters. It can fail ethically, by encouraging overconfident claims about bodies, trauma, or therapy. These failures should be separated rather than hidden under a single verdict `open-hypothesis`.

Descriptive failure is the least catastrophic and perhaps the most artistically informative. If composers find that "barrier", "basin", "temperature", or "kernel" does not help them think, the vocabulary may still serve researchers. If listeners find the field report form alienating, it may need better language. If performers find the model too coarse for timing and touch, it may need a performance-specific layer. Aesthetic usefulness is not the same as empirical truth, but it matters for a volume addressed to artists `interpretive`.

Predictive failure is sharper. Suppose a study logs state trajectories, physiological signals, and reports across carefully designed expectancy manipulations, but the field model predicts transition timing no better than a simple surprise rating. Then the expectancy-to-barrier mapping is weak. Suppose entrainment measures are better predicted by motor preference and familiarity than by any Arnold-tongue estimate. Then the phase-locking story needs revision. Suppose memory-heavy songs do not show slower decay than unfamiliar controls. Then the $K(\tau)$ account may be overextended. Such failures would improve the theory by localising its errors `open-hypothesis`.

Mechanistic failure is subtler because mechanisms interact. A passage designed as expectancy may actually work through memory; a supposed brainstem reflex may be learned genre response; an apparent aesthetic judgement may be post-hoc rationalisation of bodily entrainment. This is why mechanism-specific studies must be paired with whole-music studies. Laboratory isolation without ecological music risks triviality; ecological music without isolation risks interpretive sprawl. The field account needs both `interpretive`.

Ethical failure is the most serious. It occurs when a model of response becomes a claim of access or control. An interface that says "you are in freeze" because a visual attractor is active has crossed a boundary. A performance that uses biometric input without clear consent has crossed a boundary. A paper that treats pilot art response as clinical evidence has crossed a boundary. A cross-scale artwork that suggests non-human systems literally feel human emotions has crossed a boundary. These are not merely communication problems; they are misuse of the field vocabulary `derived-under-assumptions`.

The evidence labels are designed to prevent such misuse. `Derived-under-assumptions` means the result follows inside a model; it does not mean the world has adopted the model. `Empirical-result` means a documented measurement or experiment produced the stated result; it does not mean the result generalises beyond its design. `Interpretive` means the reading may be fertile without being proven. `Open-hypothesis` means the claim is proposed with a path to discrimination. Music and arts work should be comfortable carrying these labels visibly `interpretive`.

One useful falsification test is cross-mechanism substitution. If a piece said to work through expectancy still produces the same trajectory when expectation is neutralised but memory cues remain, the mechanism assignment is wrong. If a groove effect remains when the pulse is removed but timbre and genre cues remain, entrainment was not the main route. If a claimed brainstem-reflex response depends on knowing the performer, it is not primarily brainstem. The model should welcome such substitutions because they distinguish route from outcome `open-hypothesis`.

Another test is listener expertise. Expectancy effects should vary with stylistic knowledge; entrainment effects may vary with movement practice, motor timing, and tempo preference; aesthetic judgement effects should vary with evaluative criteria; memory effects should vary with personal and cultural history. If the model predicts the same trajectory for all listeners under all conditions, it has become too blunt. A field is not a universal listener; it is a formal grammar for situated response `interpretive`.

A third test is path dependence. The field model predicts that order matters. Hearing A then B should not always equal hearing B then A. A cadence after long suspension should not equal the same cadence after neutral material. A familiar song after biographical priming should not equal the same song after analytic instruction. If experiments find no order effects where the model predicts memory, barrier, or basin changes, the field account loses a key advantage over static maps `open-hypothesis`.

A fourth test is after-response. Many musical effects live after the sound: earworms, mood residues, bodily agitation, calm, tears, changed silence, or altered perception of the next piece. The field vocabulary predicts that memory kernels, basin depth, and temperature should shape decay. Measuring only during-stimulus ratings misses this. Studies should include post-sound windows, delayed reports, and possibly next-stimulus effects. If after-response does not align with kernel predictions, the model must revise its temporal claims `open-hypothesis`.

A fifth test is transfer across renderers. The Tensor claims that an emotional score can be realised in different containers while preserving trajectory. That claim can be tested artistically and empirically. Render the same abstract path as music, film, projection, and live performance; ask whether response signatures preserve any invariant. If they do, the emotional-score idea gains support. If not, medium-specific affordances dominate more strongly than the abstract trajectory. Either result would be useful for artists `open-hypothesis`.

Artistic usefulness can survive empirical narrowing. Even if the full USF ontology fails, artists may still use attractor traversal as a compositional metaphor, the Soma Machine as an educational visualiser, and BRECVEMA-parameter labels as a workshop tool. This does not trivialise the science; it separates the fate of a practice from the fate of an ontology. Many artistic concepts remain valuable as concepts even when their original scientific theories change. The ledger should record that value as `interpretive`, not as proof `interpretive`.

The reverse is also true: empirical support would not automatically make the art good. A piece might successfully manipulate predicted parameters and still be aesthetically dull, coercive, sentimental, or crude. Science can help describe response, but it cannot by itself settle value. A field aesthetics must leave room for taste, judgement, tradition, politics, craft, moral criticism, and surprise. The model is a tool for thinking about affective motion, not a machine for producing greatness `interpretive`.

This is where aesthetic judgement returns as both mechanism and critique. Listeners judge not only sounds but the work's relation to them. They may resent obvious manipulation, admire restraint, forgive harshness that feels earned, reject beauty that feels false, or accept discomfort when the work gives them agency. These judgements feed back into the response. A theory that treats the listener as a passive field will misunderstand art. The field must be a field of appraisal as well as sensation `interpretive`.

Composers should therefore use the model experimentally rather than dogmatically. Write a passage meant to deepen a basin, then ask if it did. Write a threshold event, then ask whether listeners felt crossing, avoidance, or nothing. Use labels in rehearsal, then remove them in performance and compare. Try a Tensor-style score in different renderers. Build a piece around one BRECVEMA mechanism, then deliberately contaminate it with another. The model becomes artistically alive when it becomes a source of compositional questions `interpretive`.

Music psychologists should likewise avoid treating [T]-Theory as a package to accept or reject wholesale. The theory contains formal claims, model assumptions, instrument designs, pilot data, aesthetic interpretations, and speculative clinical-adjacent language. Each must be evaluated at the correct level. A negative result for one mechanism does not refute the idea that dynamics matter. A compelling artwork does not validate the Hamiltonian. A useful interface does not prove consciousness. This sorting is the real contribution of the label discipline `interpretive`.

The most promising collaboration is iterative. Artists design perturbations with clear mechanism labels. Psychologists test response. Engineers improve logging and rendering. Theorists revise equations. Philosophers audit claim status. Audiences teach everyone where the model missed lived experience. The cycle repeats. In such a cycle, failure is not embarrassment; it is how the field learns its boundaries `open-hypothesis`.

For this reason, the book's claim about "physics" should be heard carefully. It is not saying that every aesthetic event has already been reduced to a physical law. It is saying that music and art can be investigated as response dynamics, with equations where equations help, labels where labels protect, and artistic practice where practice discovers cases no laboratory would invent. That is a physics of music and affect in the programme's sense: not a closure of art, but an opening of its temporal mechanics `interpretive`.

## What this gives the three intended audiences

For music psychologists, the book offers a research scaffold rather than a finished theory. Its first contribution is to separate mechanism, state, and trajectory. BRECVEMA names routes; the affect vector names a provisional state space; the Langevin equation names motion; the ledger names evidence status. That separation should make experiments cleaner. A study can test one route without claiming to validate the whole field ontology. It can also compare dynamic predictions with existing tools rather than treating [T]-Theory as an isolated vocabulary `open-hypothesis`.

The second contribution for psychologists is a stronger account of mixed emotion. Music-induced affect is often composite: pleasure with sadness, fear with awe, calm with tears, absorption with agitation, nostalgia with pain. Static coordinate systems can register mixtures, but they struggle to explain how the mixture changes moment by moment. A field trajectory can represent co-activation, inhibition, barrier crossing, memory persistence, and appraisal update in one temporal description. Whether this description predicts better is empirical; its conceptual advantage is that mixture is expected rather than anomalous `interpretive`.

The third contribution is a bridge to instrument design. The music-affect paper and Soma Machine MVP specify what should be logged: state components, energy, gradient, temperature, threshold, nearest attractor, selected mechanism, forcing event, and response decay. Even researchers sceptical of the broader USF can use this as a checklist for richer music-affect data. Many studies still collect ratings before and after stimuli; the field approach insists that the middle of the episode is where the theory lives `interpretive`.

For composers, the book offers permission to think operationally without becoming reductive. Composers already know how to write toward, around, and away from affective states. The field vocabulary gives them a way to annotate those operations: deepen, tilt, heat, cool, lock, loosen, couple, remember, delay, divert, cross, return. This is especially helpful in collaborative settings where a composer must communicate with visual artists, programmers, performers, and researchers. The vocabulary does not dictate style; it names intentions and suspected mechanisms `interpretive`.

The second contribution for composers is a way to work with listener variability. A field score is not a command. It proposes a trajectory to listeners whose histories differ. This can reduce frustration: a failed intended emotion is not merely a bad listener or bad piece; it may reveal a different landscape, kernel, threshold, or appraisal. Composers can then decide whether to narrow the work, broaden its affordances, or embrace divergence. The model treats response variability as structurally meaningful `interpretive`.

The third contribution is a renewed seriousness about timbre, rhythm, and performance. In many theoretical traditions, harmony and pitch structure receive the deepest formal attention while timbre and bodily timing remain hard to discuss. In the field account, timbre can be directional forcing, rhythm can be coupling, and performance can be real-time parameter control. This better matches many contemporary practices: electronic music, improvisation, sound art, metal, club music, installation, film sound, and ritual performance all operate heavily through these dimensions `interpretive`.

For artists, the book offers a labelled practice of affective media. A [T]-Theory artwork can be a film score, projection, live instrument, browser interface, body-grid visualisation, or installation. What distinguishes it is not a house style but a commitment to response grammar and claim status. The artist can say which parts are sourced, which are interpretive, which are design maps, which are measured, and which are speculative. This is a rare combination: ambitious affective art with visible epistemic humility `interpretive`.

The second contribution for artists is the emotional score as a portable form. A trajectory can migrate across media while changing renderers. This supports collaboration between composer, filmmaker, choreographer, installation artist, and programmer. It also supports critique: if the same abstract trajectory fails in one medium and succeeds in another, the renderer matters more than the score; if it persists across media, the score has some invariant force. Either way, artistic knowledge becomes more precise `open-hypothesis`.

The third contribution is a boundary around care. Many artists work with trauma, memory, dissociation, grief, and bodily intensity. This book does not tell them to stop. It tells them to label, scaffold, and avoid false clinical claims. The body is not an unlimited artistic substrate. Participants need exits, context, consent, and non-diagnostic language. A field aesthetics that forgets this becomes just another technology of affective capture. A field aesthetics that remembers it can make difficult work with greater responsibility `interpretive`.

Across all three audiences, the shared gain is a change of question. Instead of asking only "what emotion does this music express or induce?", ask: what mechanism is active, what parameter changes, what path opens, what path closes, what memory returns, what body locks, what judgement updates, what remains after the sound, and what evidence would change our mind? That is the book's practical discipline `interpretive`.

# Conclusion: Composing with the Field

This volume has argued for a disciplined, labelled, and testable field aesthetics. Its strongest claim is not that [T]-Theory has proved why music moves us. It has not. Its strongest claim is that music-induced affect can be modelled as a response trajectory in a forced, damped, noisy, memory-bearing field, with BRECVEMA mechanisms interpreted as distinct parameter operations `open-hypothesis`.

For music psychology, the value is integration. Brainstem reflex, entrainment, conditioning, contagion, imagery, memory, expectancy, and judgement already have their own literatures and experimental traditions. The field account asks how they combine in a single episode. It thereby shifts the research question from "which mechanism caused this emotion?" to "which mechanisms modulated which parameters of the response, and how did their interaction shape the path?" That is a better question for real listening `interpretive`.

For composers, the value is a new sketch grammar. A passage can be described as an impulse, a lock, a bias, a coupling, an image source, a memory kernel, a barrier, or an appraisal update. A form can be designed as a sequence of basins and transitions. Timbre can be treated as direction, rhythm as phase relation, harmony as barrier and release, quotation as kernel activation, performance as coupling, and silence as a perturbation in its own right. These descriptions do not replace craft; they give craft an additional analytic layer `interpretive`.

For artists, the value is the emotional score. *The Tensor* shows how an artwork can be defined as a trajectory rather than a container. The Soma Machine shows how a trajectory can be rendered with claim labels and an equation ledger. [T]-Theory practice is strongest when it keeps those labels visible: ARTWORK, SCORE, RENDERER, RESPONSE, EVIDENCE, DESIGN MAP, INTERPRETIVE, NOT MEDICAL. In a culture of immersive media and affective technology, that explicit labelling is not optional. It is an ethical feature `interpretive`.

For performers, the field vocabulary honours what performance already knows: that timing, touch, breath, room, attention, gesture, and audience response are not secondary to the work. They are part of the perturbation. A performance does not merely instantiate a score; it traverses a field with a specific audience at a specific time. That is why the same song can be trivial in one setting and life-changing in another. The model must remain humble before that variability `interpretive`.

For researchers, the agenda is clear. Test entrainment as a listener-specific locking region. Test expectancy as barrier modulation against Meyer-Huron style predictions. Test memory kernels with autobiographical and culturally familiar stimuli. Test timbre as directional forcing using controlled synthesis. Test multi-mechanism stacking. Compare field models with circumplex, cue-based, predictive-processing, and autoregressive baselines. Publish failures as well as passes. A field aesthetics becomes science only when it risks losing `open-hypothesis`.

For the Soma Machine, the next step is not more spectacular visuals. It is stricter alignment between mechanism, equation, renderer, and claim status. The MVP's acceptance criteria are well chosen: select 4D, 8D, and 11D; choose a BRECVEMA mechanism; trigger a poke; watch a time-dependent response; ensure equation ledger, explanation, and visual state agree; identify selected system, parts, relations, active field, and aggregation rule; declare morphism status for curated paths. Meeting those criteria would make the interface a real educational instrument `derived-under-assumptions`.

For clinical or wellbeing applications, caution is non-negotiable. Music can regulate, disturb, accompany, console, organise, and transform; many traditions already know this. But this volume does not provide treatment advice, diagnostic categories, or claims of efficacy. Clinical language in the embedded materials must be read as testimony, modelling, and future research unless independent clinical evidence is supplied. A composer can design for care without pretending to be a clinician `open-hypothesis`.

The central artistic maxim can now be restated with labels. The score is the proposed trajectory `interpretive`. The performance is the traversal through body, room, medium, and audience `interpretive`. The experience is the listener's actual attractor path `open-hypothesis` when claimed by the model, and a lived aesthetic fact when reported by the listener. Evidence begins only when response is measured, logged, replicated, and compared with alternatives `empirical-result` when such data exist.

The physics of music and affect, then, is not a reduction of art to equations. It is an attempt to write the dynamics that artists already manipulate, listeners already undergo, and psychologists already study in fragments. If the model survives testing, it may offer music psychology a richer account of temporal affect. If parts of it fail, the labels will show where. Either outcome is useful. The field is not protected by poetry; it is exposed by it `interpretive`.
